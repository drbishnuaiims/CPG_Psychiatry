High-yield
Diagnosis is a continuous process.

The guideline stresses repeated clinical evaluation and re-evaluation when new information emerges from the patient or caregivers. In doubtful cases, a medication-free observation period can be considered, but this must be balanced against treatment delay and risk of harm in acutely ill patients.

Introduction & scope

The guideline describes schizophrenia as a serious mental disorder and notes reported Indian prevalence rates of 2–3 per 1000. It emphasizes that resource constraints, poverty, limited education and inadequate access to healthcare make delivery of care particularly challenging in India.

The Indian Psychiatric Society had previously issued schizophrenia treatment guidelines in 2005. The 2017 document aims to update those recommendations in areas where management had undergone significant development and is intended to be read alongside the 2005 guideline.

Scope statement

The recommendations are not tied to one specific treatment setting and may need minor modification for local circumstances. They are primarily intended for adults. Children and older adults may have different needs, and recommendations should be tailored to the individual patient.

Authors: Sandeep Grover, Subho Chakrabarti, Parmanand Kulhara and Ajit Avasthi, Department of Psychiatry, PGIMER, Chandigarh.

Assessment & evaluation

A comprehensive assessment of both patient and caregivers is the cornerstone. The core components are detailed history, physical examination and mental state examination, with information obtained from all possible sources, particularly the family.

Diagnostic approach

  • Prefer diagnosis according to current diagnostic criteria because this improves reliability, communication between clinicians and applicability of evidence-based recommendations.
  • Actively rule out mimics including mood disorders, substance-induced psychoses and psychoses secondary to physical illnesses.
  • Use history, examination and additional investigations as appropriate.
  • In doubtful cases, a medication-free observation period can be considered, balancing diagnostic clarification against risks of treatment delay.
  • Remember that diagnosis is not a one-time event; it may require re-evaluation as longitudinal information accumulates.

What the assessment should cover

Clinical domains

  • Symptom dimensions: reality distortion, disorganization, negative, depressive and cognitive symptoms.
  • Symptom severity.
  • Comorbid psychiatric, medical and substance-use conditions.
  • Risk of harm to self and others.
  • Level of functioning.
  • Socio-cultural milieu.

Physical & investigation domains

  • Detailed physical examination.
  • Blood pressure and weight; BMI and waist circumference where indicated.
  • Haemogram, blood glucose, lipid levels, liver and renal functions.
  • ECG, with attention to QTc.
  • Neuroimaging where indicated.

Substance use and caregiver assessment

Maintain a high index of suspicion for comorbid substance abuse/dependence. Where facilities exist, urine or blood screening may be used with prior consent. Caregiver assessment includes knowledge and understanding of illness, attitudes and beliefs about treatment, impact of illness, and personal/social resources.

Additional / optional assessments

  • Standardized rating scales for relevant aspects of illness.
  • Detailed cognitive testing when required and feasible.
  • Neuroimaging particularly in first-episode psychosis, neurological signs, non-response to treatment and elderly patients.
Ongoing assessment

As treatment progresses, assess response, side effects, treatment adherence, the immediate environment, disability, other healthcare needs, ease of access and the patient’s relationship with the treatment team.

Formulating the treatment plan & choosing the setting

Treatment planning involves deciding the setting, treatments and domains to be addressed. Patients, caregivers and staff involved in care may participate.

Treatment plans should be needs-based, practical, feasible and flexible.

They should be continuously re-evaluated and modified as required.

Least restrictive appropriate setting

The basic principle is to provide care in the least restrictive setting that nevertheless meets patient and caregiver needs. Most patients will probably receive care as outpatients, while inpatient beds may be scarce.

Indications for inpatient care during acute episodes

  • Suicidal behaviour putting the patient’s life at risk.
  • Severe agitation or violence putting others at risk.
  • Refusal to eat putting the patient’s life at risk.
  • Severe malnutrition.
  • Unable to care for self and requiring constant supervision/support.
  • Catatonia.
  • General medical or comorbid psychiatric conditions making outpatient management unsafe or ineffective.

Where inpatient facilities are unavailable, the patient/family should be informed and admission to the nearest available inpatient facility may be facilitated. Whenever possible, an admitted patient should have accompanying family caregivers.

Antipsychotic treatment

Overall choice: FGA vs SGA

The guideline notes that large-scale evidence and real-world studies show little difference in efficacy, adherence and subjective aspects between FGA and SGA groups, or among individual antipsychotics within either group. The major exception highlighted is clozapine, which appears more efficacious than other antipsychotics in treatment-resistant patients.

Dose principles

  • First-episode psychosis generally responds to lower doses.
  • High or very high (“mega”) doses, if used, should be reserved for exceptional circumstances and used cautiously.
  • The guideline notes clinical/pharmacokinetic suggestions that Indian patients may require lower doses than Western patients, but also states that clinical-trial evidence for or against this is limited.

Interpretation

These are the dose ranges printed in the 2017 source. They should not be treated as current prescribing limits without checking current product information and contemporary guidance.

Route of administration

  • Parenteral antipsychotics may be needed in acutely agitated patients for rapid control of behavioural disturbance.
  • Liquid or mouth-dissolving formulations may help in non-compliant patients.
  • Depot preparations are generally not used in acute agitation, with the guideline noting an exception for zuclopenthixol acetate.
  • In general, use one drug by one route to minimize interactions and simplify clinical observation.
  • Depot preparations can help ensure medication adherence and may be chosen when patients/relatives prefer them.
  • Test doses are administered at initiation of depot treatment.
  • Depot dosing should remain within recommended dose and interval ranges to optimize relapse prevention.

What constitutes an adequate trial?

All antipsychotics: highest tolerable dose for 6–8 weeks.
Clozapine: minimum treatment period 3–6 months.

Response

Antipsychotics are expected to produce significant remission of positive symptoms; therefore, this is a reasonable acute-phase treatment goal. They are less effective for negative symptoms, so mild-to-moderate reduction in negative symptoms may be an acceptable goal. Response indicators for cognitive symptoms were described as unclear. Regular monitoring of response and emergent adverse effects is required.

Poor compliance/non-compliance should be evaluated before switching medication. If adherence is poor, identify and address its causes rather than automatically labeling the patient as pharmacologically non-responsive.

Clozapine

  • Consider after failure of sequential adequate trials of 2 antipsychotics, at least one being an SGA.
  • The guideline also describes earlier consideration in patients who are violent, at risk for suicide, not responding to current medication, or experiencing intolerable side effects with two different antipsychotic classes.

Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS)

  • First establish that the disorder failed adequate antipsychotic trials in terms of dose, duration and adherence.
  • Consider alternative causes of non-response, including non-compliance, adverse effects and comorbid conditions such as substance misuse.
  • Clozapine is reported as beneficial in TRS.
  • The role of ECT in TRS had not been examined adequately, although studies suggested potential usefulness.
  • Psychosocial approaches including cognitive therapy, family treatment, assertive outreach and crisis intervention may be beneficial.

Clozapine-resistant illness

The guideline notes some evidence that combining ECT with clozapine can improve outcomes in patients who do not respond to clozapine alone. Evidence for combining clozapine with other antipsychotics, antidepressants or mood stabilizers was described as limited and unconvincing.

Difficult-to-treat schizophrenia

Factors include inadequate response, adverse drug effects, poor adherence, comorbidity, treatment failure and relapse despite adequate doses. Address adherence and adverse effects, select antipsychotics with medical/psychiatric comorbidities in mind, consider clozapine for treatment resistance or relapse despite adequate doses of two or more antipsychotics, and address psychosocial contributors to non-adherence.

Adjunctive medication & ECT

Combination antipsychotics

Evidence for efficacy of antipsychotic combinations is limited. In the initial phase, combinations may sometimes be used to manage agitation, but the guideline advises caution: use on an SOS basis and for the shortest possible time. Prolonged combinations should generally be avoided except in patients not responding to clozapine.

Adjunctive medications

  • Antidepressants, mood stabilizers and benzodiazepines may be used when there is a clear rationale and generally for the shortest duration necessary.
  • Lithium and other mood stabilizers may be considered in agitated/overactive patients or those with affective symptoms responding poorly to the current drug.
  • Benzodiazepines may help agitation and sleep disturbance.
  • Antidepressants may help post-psychotic depression and may be avoided during florid psychosis.
  • Routine prophylactic anticholinergics are not recommended; use them when EPS actually develops, at the lowest possible dose and shortest possible duration.

ECT

ECT is used relatively frequently for schizophrenia in India. The guideline describes acute-phase efficacy as somewhat controversial but notes evidence for augmentation and acceleration of response. Benefits of adding ECT to antipsychotics may be transient, particularly over the first few weeks.

Possible ECT indications listed

Catatonic symptoms; affective symptoms; need for rapid symptom control; suicidal behaviour; severe agitation/violence; good previous ECT response; inadequate response to an adequate antipsychotic trial; augmentation of partial antipsychotic response; clozapine-resistant schizophrenia; and inability to tolerate antipsychotic medications.

ECT is described as not useful in chronic schizophrenia, with insufficient evidence for longer-term use except exceptional circumstances. When used, patients/caregivers should receive adequate information and support, informed consent should be obtained, and response/adverse effects should be carefully monitored.

Psychoeducation, psychosocial interventions & rehabilitation

Psychoeducation

Psychoeducation can be offered to both patients and family members. Explanations should be simple and brief but clear and thorough. The aim includes understanding illness, treatment, likely adverse effects and expected treatment duration.

Core content

  • Knowledge of aetiology, treatment and prognosis.
  • Diagnosis and symptom dimensions.
  • Treatment options, effectiveness, side effects and duration.
  • Importance of medication/treatment adherence.
  • Expected course and long-term outcome.

Practical family work

  • Substance use, marriage and other issues.
  • Communication patterns and problem solving.
  • Disability benefits.
  • Relapse and early warning signs.
  • Daily stress management.
  • Insight, expressed emotion, coping with residual symptoms.

No blame should be attached to the family. Adherence is a major objective. Psychoeducation should be tailored to patient/caregiver needs and can incorporate feedback from previous sessions.

Psychosocial interventions

The guideline considers psychosocial interventions integral to schizophrenia management. Options include family intervention, CBT, social skills training, cognitive remediation, individual supportive therapy, group therapy, vocational rehabilitation, early-intervention programmes, case management, community mental-health teams and crisis-resolution teams.

Indian context

The guideline notes Indian evidence and experience with family treatments, rehabilitation, community programmes, yoga and cognitive remediation, and recommends needs-based psychosocial care from simple to complex depending on patient/family needs and available resources.

Family interventions

  • India has a long tradition of family involvement in mental healthcare.
  • Formats include informal/unstructured psychoeducation, structured treatments, group interventions and integrated psychosocial treatments.
  • Continued contact, medication adherence, emotional support and practical support are core emphases.
  • Tailoring to family background and needs improves acceptability and readiness to participate.

Individual supportive therapy

A supportive, empathic relationship with good listening promotes a lasting therapeutic alliance. Additional elements include support/advice, continued engagement, treatment adherence, healthy lifestyles and minimizing stress. Individual support plus medication was described as common and highly valued by patients/families.

Other psychosocial interventions

Home-based care, caregiver support groups, community interventions, cognitive training/remediation and yoga were described as promising; the guideline calls for more research and wider implementation.

Rehabilitation

Vocational rehabilitation resources may be scarce. If the patient is already employed, address illness-related workplace problems. If unemployed, assess suitability for work and encourage appropriate employment when ready. Where rehabilitation is required, culture-specific adaptation is emphasized. Simple, inexpensive strategies can be part of the overall psychosocial package.

Lifestyle & dietary measures

All patients should receive advice on lifestyle and diet

Physical exercise, dietary modification and smoking abstinence are recommended to reduce metabolic adverse effects and cardiovascular morbidity/mortality.

Treatment adherence

The guideline defines adherence as the extent to which behaviour—clinic attendance, medication taking, diet and lifestyle changes—coincides with the clinical prescription. Non-adherence includes failure to enter treatment, premature termination and incomplete implementation of instructions.

Clinical approach to non-adherence

Assess familial, social, biological and pharmacological contributors. Address concerns through psychoeducation and modification of pharmacological treatment. Depot and supervised mouth-dissolving formulations may help. Cognitive-behavioural approaches and motivational interviewing may also be beneficial.

Phases of illness and treatment

The guideline divides management into prodromal, acute, continuation/stabilization and maintenance/stable phases.

Prodromal stage

Psychological/behavioural abnormalities in cognition, emotion, perception, communication, motivation and sleep may precede frank psychosis by weeks to years. Described categories include basic symptoms, attenuated positive symptoms, brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms, schizotypal features, genetic risk plus functional deterioration, and non-specific general symptoms.

Reported conversion rates in high-risk groups were 25–40%. The prodrome can itself affect social, emotional and cognitive development.

Factors associated with conversion

  • Genetic risk with recent functional deterioration.
  • Higher unusual thought content.
  • Suspiciousness/paranoia.
  • Social impairment.
  • Longer symptom duration.
  • High levels of depression.
  • Reduced attention.
  • Substance-use history.

Management should be needs-based. Evidence for antipsychotics in the prodromal phase was described as insufficient to recommend routine use for all patients; antidepressants may improve symptoms in a subgroup, and preliminary evidence existed for omega-3 fatty acids. Psychological approaches such as CBT, cognitive therapy and psychosocial stress management may improve functioning/symptoms. Initial management is described primarily in terms of psychosocial intervention, weighing medication adverse effects and theoretical concerns about dopamine-receptor sensitization/supersensitivity psychosis.

Acute phase

Patients may present with florid delusions/hallucinations, disorganization, behavioural disturbance, severe functional impairment and risk to self/others. Patient and family may have difficulty adjusting to the onset of acute illness.

Patient goals

  • Reduce/eliminate schizophrenia symptoms.
  • Improve functioning.
  • Promote safety and reduce harm.
  • Reduce stress.

Caregiver + shared goals

  • Minimize caregiver distress.
  • Support coping.
  • Develop therapeutic alliance.
  • Enable active patient/caregiver engagement.
  • Provide basic tailored information/support.

Acute management includes comprehensive assessment, goals, setting selection, antipsychotic choice/dose/route/duration, response/non-response assessment, cautious adjunctive use, ECT when indicated, basic psychosocial interventions and planning for subsequent treatment.

Continuation / stabilization phase

Begins after acute symptoms reduce or remit and conventionally lasts approximately 6–12 months. Goals include consolidating remission, further reducing symptoms and preventing early relapse. Medication should preferably continue at the same dose for the next 6–12 months, while monitoring response, side effects and adherence. Psychosocial work should maintain engagement/adherence, support patients/families and prepare the patient for community life; more elaborate psychosocial interventions may be introduced.

Maintenance / stable phase

Symptoms are stable and usually less severe; negative symptoms may predominate and social/occupational deficits become more visible. Goals are maintaining/improving functioning, preventing recurrence, quality of life and personal recovery.

Stable-phase follow-up: follow-ups may be scheduled every 2–3 months, more often during crises or if the family desires. Regular family feedback is recommended.

Dose reduction / withdrawal in stable patients

  • Individualize dose and balance adverse-effect reduction against relapse prevention.
  • Stable patients without positive symptoms may be candidates for dose reduction.
  • The guideline suggests gradual reduction at about 20% every 6 months until a minimum effective dose is reached.
  • Withdrawal/reduction should be gradual with regular monitoring for relapse.
  • After withdrawal, monitoring for relapse should continue for at least 2 years after the last acute episode.
  • Any re-emergence of symptoms should be treated immediately.

Rehabilitation and relapse prevention

Carry forward gains from earlier phases and modify goals/strategies when needed. Patients and relatives should be taught early relapse signs and the need for early intervention. Easy access to emergency/inpatient services should be arranged where possible. During impending relapse, increase contact and consider brief admissions or frequent home visits when feasible.

Special situations

First-episode psychosis & early intervention
  • Longer duration of untreated psychosis is described as a powerful predictor of poorer subsequent outcome.
  • Reduce DUP, promote remission, maximize functioning and prevent relapse/adverse outcomes.
  • Early detection, comprehensive assessment, continued engagement and flexible treatment are emphasized.
  • Optimal duration of maintenance antipsychotic treatment is described as uncertain; 1–2 years is usually recommended, with some patients requiring longer or shorter treatment.
Suicidality
  • Suicide is more common during the initial period after hospital discharge.
  • Risk factors listed include younger age, higher socioeconomic status, high premorbid scholastic achievement/IQ, high aspirations/expectations, early onset, younger age at first hospitalization, chronic deteriorating course with multiple relapses, and higher insight.
  • Treatment-related associations include inadequate treatment, poor adherence, poor response and akathisia.
  • Because suicidal behaviour cannot be predicted reliably, assess suicidal ideation at every phase.
  • High-risk management and efforts to retain the patient in inpatient care are emphasized during high-risk admission.
  • Treat psychosis adequately and treat depression when present.
  • Clozapine is described as reducing suicidal behaviour and may be preferred in patients with multiple attempts or persistent suicidal ideation.
  • Psychoeducation should address warning signs; severe distress related to loss/stigma requires psychological support and close monitoring.
Violence & aggression
  • Assess violence/dangerousness during every assessment, particularly in acute illness.
  • Consider inpatient management for serious threat or actual violence.
  • Injectable antipsychotics such as haloperidol or lorazepam are listed for management of violence/aggression.
Comorbid substance-use disorders
  • Associated with poorer overall outcome.
  • Treatment aims include abstinence or at least harm reduction.
  • Use pharmacological agents for detoxification/pharmacoprophylaxis where indicated.
  • Relapse-prevention counselling, CBT and motivational enhancement may be incorporated.
  • Continuously monitor for substance-use relapse.
Depression in schizophrenia
  • First rule out organic causes, negative symptoms, antipsychotic adverse effects such as dysphoria/akinesia/akathisia, schizoaffective depression, stress reactions and impending psychosis.
  • Controlled trials cited by the guideline found SGAs superior to FGAs in antidepressant efficacy.
  • Clozapine may be particularly useful when suicide risk is high.
  • Antidepressants are generally ineffective in the acute phase, may worsen psychosis and are best avoided then.
  • Adjunctive antidepressants have evidence in post-psychotic depression.
  • Clinical experience suggests ECT may help.
Catatonia
  • Consider all possible differentials and investigate/manage underlying causes.
  • Benzodiazepines, especially lorazepam, may produce symptomatic relief in a significant proportion.
  • If non-responsive or if catatonia relapses after benzodiazepine withdrawal, consider ECT.
Negative symptoms
  • First rule out secondary negative symptoms.
  • Persistent symptoms may represent primary negative symptoms/deficit state.
  • No treatment was described as having proven efficacy for primary negative symptoms, though some benefit had been reported with clozapine and amisulpride.
  • Social-skills training and token economy may be attempted.
Obsessive-compulsive symptoms
  • OCD/OC symptoms may be prodromal, part of schizophrenia, treatment-emergent, or precede schizophrenia.
  • Treatment-emergent symptoms are commonly reported with clozapine, olanzapine and risperidone.
  • Randomized controlled evidence was described as limited.
  • If symptoms are part of schizophrenia, initially treat with antipsychotic alone and monitor.
  • If treatment-emergent, consider dose reduction, changing antipsychotic or anti-obsessional agents.
  • Some data existed for clomipramine and fluvoxamine.
  • CBT with exposure and response prevention had also been tried with beneficial effects.
Comorbid physical illness
  • Patients with schizophrenia have high rates of physical illness.
  • All patients require thorough physical assessment and investigations according to feasibility/need.
  • Comorbid illness should be monitored throughout treatment.
  • Physical comorbidity and concomitant medications should influence treatment setting and antipsychotic selection.
Pregnancy
  • Women with schizophrenia are described as having higher rates of low birth weight, preterm birth, stillbirth and perinatal death, and unplanned pregnancies are common.
  • Counsel patients/caregivers about pregnancy and risks; avoid unplanned pregnancy where possible.
  • The guideline states most psychotropics were categorized C/D except clozapine in the classification system it used.
  • High-potency antipsychotics were generally said to have lower risk of fetal malformations.
  • Decisions to start/continue/discontinue antipsychotics should consider current symptoms, longitudinal course, relapse risk after stopping, fetal malformation risk and obstetric complications.
  • Medication decisions should involve patient, spouse/caregivers, with close liaison with obstetricians.
Metabolic / cardiovascular consequences

See the detailed adverse-effect and metabolic-monitoring sections below. The guideline emphasizes that cardiovascular adverse effects may be life-threatening and metabolic monitoring is essential.

Antipsychotic adverse effects & management

Extrapyramidal symptoms

EPS occur especially with high-potency FGAs but also with SGAs. Acute EPS—acute dystonia, pseudo-parkinsonism and akathisia—usually occur in the first days/weeks, are dose-dependent and subside after stopping the offending drug. Chronic EPS—tardive dyskinesia, tardive dystonia and tardive akathisia—usually occur after months/years, are not dose-dependent and can persist after stopping the offending agent.

Parkinsonism

During initial treatment, lower the antipsychotic dose. If efficacy becomes unacceptable, consider switching to an agent with lower EPS potential. If the patient continues to respond but has Parkinsonism, a short course of anticholinergic medication may be considered.

Acute dystonia

Usually follows the first few doses. Parenteral anticholinergic or antihistaminergic medication produces a dramatic response; short-course anticholinergic treatment may prevent recurrence.

Akathisia

Reduce dose or switch to a lower-EPS agent. Beta-blockers and benzodiazepines such as clonazepam or lorazepam may be required in some patients.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Emergency

NMS is described as an acute psychiatric emergency. Management in the guideline: stop the antipsychotic, provide supportive measures and consider bromocriptine, amantadine or dantrolene; lorazepam may help. Treatment-refractory cases may benefit from ECT.

Sedation

Antihistaminergic, antiadrenergic and antidopaminergic actions contribute. Risk is high with chlorpromazine, clozapine and quetiapine. Initially wait for tolerance; if persistent, reduce dose where possible.

Anticholinergic / antiadrenergic effects

Features include dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, thermoregulatory effects, impaired learning/memory and slowed cognition. Severe effects can cause confusion, delirium, somnolence and hallucinations. Clozapine and chlorpromazine are particularly associated. Effects are generally dose-dependent.

Cardiovascular effects

  • Common problems include QTc prolongation, orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia.
  • QTc >500 ms is associated with increased risk of torsades de pointes, ventricular fibrillation and sudden cardiac death.
  • Among older agents, thioridazine, pimozide and high-dose IV haloperidol were described as higher-risk for QTc prolongation.
  • Among SGAs, ziprasidone was described as having higher QTc risk, although this was not shown to be associated with sudden cardiac death.
  • If QTc prolongation occurs, consider changing the antipsychotic.
  • Orthostatic hypotension is linked to antiadrenergic activity and is commonly seen with clozapine, risperidone and quetiapine; among FGAs, chlorpromazine is highlighted.
  • Prevent hypotension with low starting doses and slow titration; if it occurs, reduce dose and consider switching.
  • Stockings, increased salt intake and fludrocortisone are listed as additional options.
  • Clozapine-related anticholinergic tachycardia may be treated with a low-dose peripherally acting beta-blocker.

Hyperprolactinemia & sexual dysfunction

Sexual dysfunction can occur with all antipsychotics, with higher rates reported with FGAs and risperidone. Prolactin elevation disrupts the hypothalamo-pituitary-gonadal axis; women may be more sensitive to hyperprolactinemia-related dysfunction. First step: reduce dose. If unacceptable, switch antipsychotic. If switching is not possible, bromocriptine or amantadine may be considered for hyperprolactinemia. For sexual dysfunction not associated with hyperprolactinemia, the guideline lists yohimbine, cyproheptadine, imipramine for retrograde ejaculation, phosphodiesterase inhibitors for erectile dysfunction, and paroxetine/dapoxetine for premature ejaculation.

Metabolic monitoring

The guideline emphasizes that people with schizophrenia have higher rates of metabolic syndrome than the general population and that antipsychotics increase diabetes risk. Metabolic adverse effects need active monitoring to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.

Higher-risk patients

Patients with personal/family history of obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension and/or cardiovascular disease are suggested to be monitored every 3 months.

Indian resource-sensitive implementation

The guideline recognizes that complete regular monitoring may be difficult in India because of poor follow-up and resource constraints. It emphasizes trying to measure weight and fasting glucose at every treatment facility. Training centres and resource-rich settings may consider complete metabolic monitoring.

When metabolic problems develop

If weight increases by more than 7% of baseline or hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypertension or another significant metabolic/cardiovascular adverse effect develops, changing the antipsychotic may be considered. The decision should consider the full illness course, comorbid physical illness, the adverse-effect profile of the current medication and potential adverse effects of the replacement.

If switching is not possible, the guideline lists metformin or topiramate together with more intensive dietary and lifestyle interventions. Close liaison with endocrinology and cardiology is recommended when appropriate.

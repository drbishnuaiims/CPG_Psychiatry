1. Executive overview

4
cardinal motor features

Rest tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity and postural instability.

15–20y
possible prodromal window

Neurodegeneration may precede clinically evident motor disease by years.

5
core molecular hallmarks

α-synuclein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired protein clearance, neuroinflammation and oxidative stress.

Central concept: PD is not simply a dopamine-deficiency disorder. The review emphasises clinical and biological heterogeneity, multiple neurotransmitter systems, genetic/environmental interactions and extensive non-motor disease.
  • Classic motor manifestations are accompanied by non-motor symptoms such as RBD, anosmia, constipation and depression, which may occur in the prodromal phase.
  • As disease progresses, cognitive impairment and dysautonomia may become major determinants of disability and quality of life.
  • Important pathways beyond dopamine include noradrenergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic and adenosine systems.
  • Potential disease-modifying approaches include α-synuclein immunotherapy/anti-aggregation strategies, GLP-1 receptor agonists, GBA/LRRK2-targeted agents, cell replacement and gene therapy—but the review concludes that effective neuroprotective/disease-modifying therapy was not yet available.

2. Historical milestones & disease phenotypes

1817 — James Parkinson Described the clinical syndrome in An Essay on the Shaking Palsy.
1912 — Lewy bodies Frederick Lewy identified intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies that became a pathological hallmark.
1957 onward — Dopamine deficiency Work by Arvid Carlsson and Oleh Hornykiewicz established the relationship between dopamine deficiency and parkinsonism.
1961 — Intravenous levodopa First clinical proof-of-concept rescue in PD patients.
1967 — High-dose levodopa George Cotzias helped establish high-dose levodopa therapy.
1982 — MPTP Synthetic-heroin-associated parkinsonism led to identification of MPTP toxicity to substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons and transformed experimental PD research.
1996–1997 — SNCA Genetic linkage and then mutation discovery established a genetic cause of PD and accelerated gene discovery.

Clinical subtypes

Tremor-dominant phenotype

Characterised by relative predominance of tremor. The review contrasts this with the PIGD phenotype.

PIGD phenotype

Postural-instability-gait-disorder phenotype has been associated in many studies with more severe disease and faster progression.

Important nuance: motor subtypes are not necessarily fixed. They may change with disease progression and treatment, so phenotype classification should not be treated as a permanent biological category.

3. Clinical syndrome & diagnosis

Parkinsonism

Bradykinesia + either rest tremor or rigidity constitutes parkinsonism under the MDS clinical diagnostic framework described in the review.

UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank approach

  • Bradykinesia is required.
  • Plus one of: rigidity, 4–6 Hz rest tremor, or postural instability.
  • For probable PD, three supportive features are additionally required.

MDS clinical diagnostic criteria

  1. Presence of parkinsonism.
  2. No absolute exclusionary criterion.
  3. Supportive criteria present.
  4. No red flags.

Clinical assessment

  • Clinical rating scales are important, particularly the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) for disease severity and treatment outcomes.
  • The review notes ongoing development of diagnostic, presymptomatic and progression biomarkers.
Neurobiology of symptoms: disease involvement extends beyond basal ganglia dopamine neurons. The dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, locus coeruleus and raphe nuclei are among other regions implicated, providing a biological basis for prominent non-motor symptoms.

4. Prodromal Parkinson’s disease

The prodromal phase refers to a period in which underlying neurodegeneration has begun but clinically evident motor symptoms are not yet present.

Underlying neurodegeneration→Prodromal symptoms / markers→Subtle motor impairment→Clinical PD→Motor + non-motor complications

Sleep

REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) is a major prodromal feature.

Olfaction

Olfactory dysfunction/anosmia may precede motor disease.

GI

Constipation and gastrointestinal dysfunction may occur early.

Mood

Depression and anxiety may be present before motor onset.

Autonomic

Autonomic dysfunction can appear during the prodromal period.

Other markers

The 2019 MDS prodromal criteria discussed in the review added markers such as diabetes mellitus and physical inactivity.

Important: the review describes the prodromal phase as potentially extending up to 15–20 years before motor symptoms. A growing set of clinical, biochemical, imaging and genetic risk markers may support probabilistic prodromal risk estimation.

5. Etiology, epidemiology & risk factors

Age

Median age at onset in the review: ~60 years. Age is described as the single most important risk factor.

Sex

Frequency is higher in men than women, with a reported ratio of approximately 1.3–2.0.

Burden

The cited GBD estimate projected PD cases to increase from about 7 million in 2015 to ~13 million in 2040.

Age-related biological vulnerability

Ageing-related biological dysfunction discussed in the review includes telomere dysfunction, genomic instability, epigenetic changes, ubiquitin–proteasome/autophagy–lysosomal dysfunction and mitochondrial defects, all of which may facilitate neuronal demise.

Gene–environment interaction

The review frames PD as an interaction among genetic, epigenetic, environmental/lifestyle and ageing-related factors, rather than a single-cause disorder.

Environmental / lifestyle-associated factors discussed

Pesticide exposureHeavy metalsRural livingAgricultural occupationHead injuryMelanomaDairy productsType 2 diabetes mellitus

Interpret epidemiology carefully

The review explicitly notes inconsistent replication for several associations. Meta-analytic evidence was not uniformly robust for rural living, well-water consumption, farming and pesticide exposure, while some analyses supported an association with pesticides and others did not support traumatic head injury as a risk factor. Observational associations therefore should not automatically be treated as causal.

6. Genetics of Parkinson’s disease

Genetic discoveries have challenged the idea of PD as a single idiopathic entity. The review describes >20 monogenic forms and >100 risk loci.

Gene / pathwayInheritance / roleKey features from the review
SNCARare monogenic PD; α-synuclein biologyα-synuclein is a 140-amino-acid protein involved in vesicle trafficking/docking/fusion and axonal transport. Aggregated fibrillar α-synuclein is a major component of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. Gene duplication/triplication/quadruplication support a gene-dosage mechanism; triplication is associated with earlier disease and cognitive impairment.

G2019S is common in some populations but largely absent in Asians; Asian populations have ethnic-specific coding risk variants such as G2385R and R1628P. Typically late-onset, clinically resembling typical PD. GBAMajor genetic risk factorEncodes glucocerebrosidase, a lysosomal enzyme. Mutations are the single most important genetic risk factor for PD in the general population in the review, conferring >5-fold increased risk. GBA-associated PD tends to have younger onset, more cognitive impairment and more RBD. Proposed mechanism: impaired lysosomal function → α-synuclein accumulation/aggregation.

Genetic testing caveat: the review warns that direct-to-consumer genetic testing and whole-exome/genome sequencing can generate variants of uncertain significance that are difficult to interpret clinically.

7. Pathophysiology: the convergent cellular cascade

Genetic + environmental + ageing factors→Oxidative stress / cellular stress→α-synuclein + mitochondrial + lysosomal dysfunction→Neuroinflammation / excitotoxicity / transport failure→Neuronal death

1. α-synuclein misfolding & aggregation

  • Abnormal conformational change and aggregation can be toxic to dopaminergic neurons.
  • Oxidative stress, PD gene mutations and overexpression can influence aggregation.
  • Oligomeric/fibrillar species may activate neuroinflammation and seed cell-to-cell spread.
  • Therapeutic strategies include reducing expression, oligomer formation and transmission.

2. Mitochondrial dysfunction

  • Reduced complex I activity is reported in PD.
  • Rotenone can reproduce mitochondrial injury in experimental models.
  • Parkin, PINK1 and DJ1 are linked to impaired mitophagy.
  • Mitochondrial damage can promote oxidised dopamine accumulation and reduced glucocerebrosidase.

3. Impaired protein clearance

  • Defects involve the ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy–lysosomal systems.
  • GBA dysfunction links lysosomal impairment with α-synuclein accumulation.

4. Neuroinflammation

  • Innate and adaptive immune abnormalities are described.
  • Pro-inflammatory cytokines and altered immune-cell populations have been reported.
  • Microglial activation is supported by molecular imaging and experimental models.

5. Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is presented as a major background mechanism interacting with genetic, environmental, mitochondrial, protein-clearance and inflammatory pathways.

6. Additional linked processes

  • Vesicular transport disruption
  • Microtubular integrity loss
  • Neuronal excitotoxicity
  • Trophic-factor disruption
  • Iron metabolism dysregulation
  • Endoplasmic-reticulum impairment
  • Enzymatic activation including PARP-related pathways

Braak hypothesis — as presented in the review

Stages 1–2 Early pathological changes proposed in medulla oblongata and olfactory bulb.
Stages 3–4 Progression rostrally toward substantia nigra/midbrain; clinical symptoms become likely.
Stages 5–6 Later cortical involvement.
Nuance: the review notes that the Braak hypothesis has been challenged; it remains a useful model but should not be interpreted as an uncontested, universal sequence.

Gut–brain axis

  • The review discusses the possibility that the vagus nerve can act as a route for α-synuclein transmission from the gastrointestinal tract toward the lower brainstem.
  • Experimental observations involving microbiota transplantation, antibiotics, recolonisation, vagotomy and appendectomy are described.
  • The authors emphasise that further work is required to establish the role of microbiota, dysbiosis, infection and inflammation in triggering α-synuclein aggregation and CNS spread.

8. Treatment framework

Symptomatic motor treatment

Levodopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors, amantadine, anticholinergics, istradefylline and rescue/continuous-delivery approaches.

Non-motor treatment

Address cognition, psychosis, sleep, autonomic dysfunction, GI symptoms, mood, pain, fatigue and impulse-control problems.

Advanced disease

Optimise delivery and fluctuations; consider DBS or other device/surgical strategies in appropriately selected patients.

Core principle: treatment must be individualised. The review emphasises that clinical trials require well-defined populations and validated outcomes; UPDRS is commonly used as an outcome measure.

Disease-modifying / neuroprotective therapy

  • Variable progression rates reflect clinical and pathological heterogeneity.
  • Potential early/prodromal intervention is biologically attractive.
  • DATATOP: selegiline delayed the endpoint, but interpretation was confounded by symptomatic/antidepressant effects and amphetamine metabolites.
  • Rasagiline showed modest symptomatic benefit; ADAGIO did not establish a convincing disease-modifying effect.
  • The review concludes that there were no established effective neuroprotective/disease-modifying therapies that slowed progression.

9. Levodopa: cornerstone of motor treatment

Why it matters

Described as the most effective drug for PD motor symptoms.

Combination

Usually combined with carbidopa or benserazide to reduce peripheral conversion and nausea.

Response

A robust response to levodopa supports the clinical diagnosis of PD.

Adverse effects

Nausea/vomitingOrthostatic hypotensionSedationConfusionSleep disturbanceHallucinationsDyskinesias

Motor complications

Wearing-off

Shortening duration of benefit between doses. Fractionating the total daily dose is described as an initial strategy.

Peak-dose dyskinesia

Choreic/stereotypic movements occurring at higher levodopa concentrations.

Off-period dystonia

A recognised wearing-off phenomenon.

Management concept

Extend benefit by adjusting levodopa delivery or adding MAOIs, COMTIs, dopamine agonists or extended-release amantadine.

“Levodopa phobia” — what the review says

Concern about future motor complications can delay effective treatment. The review states that available animal/human evidence did not show levodopa accelerates disease progression, and delaying levodopa did not demonstrate prevention of dyskinesia. The earlier-versus-later L-dopa trial did not show toxicity, while dyskinesia occurred in a subset at higher dose. A delayed-start trial likewise did not show a disease-modifying effect.

Delivery formulations discussed

FormulationRole / key point in review
Immediate-release levodopaCore symptomatic treatment.
Extended-release carbidopa/levodopa (IPX066)Useful in persistent motor fluctuations despite frequent levodopa; reduction in daily off-time was modest (~1 hour in cited studies).
Levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gelContinuous intrajejunal infusion can smooth fluctuations; efficacy was compared with STN DBS.

Selected evidence points

  • Dopamine agonists: early use may delay levodopa-related complications, but evidence did not establish slower disease progression or better long-term quality of life. PD MED showed better mobility scores with levodopa than dopamine agonists/MAO-B inhibitors in newly diagnosed patients.
  • Apomorphine: subcutaneous injection can provide rapid rescue from end-of-dose wearing off/unpredictable episodes. Sublingual film improved MDS-UPDRS part 3 within 30 minutes in the cited trial, but oropharyngeal adverse effects caused discontinuation in a subset.
  • COMT inhibitors: prolong levodopa action; they do not prevent or delay motor complications.
  • Amantadine ER: cited randomised evidence showed reduced off time and increased on time without troublesome dyskinesia.

11. Levodopa-resistant motor & non-motor symptoms

Levodopa-resistant motor manifestations

Freezing of gait

May occur in either off or on state. Off-period freezing may improve with medication optimisation; on-period freezing is often pharmacologically resistant.

Postural instability

Often less responsive to dopaminergic treatment.

Dysarthria / dysphagia

Speech and voice therapy are important.

Non-motor symptom matrix

Psychiatry-relevant high-yield point

Dopamine agonists can precipitate impulse-control disorders. The review stresses that patients/caregivers should be counselled before treatment and at every visit because patients may not volunteer these behaviours. Associated psychiatric features can include psychosis, interpersonal sensitivity, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, depression and dopamine dysregulation syndrome.

12. Rehabilitation & exercise

Physical therapy

Important for gait, freezing, mobility and functional independence. Sensory cueing, including visual cues such as stepping over a laser line, may help freezing.

Speech / voice therapy

Important for dysarthria and dysphagia.

Occupational therapy

Supports activities of daily living and adaptation to disability.

Exercise: the review emphasises regular exercise and cites a double-blind randomised trial showing benefit from home-based/remotely supervised aerobic exercise on off-state MDS-UPDRS scores.

A meta-analysis of eight prospective studies involving 2,192 PD patients with mean follow-up of 12 years found that high/moderate—but not light—physical activity was associated with reduced PD risk; the reported risk reduction was 10–17% per additional 10 MET-hours/week of high or moderate physical activity. This is an epidemiological association, not proof of causality.

13. Surgical treatment

Deep brain stimulation (DBS)

Typical context: moderate-to-advanced PD with troublesome motor fluctuations, dyskinesia or levodopa-unresponsive symptoms despite optimal medical therapy.

Why DBS replaced much ablative surgery

Traditional thalamotomy/pallidotomy create permanent lesions. DBS permits stimulation parameters to be customised and adjusted to the patient's needs.

Common PD targets

  • STN — subthalamic nucleus
  • GPi — globus pallidus interna

Evidence cited in the review

Study / comparisonKey findings reported
DBS vs best medical therapy; 255 patients; 6 monthsDBS produced mean 4.6 hours/day additional on-time without troublesome dyskinesia vs 0 hours/day with best medical therapy. ≥5-point motor UPDRS improvement occurred in 71% vs 32%. Serious adverse events were higher with DBS: 40% vs 11%.
STN vs GPi; 299 patients; 24 monthsNo difference in mean motor UPDRS change. STN required lower dopamine-agonist doses; visuomotor processing speed declined more after STN. Depression worsened after STN but mood improved after GPi in the cited analysis.
Early STN DBS; 251 relatively young patientsDBS plus medical therapy improved quality of life, motor disability, ADLs and levodopa-related complications compared with medical therapy alone.

STN vs GPi — practical distinctions reported

GPi DBS
  • May be particularly suitable when troublesome dyskinesia is prominent.
  • Potentially useful with mild cognitive/behavioural impairment.
STN DBS
  • May be preferred when cognitively intact and reduction in levodopa dose is a primary goal.
  • Greater benefit on off periods in the review, but adverse effects such as ICD may be more likely.
Patient selection is critical. DBS outcomes depend on appropriate selection and surgical expertise. The review highlights emerging technologies including adaptive stimulation, improved connectivity-based targeting and directional stimulation.

Focused ultrasound & other procedures

  • Unilateral focused ultrasound lesioning of STN or thalamus may benefit selected patients, especially markedly asymmetric/tremor-dominant disease.
  • Spinal cord stimulation is being explored for patients whose major problem is gait dysfunction.

14. Emerging / experimental therapies

α-synuclein-directed therapies

  • Monoclonal antibodies against aggregated toxic α-synuclein.
  • Active immunisation.
  • Anti-aggregation strategies.
  • Strategies to increase protein clearance.
  • c-Abl kinase inhibitors such as nilotinib/K0706 were under investigation.

Metabolic / genetic targets

  • GLP-1 receptor agonists.
  • GBA-targeted strategies such as ambroxol.
  • LRRK2 modifiers.
  • Other genetically defined approaches including DNL201/DNL151 were being tested in the review era.

Cell replacement

  • Fetal tissue transplantation had variable outcomes and could produce off dyskinesias despite graft survival.
  • Human embryonic stem-cell and somatic-cell-derived midbrain dopaminergic cells offer potentially scalable sources.
  • Major issues: tumorigenicity, immunosuppression, off-target effects and delivery techniques.

Gene therapy

MRI-guided AAV2 delivery of AADC gene therapy into putamen was studied. The cited phase 1 study reported up to 42% putamen coverage, up to 79% increases in corresponding enzyme activity on PET and dose-related improvements in ON-time and quality of life at 12 months.

2020-era status: these approaches were experimental or investigational in the source review. The review’s conclusion was that effective neuroprotective/disease-modifying treatment capable of slowing PD progression was not yet established.

15. High-yield takeaways

Diagnosis

  1. Parkinsonism = bradykinesia + rest tremor or rigidity.
  2. Use exclusion criteria, supportive criteria and red flags.
  3. UPDRS is a key severity/outcome instrument.

Prodrome

  1. RBD
  2. Anosmia/olfactory dysfunction
  3. Constipation/GI dysfunction
  4. Depression/anxiety
  5. Autonomic dysfunction

Pathogenesis

  1. α-synuclein aggregation
  2. Mitochondrial dysfunction
  3. Protein-clearance failure
  4. Neuroinflammation
  5. Oxidative stress

Genetics

SNCA → α-synuclein; PRKN → AR early-onset PD; LRRK2 → AD PD; GBA → major genetic risk factor.

Motor treatment

Levodopa remains the most effective symptomatic motor drug. Do not equate motor complications with disease acceleration; the review found no disease-modifying effect from delaying levodopa.

Psychiatric relevance

Dopamine agonists → impulse-control disorders, hallucinations and sleepiness; PD itself can produce depression, anxiety, apathy, psychosis and dementia.

Advanced disease

Consider optimisation of drug delivery, rescue therapies, continuous infusion or DBS in appropriately selected patients.

Big limitation

No established neuroprotective/disease-modifying treatment in the source review; non-motor and advanced disease burden remains substantial.

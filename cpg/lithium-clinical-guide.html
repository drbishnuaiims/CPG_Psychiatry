1 Quick Clinical Summary

Core role

Lithium is a major mood stabiliser with established roles in bipolar disorder, particularly mania prevention and long-term relapse prevention.

Therapeutic drug monitoring

Interpret serum lithium in relation to the dose, formulation, timing of sample, renal function, hydration and clinical state.

Major safety domains

Kidney function, thyroid function, calcium, hydration/sodium balance and interacting medications.

Toxicity principle

Do not interpret toxicity from the lithium concentration alone. Clinical toxicity can occur at apparently therapeutic concentrations.

High-yield safety rule: Vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration, reduced sodium intake, renal impairment or addition of interacting medication can increase lithium exposure and precipitate toxicity.
Clinical situation Approximate target range Clinical comment
Maintenance ~0.6–0.8 mmol/L Common practical target; some patients require a broader individualised range.
Acute mania ~0.8–1.2 mmol/L Higher concentrations may be required during acute treatment, subject to tolerability and safety.
Elderly ~0.4–0.6 mmol/L may be adequate Use lower targets when clinically appropriate because of greater vulnerability to adverse effects and renal changes.
Depression augmentation ~0.6–1.0 mmol/L Use clinical response, tolerability and indication to individualise.
Ranges are clinical guides rather than absolute rules. Always interpret the concentration with the clinical context, formulation and local prescribing protocol.

2 Indications

Bipolar disorder

  • Acute mania
  • Maintenance treatment
  • Prevention of mood episode recurrence

Bipolar depression

Lithium may have a role in the overall long-term treatment strategy and in selected augmentation approaches.

Suicide prevention

Lithium has an important evidence base for reducing suicide risk in mood disorders and is often considered when selecting long-term mood-stabilising therapy.

Augmentation

Can be considered as augmentation in selected treatment-resistant depressive disorders under specialist supervision.

3 Mechanism

Lithium is a simple monovalent cation with complex intracellular effects. Its therapeutic effects are believed to involve multiple signalling pathways rather than a single receptor target.

Intracellular signalling

Modulation of phosphoinositide signalling and second-messenger systems.

GSK-3

Lithium inhibits glycogen synthase kinase-3 and influences downstream cellular signalling.

Neuroplasticity

Effects on neurotrophic and cellular resilience pathways may contribute to mood-stabilising effects.

Neurotransmission

Modulates multiple neurotransmitter systems indirectly.

4 Before Starting Lithium

Baseline principle: Lithium is a medication where baseline laboratory assessment and subsequent longitudinal monitoring are part of the treatment, not optional extras.
Assessment What to check Why
Renal Serum creatinine, eGFR, urea/U&E as locally available Lithium is predominantly renally eliminated.
Electrolytes Sodium and other relevant electrolytes Sodium balance influences lithium handling.
Thyroid TSH ± FT4 Lithium can cause hypothyroidism and goitre.
Calcium Serum calcium Screen for lithium-associated calcium/parathyroid abnormalities.
Weight Weight ± BMI Useful for subsequent metabolic and adverse-effect monitoring.
ECG When clinically indicated, particularly in patients with cardiac risk factors Lithium can affect cardiac conduction in susceptible patients.
Pregnancy Pregnancy testing where relevant Important for reproductive risk-benefit discussion.
Medication review NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics and other interacting drugs Several medications can increase lithium exposure.

Baseline Clinical Assessment

  • Confirm diagnosis and treatment indication.
  • Document current mood state.
  • Assess suicidal risk.
  • Assess psychotic symptoms where relevant.
  • Record previous response to lithium.
  • Record previous adverse effects.
  • Assess alcohol/substance use.
  • Review adherence history.
  • Review renal, thyroid and endocrine history.
  • Document concurrent medications and OTC medication use.

5 Dosing & Titration

Adult starting dose

A practical starting regimen from the supplied reference is 400 mg at night, followed by serum-level guided titration.

Elderly

A lower starting dose such as 200 mg may be appropriate, followed by cautious titration.

Steady state

A serum concentration is generally interpreted after adequate time to approach steady state, commonly approximately 5–7 days after a dose change depending on protocol.

Practical Titration Sequence

→
Check tolerability
→
Wait for steady state
→
12-h serum level
→
Adjust dose
→
Recheck
Do not chase a number blindly. Dose changes should consider symptoms, adverse effects, renal function, hydration, interacting medicines and the timing/quality of the lithium level.

Formulation Matters

  • Immediate-release and modified-release preparations are not automatically interchangeable on a milligram-for-milligram basis.
  • Check the exact formulation before changing brands.
  • Document the brand/formulation when clinically important.
  • After a formulation change, reassess the lithium level appropriately.

6 Serum Lithium Levels

Preferred sampling point

Usually approximately 12 hours after the previous dose, according to the standard monitoring approach.

Always document

Time of last dose + time of blood collection + current dose + formulation.

Unexpected level?

First verify timing, adherence, formulation, dose changes, hydration and interacting medication before changing the dose.

12-Hour Level Calculation

Interpreting a Level

Question Clinical interpretation
Was it a true 12-hour sample? If not, the result may not be directly comparable with previous levels.
Was the patient adherent? Missed doses can produce an apparently low concentration.
Any recent dose change? The level may not represent the new steady state.
Any dehydration? Can increase lithium concentration.
Any interacting medication? NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and thiazides are particularly important.
Is the patient clinically toxic? Clinical symptoms take priority over an isolated number.

When Lithium Is Appropriate

  • Established bipolar mania where lithium is clinically appropriate.
  • Patients with previous good response to lithium.
  • Situations where long-term continuation is likely to be useful.

In severe mania, lithium may not provide sufficiently rapid control as monotherapy. Combination treatment may be clinically required depending on severity and presentation.

Acute Target

A practical reference target is approximately 0.8–1.2 mmol/L, with the exact target individualised according to response and tolerability.

9 Maintenance Treatment

Typical target

Approximately 0.6–0.8 mmol/L for many patients.

Higher target

May be considered in selected patients with inadequate response, subject to tolerability and safety.

Lower target

Older or medically vulnerable patients may sometimes be managed at lower concentrations.

Maintenance Review

  • Current mood stability.
  • Number of breakthrough episodes.
  • Adherence.
  • Serum lithium trend.
  • Renal trajectory.
  • TSH trajectory.
  • Calcium.
  • Weight and tremor.
  • Polyuria/polydipsia.
  • Drug interactions.

10 Bipolar Depression

Lithium has an important role in the overall management of bipolar disorder, particularly for long-term mood stabilisation and relapse prevention. Its use in an acute depressive episode should be individualised according to the overall bipolar treatment plan.

Assess first

  • Current depressive severity
  • Suicide risk
  • Mixed features
  • Psychotic symptoms
  • Previous response

Target

A practical augmentation target may fall around 0.6–1.0 mmol/L, depending on indication and individual circumstances.

11 If Lithium Is Not Working

First Verify

  1. Diagnosis: Is the presentation truly bipolar disorder / mania?
  2. Adherence: Is lithium actually being taken consistently?
  3. Level: Was the sample collected at the correct time?
  4. Duration: Has there been sufficient treatment duration?
  5. Interactions: Are other drugs changing lithium exposure?
  6. Comorbidity: Are substances, personality factors, sleep deprivation, medical illness or psychosocial stressors contributing?
Do not label lithium failure based on a single low or poorly timed serum concentration.

Alopecia Consider dose relationship and other causes.
Oedema Evaluate clinically and review other causes.

13 Lithium Toxicity

Suspect toxicity clinically. Symptoms may include gastrointestinal disturbance, coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, drowsiness, muscle weakness, abnormal movements, seizures or reduced consciousness.

Important Risk Factors

Volume depletion

  • Vomiting
  • Diarrhoea
  • Fever
  • Heavy sweating
  • Dehydration

Sodium depletion

  • Low-salt diet
  • Salt loss
  • Significant fluid shifts

Drug interactions

  • NSAIDs
  • ACE inhibitors
  • ARBs
  • Thiazides
  • Some other interacting agents

Renal impairment

  • CKD
  • Acute kidney injury
  • Age-related renal decline

Approximate Concentration–Toxicity Relationship

Lithium concentration Clinical significance
>1.5 mmol/L GI and CNS toxicity may occur, depending on clinical context.
>2.0 mmol/L More severe neurological manifestations may occur.
>3.0 mmol/L Severe poisoning; renal replacement therapy may be considered depending on clinical circumstances.
These thresholds are not standalone dialysis rules. Clinical severity, renal function, chronicity, lithium kinetics, neurological status and trajectory must be considered.

Immediate Clinical Approach

  1. Stop further lithium temporarily in suspected significant toxicity.
  2. Check serum lithium urgently.
  3. Check renal function and electrolytes.
  4. Assess hydration and volume status.
  5. Assess neurological status.
  6. Identify interacting medications.
  7. Manage dehydration/electrolyte abnormalities appropriately.
  8. Discuss severe toxicity urgently with nephrology/critical care/toxicology as appropriate.

14 Drug Interactions

Drug / class Potential effect Clinical action
NSAIDs May increase lithium concentration Avoid casual OTC NSAID use; monitor closely if required.
ACE inhibitors May increase lithium exposure Monitor closely after initiation/change.
ARBs May increase lithium exposure Monitor lithium and renal function.
Thiazide diuretics Can substantially increase lithium High-risk interaction; requires careful management.
Loop diuretics May alter lithium levels Monitor when started/stopped.
Metronidazole May increase lithium concentration Consider alternative or monitor carefully.
Calcium-channel blockers Potential neurological/toxic effects reported Clinical monitoring.
Carbamazepine May increase neurotoxicity Use cautiously and monitor clinically.
Phenytoin Potential neurotoxicity/interactions Monitor clinically.
Serotonergic antidepressants Potential serotonergic toxicity Monitor for relevant symptoms.
Antipsychotics Rare but important neurotoxicity may occur with combinations. Monitor for rigidity, confusion, altered consciousness and other neurological changes.
Caffeine / theophylline Changes in intake can alter lithium clearance. Maintain reasonably consistent intake and review major changes.
Ask specifically about OTC ibuprofen, naproxen and other NSAIDs when prescribing lithium.

15 Renal Issues

Lithium is predominantly renally cleared. Therefore, changes in renal function can alter lithium exposure and increase toxicity risk.

What to Monitor

  • Serum creatinine
  • eGFR
  • Electrolytes
  • Urine symptoms
  • Polyuria
  • Polydipsia
  • Episodes of dehydration
  • Serum lithium trajectory
A single creatinine value is less informative than the longitudinal renal trajectory.

Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus

Long-term lithium exposure may impair renal concentrating ability, leading to polyuria and polydipsia.

When symptoms are persistent, evaluate renal function, electrolytes and the possibility of lithium-associated nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

16 Thyroid & Calcium

Hypothyroidism

Lithium can impair thyroid function. Monitor TSH ± FT4.

Goitre

Can occur with or without biochemical hypothyroidism.

Hyperparathyroidism

Lithium can be associated with elevated calcium and altered parathyroid physiology.

Important Clinical Principle

Development of hypothyroidism does not automatically mean lithium must be stopped. Thyroid replacement and continued lithium may be appropriate when the psychiatric indication remains strong.

When Calcium Is Raised

  1. Repeat calcium.
  2. Assess albumin/corrected calcium as appropriate.
  3. Consider PTH measurement.
  4. Review lithium exposure.
  5. Evaluate other causes of hypercalcaemia.

18 Pregnancy

Lithium during pregnancy requires an individualised risk-benefit assessment involving psychiatric and obstetric considerations.

Important Considerations

  • First-trimester exposure is associated with a relative increase in congenital cardiac malformation risk, but the absolute risk remains low.
  • Pregnancy alters fluid balance and renal physiology, which can affect lithium concentrations.
  • Dehydration during pregnancy can increase toxicity risk.
  • Neonatal lithium exposure can be associated with neonatal toxicity and disturbances including thyroid and renal concentrating effects.
  • Near delivery, obstetric and neonatal teams should be aware of maternal lithium exposure.

19 Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding decisions with lithium require individualised risk-benefit assessment because lithium passes into breast milk and infant exposure can be clinically relevant.

If Breastfeeding Is Considered

  • Discuss maternal psychiatric relapse risk.
  • Assess infant age and health.
  • Consider neonatal/paediatric input.
  • Monitor the infant when clinically appropriate.
  • Ensure clear communication between psychiatry and paediatrics.
Do not make breastfeeding decisions based solely on a generic online statement. Use current local/perinatal prescribing guidance.

20 Discontinuation

Avoid abrupt discontinuation whenever clinically possible because rapid withdrawal can be associated with relapse.

Tapering

  • A practical minimum taper may be around 4 weeks.
  • The supplied Maudsley-based reference favours a slower taper, preferably around 3 months where feasible.
  • Avoid unnecessarily large sudden reductions in serum exposure.
  • Monitor for early manic or depressive relapse during and after taper.

21 Ward-Round Checklist

1. Current dose

Exact dose + formulation + dosing schedule.

2. Last level

Value + date + timing relative to dose.

3. Clinical response

Mania/depression/irritability/sleep/psychosis.

4. Toxicity

Tremor, GI symptoms, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion.

5. Renal

Creatinine + eGFR + hydration status.

6. Thyroid

TSH ± FT4.

7. Calcium

Check according to monitoring schedule.

8. Interactions

NSAIDs, ACEi, ARBs, diuretics and new medicines.

One-Minute Ward Question Set

  1. What is the lithium dose and formulation?
  2. When was the last dose?
  3. When was the last lithium level?
  4. Was it a true 12-hour level?
  5. What is the current clinical response?
  6. Any tremor or GI symptoms?
  7. Any ataxia or dysarthria?
  8. Any vomiting/diarrhoea/dehydration?
  9. Any new medications?
  10. What are creatinine/eGFR and sodium?
  11. What is the latest TSH?
  12. What is the latest calcium?

24 Clinical Pearls

A lithium concentration without the time of the last dose and time of blood collection is difficult to interpret accurately.

Neurotoxicity may occasionally occur despite apparently therapeutic serum concentrations. Clinical assessment remains essential.

Patients frequently do not consider ibuprofen or naproxen to be "medicines" worth mentioning. Specifically ask about OTC NSAID use.

Vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, sweating or reduced fluid intake can increase lithium exposure.

Longitudinal creatinine/eGFR trends should be reviewed whenever possible.

Different brands may have different release characteristics. Confirm whether the product is IR, SR, CR, PR or XR before switching.

When lithium is otherwise clinically beneficial, thyroid dysfunction can often be managed concurrently with appropriate endocrine treatment.

For clinical continuity, record the exact brand, strength and formulation whenever possible, particularly when patients obtain medicines from different pharmacies.

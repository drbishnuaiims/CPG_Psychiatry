Important terminology: the guideline deliberately avoids the term “mood stabilizer” because it is used inconsistently in the literature. “Divalproex” encompasses valproate, valpromide, valproic acid and divalproex sodium. “Maintenance” means prophylactic treatment after stabilization of acute mania or depression.

03. Diagnostic Assessment — Do Not Diagnose Bipolarity from a Checklist Alone

Bipolar I

Defined by the occurrence of a threshold manic episode. Mania may include inflated self-esteem/grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, psychomotor agitation and risky behaviour, with marked functional impairment and possible psychosis or hospitalization.

Bipolar II

Requires hypomania + threshold major depressive episodes. Hypomania is qualitatively similar to mania but is not sufficiently severe/durable to cause marked functional impairment, hospitalization or psychosis.

Cyclothymia

Chronic subthreshold hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria for hypomanic/manic or major depressive episodes.

DSM-5 related categories

DSM-5 replaced bipolar NOS with other specified bipolar and related disorder and unspecified bipolar and related disorder, and includes substance/medication-induced bipolar and related disorder and bipolar/related disorder due to another medical condition.

Common diagnostic traps

  • MDD: actively probe for past mania/hypomania before assigning unipolar depression.
  • Schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder: establish whether psychosis occurs outside prominent mood episodes; assess longitudinal course and family history.
  • Borderline personality disorder: chronic interpersonal instability, unstable self-image, abandonment fears and impulsivity; true sustained euphoria and prolonged well-functioning intervals are uncommon.
  • ADHD: lifelong trait-like symptoms and developmental history can mimic distractibility/activation but differ from episodic mania.
  • Substance/medication-induced states: ask specifically about stimulants, steroids, L-dopa, antidepressants, illicit drugs and withdrawal/intoxication.
  • Medical/neurological causes: consider thyroid disease, Cushing disease, stroke, MS, traumatic brain injury, frontal lesions and other relevant disorders.
  • Late first mania: a first manic presentation after age 50 warrants careful assessment for an organic cause.
MDQ: useful only as an adjunctive screening tool in clinical populations. The guideline notes poor sensitivity/specificity, especially in community/highly comorbid populations. Do not use an MDQ score as a diagnostic or treatment-planning substitute.

Collateral + longitudinal diagnosis

  • Obtain collateral from family/friends whenever possible.
  • Ask first-degree relatives about periods of increased activity, irritability and behavioural change.
  • Use prospective mood charting where diagnosis is uncertain.
  • Do not rely only on the patient's recall during severe depression; concentration and memory may be impaired.

04. Suicide Risk — Mandatory in Every Clinical Interaction

6–7%

Approximate proportion of identified patients with BD reported in the guideline as dying by suicide.

43%

Reported suicidal ideation within the past year in the cited worldwide survey.

21%

Reported suicide plan within the past year.

16%

Reported suicide attempt within the past year.

High-risk windows: the guideline reports that 14% of suicides occurred during an inpatient stay and another 26% within 6 weeks of discharge. Discharge planning therefore needs to be treated as an active suicide-prevention intervention, not an administrative endpoint.

Bedside suicide assessment

Do not substitute a numerical risk score for clinical formulation. The guideline specifically states that risk-stratification tools are not sufficiently accurate for clinical prediction; focus on modifiable risk factors and safety interventions.

Lithium and suicide: the guideline cites evidence suggesting lithium may contribute to prevention of suicide attempts/deaths, with anticonvulsants possibly contributing to a lesser extent; it also stresses that more comparative data are needed.

05. Foundations of Long-Term Management

Chronic disease model

BD is chronic, recurrent and remitting. Management therefore requires a multidisciplinary, longitudinal system, not episode-only prescribing.

Shared decision-making

Discuss options with the patient and caregiver where appropriate, incorporating preference, previous response, safety, tolerability and practical adherence.

Team-based care

Ideally involve at least one additional health professional, commonly nursing, for psychoeducation, monitoring, psychosocial support and community linkage.

Primary care integration

Stable patients may be followed in primary care with mental-health-system support and continuity of care.

What should be tracked longitudinally?

  • Mood polarity and subsyndromal symptoms.
  • Sleep/wake rhythm.
  • Cognition.
  • Occupational/social functioning.
  • Quality of life.
  • Medication adherence and adverse effects.
  • Substance use.
  • Early warning signs and individualized relapse signature.
Mood diary / life chart: can help identify early relapse signals and relationships between mood and treatment/lifestyle factors such as diet, exercise and stress. The guideline acknowledges that daily completion can be burdensome; simplify the monitoring plan when needed.

Illness burden

The guideline reports that people with BD experience syndromal or subsyndromal symptoms—especially depression—for approximately half of their lives and have substantial functional and quality-of-life impairment. Depression, greater episode burden/duration and lower cognition are associated with greater impairment.

Stigma

Stigma may reduce help-seeking, social support, treatment engagement, functioning and quality of life. Psychoeducation should address self-stigma, coping, empowerment and help-seeking rather than framing BD as personal weakness or moral failure.

07. Acute Mania — OPD/IPD Algorithm

Step 1 is not “pick a drug”. First determine violence/aggression risk, suicide risk (especially mixed features), psychosis, insight, ability to adhere, substance use, social support and the need for inpatient care.

Step 1 — Rule out contributors

  • Drugs of abuse/intoxication/withdrawal.
  • Medication-induced symptoms: particularly antidepressants and other activating agents.
  • Medical/endocrine/neurological causes.
  • Review current medication, dose, adherence and trough levels where relevant.
  • For established BD: start antimanic treatment promptly when clinically indicated.
  • For first-ever manic symptoms: confirm longitudinal diagnosis after stopping possible offending antidepressants and obtaining collateral.
  • Support cessation of stimulant use, including excessive caffeine, and reduce/stop alcohol/drugs as clinically appropriate.

08. Acute Bipolar I Depression

Step 1: assess severity, suicidality/self-harm, mixed symptoms, psychosis, catatonia, functional impairment, adherence, support network and need for inpatient care. Exclude alcohol/drug, medication and medical causes.

First-line treatment

Agent / strategyEvidencePractical note from guideline
QuetiapineLevel 1Monotherapy; target dose in trials/guideline discussion 300 mg/day. 300 and 600 mg/day showed no efficacy difference; lower doses were not studied for bipolar depression.
Lurasidone + lithium/divalproexLevel 1Adjunctive first-line option.
LithiumLevel 2Guideline opinion: trough 0.8–1.2 mEq/L for acute bipolar depression; also valuable for antimanic and maintenance prophylaxis.
LamotrigineLevel 2Monotherapy; target at least 200 mg/day, but slow titration limits rapid-response utility.
LurasidoneLevel 2Monotherapy; early separation from placebo makes it useful when rapid response is important.
Adjunctive lamotrigineLevel 2Useful particularly when already on lithium or another evidence-based agent.
Hierarchy matters longitudinally: the guideline explicitly considers efficacy across acute and maintenance phases, safety/tolerability and switch risk. Patient-specific prior response/non-response and clinical features can justify a different choice.

Step 3 — switch vs add-on

  • Optimize dose first and address adherence.
  • Assess whether the current drug is serving another long-term goal (e.g., lithium for antimanic prophylaxis).
  • If the current drug remains useful for another component of BD, add-on therapy may be rational.
  • If another agent is already adequately providing the needed prophylaxis, a switch can reduce polypharmacy.
  • When all else is equal, the guideline favors switching to limit polypharmacy, but rational combination therapy is often required in real patients.
  • Switch medications with an overlap/taper approach unless abrupt discontinuation is medically necessary.

Third-line bipolar depression

  • Carbamazepine monotherapy.
  • Olanzapine monotherapy.
  • Adjunctive aripiprazole, armodafinil, asenapine, levothyroxine, modafinil or pramipexole.
  • Adjunctive rTMS.
  • Adjunctive SNRI/MAOI only with adequate antimanic prophylaxis; these classes have higher switch potential than SSRIs/bupropion.
  • Adjunctive EPA, N-acetylcysteine and light therapy ± total sleep deprivation in selected cases.
  • IV ketamine as an adjunctive third-line strategy for severe/refractory depression or significant suicidal ideation; may be considered earlier if rapid response is imperative, but long-term data are limited and abuse/misuse and possible switch need consideration.

Not recommended for acute bipolar I depression in the source

  • Antidepressant monotherapy — Level 2 negative.
  • Aripiprazole monotherapy — Level 1 negative.
  • Ziprasidone monotherapy or adjunctive therapy — Level 1 negative.
  • Lamotrigine + folic acid — Level 2 negative.
  • Adjunctive mifepristone — Level 2 negative.

09. Bipolar I Maintenance

Maintenance is not optional after stabilization for most patients. The guideline states that almost all individuals with BD require maintenance treatment to prevent recurrence, reduce residual symptoms and restore functioning/quality of life.

  • Quetiapine + lithium/divalproex — Level 1.
  • Aripiprazole + lithium/divalproex — Level 2.

→

→

Second-line maintenance

  • Olanzapine — Level 1 efficacy, but second-line due to metabolic/safety concerns.
  • Long-acting injectable risperidone — evidence for prevention of any mood/manic episode; less clear for depression.
  • Carbamazepine — Level 2.
  • Paliperidone — Level 2.
  • Other second-line agents/strategies are described in the source table; selection should be individualized by polarity, response and tolerability.

Antipsychotic continuation after acute mania

When an atypical antipsychotic was combined with lithium/divalproex for acute mania, continuing it for the first 6 months after response reduced recurrence risk in the cited evidence. Benefit beyond 6 months was uncertain; the guideline recommends reassessing risk-benefit after 6 months of sustained response.

10. Bipolar II Disorder

Hypomania

Evidence is limited. When hypomania is frequent, severe or impairing enough to require treatment, clinical experience supports lithium or divalproex and/or an atypical antipsychotic. N-acetylcysteine may be useful, but further evidence is needed.

11. Special Populations

Pregnancy

  • Before treatment in a woman of childbearing potential, assess pregnancy status and discuss reproductive plans.
  • Where possible, psychosocial strategies are preferred during the first trimester because teratogenic risk is highest.
  • If medication is required, prefer monotherapy at the lowest effective dose where feasible.
  • Pregnancy requires close monitoring and appropriate fetal screening; the source specifically mentions fetal ultrasound when lithium is used in the first trimester.
  • Divalproex should be avoided in pregnancy in the guideline because of neural-tube and other congenital/neurodevelopmental risks.
  • Physiological changes later in pregnancy may increase medication clearance; doses may require adjustment.
  • The source recommends prenatal vitamins including high-dose folic acid and discusses possible choline supplementation; reproductive counseling should be individualized and based on current evidence.
  • Lamotrigine and carbamazepine may affect oral-contraceptive efficacy; this should be discussed.

Postpartum

  • Postpartum is a period of markedly increased recurrence risk.
  • The source reports postpartum mood episodes in 66% of women medication-free during pregnancy vs 23% among those on treatment in the cited data.
  • Restart/optimize maintenance treatment as soon as possible after delivery, favoring a medication that previously worked.
  • Monitor closely around delivery for early mood or psychotic symptoms.
  • If an acute episode occurs, use the usual bipolar treatment hierarchy but incorporate breastfeeding safety.
  • The source identifies evidence for lithium, antipsychotics and benzodiazepines in postpartum mania and quetiapine for postpartum bipolar depression, but notes limited data.
  • Breastfeeding decisions should weigh medication exposure, infant monitoring and the mother's need for adequate sleep. Night-time feeding by a partner/support person or formula supplementation may help protect sleep.
  • In postpartum psychosis/mania, breastfeeding may be unsafe if the mother is too disorganized to care for the infant safely.

Older adults

  • Late-onset mania should prompt careful search for neurological/medical contributors.
  • Older adults often have more cognitive/depressive symptoms and may present with insomnia, aggression, impulsivity and self-neglect.
  • Medical comorbidity is common: metabolic syndrome, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, arthritis and endocrine disorders.
  • Perform thorough physical/neurological assessment and coordinate care with other medical providers.
  • Age-related pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic changes increase the importance of low starting doses, slow titration and interaction review.

12. Comorbidities

General hierarchy

When multiple conditions compete for attention: treat the disorder/symptom with the greatest morbidity/mortality first—examples include acute mania, psychosis or suicidal ideation. Substance use may be treated concurrently or sequentially depending on severity and its contribution to mood instability. Once mood is stable, address ADHD, anxiety and metabolic disorders according to impact and patient preference.

Substance use disorders

  • Comorbid SUD is common and associated with lower remission, more hospitalization and increased suicide-attempt risk.
  • Address substance use early because it can interfere with bipolar treatment.
  • Withdrawal management and safety may determine inpatient vs community treatment.
  • Review caffeine/stimulant use, alcohol and illicit substances whenever mood destabilizes.

Anxiety disorders

  • Anxiety is common and may increase persistence of depression and suicidal ideation.
  • Choose agents that can cover both conditions where appropriate; the guideline notes anxiolytic benefits with quetiapine and some evidence for divalproex/olanzapine in selected contexts.
  • Be cautious with antidepressants because mood elevation can occur.

ADHD

  • Stabilize bipolar mood first.
  • Stimulants may destabilize mood in some patients; if used, do so after stabilization with close monitoring.
  • Distinguish trait-like ADHD symptoms from episodic manic activation.

Metabolic disorders

Higher metabolic concern in sourceLower relative metabolic impact in source
Clozapine, olanzapine; quetiapine particularly at higher doses; risperidone; lithium/divalproex also associated with weight gain.Aripiprazole, ziprasidone, asenapine and lurasidone have comparatively more favorable metabolic profiles.
  • Implement lifestyle intervention: diet, physical activity, smoking/substance management.
  • If metabolic/weight problems persist and the therapeutic advantage of a high-risk drug is small, consider switching to a more favorable option.
  • Actively manage dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular risk rather than treating them as secondary concerns.

Key adverse-effect domains

Neurological

EPS, akathisia, dystonia, parkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia; NMS is rare but life-threatening. Risk rises with high doses, rapid changes, IM/IV administration, dehydration, restraint, older age and medical/psychiatric comorbidity.

Endocrine

Lithium: hypothyroidism and hyperparathyroidism. Divalproex: menstrual irregularity/possible PCOS and metabolic effects. Antipsychotics: hyperprolactinemia with some agents.

Cardiac

Lithium and several antipsychotics may affect QT/T-wave/arrhythmia risk. Lurasidone and aripiprazole are described as comparatively favorable from a cardiac perspective, though hypotension can occur with aripiprazole.

Metabolic

Clozapine/olanzapine carry greatest metabolic risk in the source, followed by quetiapine and risperidone. Lithium/divalproex can also cause weight gain.

Dermatological

Lamotrigine: common non-serious rash, rare serious SJS/TEN; slow titration reduces risk. Carbamazepine also carries SJS/TEN risk, particularly early in treatment.

Cognition

Cognitive impairment may reflect illness itself; avoid unnecessarily exacerbating cognitive problems with treatment when alternatives exist.

Lamotrigine/carbamazepine rash rule: patients should be told to report any rash or mucosal ulcer immediately. Serious rash requires urgent assessment and may require stopping the medication.

15. High-Yield Clinical Pearls

1. “Bipolar depression” is not “MDD + a mood stabilizer.”
First confirm bipolarity longitudinally. The treatment hierarchy is different, and antidepressant monotherapy is not recommended for bipolar I depression.
2. A manic patient on an antidepressant: review it immediately.
The guideline recommends discontinuing antidepressants in mania and assessing whether symptoms persist after discontinuation, particularly when the diagnosis is not previously established.
3. Mixed features change the risk-benefit calculation.
Mixed features are associated with greater severity and suicide risk. Atypical antipsychotics/divalproex are supported; antidepressants should be avoided or used with substantial caution.
4. “Non-response” is often a process problem.
Before switching, verify diagnosis, therapeutic dose/level, duration, adherence, substance use and medical contributors.
5. Choose acute drugs with maintenance in mind.
The 2018 hierarchy deliberately considers efficacy across acute and maintenance phases, safety/tolerability and switch risk.
6. Depression + imminent suicide/catatonia/psychosis = think ECT early.
ECT is second-line in the guideline and can be considered earlier when rapid response is imperative.
7. Lamotrigine is not a rapid-response drug.
Slow titration is necessary because of serious rash risk; target at least 200 mg/day in the bipolar I depression discussion.
8. Lithium monitoring is a system, not a single serum level.
Track trough level, renal function, thyroid function, calcium, interacting drugs and clinical status. Recheck after dose changes and relevant interacting-medication changes.
9. Antipsychotic monitoring starts before the first dose.
Document weight/BMI, BP and metabolic labs, then continue structured follow-up. Do not wait for weight gain or diabetes to appear.
10. Discharge is a high-risk clinical transition.
The guideline reports substantial suicide risk during hospitalization and in the first 6 weeks after discharge; safety planning, family involvement and rapid follow-up matter.

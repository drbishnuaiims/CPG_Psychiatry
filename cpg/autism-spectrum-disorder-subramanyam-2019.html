Source boundary — read this first

This page is a structured study note derived from the uploaded 2019 Indian Journal of Psychiatry guideline by Subramanyam, Mukherjee, Dave and Chavda. It preserves the source article’s terminology, recommendations, tables and historical framing rather than silently updating them to 2026 practice. Medication doses and regulatory statements below are therefore source-era information and must be checked against current prescribing information and local/current guidelines before clinical use. The article itself notes limitations from the lack of systematic Indian research.

01 · Quick clinical map

MDT

Assessment

Psychiatrist + psychologist + special educator + occupational therapist + audiologist/speech therapist, with the pediatrician involved for physical comorbidity.

Early

Intervention

The guideline places early diagnosis and early intervention at the centre of care and describes chronic, multidisciplinary support aimed at functioning and quality of life.

Target

Symptoms

Medication is framed as symptomatic treatment for clinically significant associated problems—not a cure and not a replacement for behavioural, educational and developmental programming.

Core principle
Autism is presented as a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition requiring individualized, multidisciplinary and often long-term support. The guideline discusses a movement from a purely medical model toward disability, inclusion and neurodiversity perspectives.
1Recognize risk

Developmental surveillance, parent/school/pediatric concerns and early symptomatic markers.

2Screen

M-CHAT/M-CHAT-R/F and other screening instruments support—not replace—clinical assessment.

3Diagnose + characterize

Clinical judgment + developmental history + observation + cognitive/language/comorbidity assessment.

4Intervene

Developmental, behavioural, educational, communication, family and symptom-targeted interventions.

High-yield distinction
Screening ≠ diagnosis. A positive screen requires a thorough diagnostic evaluation; no single diagnostic instrument should be the sole basis for an ASD diagnosis.

02 · Classification, conceptual framework & scope

The guideline was constructed by amalgamating expert guidelines from across the world with existing practices in India. The authors explicitly identify the limited systematic Indian evidence base as a limitation. They also highlight the Indian indigenous assessment tool incorporated in the appendix.

Neurobiology: what the 2019 source says

The article describes multiple-gene involvement, including genes related to synaptic plasticity/scaffolding, receptors, cell adhesion, chromatin remodeling, transcription, protein synthesis/degradation and actin-cytoskeleton dynamics. Examples named include neuroligins, SHANKs, CNTNAP2 and FMR1. It discusses cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits for repetitive behaviours, VTA–nucleus accumbens connectivity for social interaction and amygdala–ventral hippocampal connectivity for social interaction.

Evidence boundary
The article states that translation of these neurobiological findings into structural diagnostic neuroimaging had not been established and that diagnostic modalities to demonstrate proposed excessive synapses from slow pruning were still awaited.

03 · Process of assessment

Because ASD is complex, assessment should, as far as possible, be multidisciplinary. The guideline names the psychiatrist, psychologist, special educator, occupational therapist, audiologist and speech therapist, with the child’s pediatrician kept involved for physical comorbidities.

History

  • Birth history
  • Current health
  • Family history
  • Longitudinal developmental trajectory
  • Current ASD symptoms and change over time

Examination & associated conditions

  • Growth and nutritional status
  • Dysmorphic features
  • Neurological examination
  • Skin evaluation
  • Audiological assessment
  • Physical and mental health comorbidities
Actively rule out
Hearing impairment and other conditions that may mimic or worsen developmental/communication problems.
Actively evaluate
Seizures and other comorbidities; search for underlying etiologies such as genetic syndromes when clinically indicated.

Investigations described

InvestigationRole in the source guideline
Genetic testingChromosomes, fragile X and microarray are mentioned as useful depending on the case.

Brain imagingOptional/as appropriate; not presented as a routine diagnostic test for ASD.
Metabolic testingOptional/as appropriate according to the nature of the case.
Clinical formulation
The assessment is intended to distinguish relatively straightforward (“essential”) from more complex autism, inform management/prognosis, and provide a foundation for parent psychoeducation.

04 · Early screening, biomarkers & assessment tools

Screening recommendations cited in the guideline

AAP developmental surveillance

The guideline cites screening for developmental delays/disabilities during well-child visits at 9, 18, and 24 or 30 months.

Child & adolescent psychiatry surveillance

ASD surveillance at developmental/psychiatric assessments, with ASD-specific screening such as M-CHAT at 18 and 24 months or whenever surveillance raises concern.

M-CHAT-R principle
M-CHAT-R is designed to maximize sensitivity and therefore has a high false-positive rate. A screen-positive child still requires evaluation; even children who do not receive an ASD diagnosis may have other developmental disorders/delays.

M-CHAT-R scoring algorithm reproduced from the source

For all items except 2, 5 and 12, a “NO” response indicates ASD risk. For items 2, 5 and 12, a “YES” response indicates ASD risk.

0–2Low risk
3–7Medium risk
8–20High risk
2+After follow-up = positive
0–1After follow-up = negative
  • Low risk (0–2): if under 24 months, rescreen after the second birthday; otherwise no further action unless surveillance indicates risk.
  • Medium risk (3–7): administer M-CHAT-R/F follow-up. If follow-up remains ≥2, refer for diagnostic and early-intervention eligibility evaluation. If follow-up is 0–1, rescreen later if surveillance remains concerning.
  • High risk (8–20): the follow-up may be bypassed and the child referred immediately for diagnostic evaluation and early-intervention eligibility evaluation.

  2. Have you ever wondered if the child might be deaf?
  3. Does the child play pretend or make-believe?
  4. Does the child like climbing on things?
  5. Does the child make unusual finger movements near the eyes?
  6. Does the child point with one finger to ask for something/help?

  8. Is the child interested in other children?

  15. Does the child try to copy what you do?

Do not over-rely on instruments
Diagnostic instruments supplement but do not replace informed clinical judgment. Screening instruments do not establish a diagnosis.

Caregiver questionnaires also mentioned include the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS/SRS-2) and the Social Communication Questionnaire.

Cognitive, language and psychiatric comorbidity

  • Assess cognitive and language impairment/ability as DSM-5 specifier domains.
  • Consider emotional and behavioural disorders in all patients with ASD.
  • ADHD is highlighted as an important comorbidity and confounder; the source cites reported ADHD symptom prevalence of 13%–50% among people with primary ASD.
  • Intellectual disability is difficult to distinguish from ASD at times and is described as highly comorbid, around 30%–45% in the paper.
  • Where diagnostic uncertainty persists, serial evaluation and observation can help clarify the final formulation.
  • The paper mentions an 84-item ASD-Ca scale for comorbid psychopathology in adults with intellectual disability.

06 · Differential diagnosis

The guideline recommends approaching differential diagnosis by specific core domains rather than trying to establish one enormous differential for ASD as a whole. This is intended to reduce both over-diagnosis and under-diagnosis.

Core ASD domain / clinical featureDifferentials listed in the guideline
Language / communication difficultyLanguage disorder; expressive or mixed receptive-expressive language disorder; speech sound/phonological disorder; social (pragmatic) communication disorder; selective mutism.
Social interaction difficultyADHD; anxiety disorders, particularly social anxiety/social phobia; major depressive disorder; personality disorders.
Restricted / repetitive behaviourStereotypic movement disorder; OCD; tic disorders including Tourette syndrome.
Multiple-domain difficultiesIntellectual disability; global developmental delay; reactive attachment disorder; childhood-onset schizophrenia; traumatic brain injury; neurobehavioural disorder associated with prenatal alcohol exposure/fetal alcohol syndrome; genetic or metabolic syndromes.

07 · Approach to treatment

Treatment philosophy
Multisensory + multidisciplinary + early. The source emphasizes early intervention, chronic management, functional independence, quality of life, social communication, learning, socialization, reduction of maladaptive behaviour, and family education/support.

Developmental

Build social communication, emotional relationships, social skills, imitation, joint attention and adaptive functioning.

Educational

Individualized educational planning, structured teaching and visual supports.

Symptom-targeted

Medication and behavioural approaches for clinically significant irritability, hyperactivity, anxiety, mood symptoms, sleep disturbance and other associated problems.

Important source statement
The guideline states that efficacious reversal of core autistic symptoms had not been accomplished at the time of publication. Treatment is therefore framed around development, adaptation, functioning, symptom reduction and quality of life rather than a “cure”.

08 · Non-pharmacological, developmental & educational interventions

Structured educational and behavioural interventions

Early Start Denver Model

Targets development across domains using assessment-derived goals, with emphasis on social communication, interpersonal engagement, imitation, social attention and motivation.

Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA)

Uses discrete trials and progression from simple to complex skills. Targets social skills, communication, reading, academics, adaptive learning, fine motor skills, hygiene/grooming, domestic capabilities, punctuality and job competence. The paper describes use across home, school, workplace and clinic settings and states that consistent ABA can improve skills/behaviour and reduce need for special services.

ABA intensity in the source
The article says that ideally more than 20 hours/week under age 4 is recommended. Table 7 describes intensive teaching of 20–40 h/week for 1–4 years in ABA-based early intensive behavioural intervention. This is a source-era recommendation, not a current universal prescription.

SCERTS

SCERTS incorporates practices from ABA, TEACCH, Floortime and RDI, with particular emphasis on child-initiated communication in everyday activities and spontaneous application of functional skills across settings and partners.

SC

Social Communication

Spontaneous functional communication, emotional expression and secure/trusting relationships.

ER

Emotional Regulation

Ability to maintain a regulated state to cope with stress and remain available for learning/interacting.

TS

Transactional Support

Modify/adapt environment and provide tools such as picture communication, written schedules and sensory supports; support family/professional teamwork.

Developmental interventions

DIR/Floortime (developmental, individual-difference, relationship-based) and RDI (relationship development intervention) are described as approaches focused on emotional relationships, social communication and social skills.

Communication interventions

  • Sign language
  • Communication boards
  • Visual supports
  • Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS)
  • Social stories
  • Social skills training

PECS

For individuals with minimal/no verbal ability; teaches approaching another person and exchanging a picture for the desired item, extending to requests, thoughts and other symbolizable communication. The source describes usefulness at home and in the classroom.

Social Stories

Describe a context, skill, achievement or concept using a patient/supportive format designed to be descriptive, meaningful and physically, socially and emotionally safe. For more advanced communication/older children, the paper also mentions social articles.

Social skills training

Uses a graded, stepwise approach to expected social behaviour, with the aim of facilitating real-world friendships and social functioning.

Educational assistance: TEACCH

Structured TEACCHing is presented as a framework rather than a curriculum. It uses strengths in visual information processing while addressing social communication, attention and executive-function difficulties.

Four framework elements

  1. Physical organization
  2. Individualized schedules
  3. Work/activity systems
  4. Visual structure of materials in tasks and activities
Goal
Meaningful engagement, flexibility, independence and self-efficacy.

Sensory integration

The guideline describes occupational-therapy sensory integration therapy as using a specially designed environment to stimulate/challenge senses, based on the assumption of over- or under-responsivity, with the aim of improving processing of sensory information and adaptive daily functioning.

Other approaches

  • CBT: some evidence for anxiety and anger management in high-functioning youth with ASD.
  • Animal-assisted therapy, particularly trained dogs: described as increasingly popular; the paper discusses a proposed nonverbal bond/oxytocin mechanism for social bonding and empathy.

09 · Pharmacological treatment

Medication principle
Medication is indicated when non-pharmacological intervention is insufficient or when problem behaviour has a negative impact on functioning. If effective, medication is symptomatic treatment and is not a cure or substitute for behavioural/educational programming.

Drug classes named in the guideline

AntipsychoticsStimulantsSSRIsα2 agonistsAnticonvulsants / mood stabilizersAnxiolytics / benzodiazepinesSleep medication

Associated symptoms / ADHD

  • Risperidone and aripiprazole are described as effective for irritability, stereotypy and hyperactivity.
  • Methylphenidate is described as effective in reducing ADHD symptoms.
  • Atomoxetine and α2 agonists appear effective for ADHD symptoms.
  • SSRIs are stated to be ineffective for improving repetitive behaviours in children with ASD and may frequently cause activating adverse events.
  • Efficacy of antiepileptic drugs is described as inconclusive.
  • Glutamatergic agents and oxytocin are described as promising but with mixed results.

Serious behavioural disturbance / irritability

Severe tantrums, aggression and self-injury are described as frequent. The guideline recommends a multimodal approach. Mild irritability may benefit from an α2-adrenergic agonist. The paper states that risperidone and aripiprazole were the two FDA-approved atypical antipsychotics for irritability in children/adolescents with autism at the time of publication.

Expert opinion in the source
Psychopharmacological treatment of core and associated symptoms is challenging; risperidone and aripiprazole had the most reliable evidence for severely disruptive behaviour/hyperactivity; randomized placebo-controlled medication studies were described as scarce.

12 · Psychoeducation for the family

How the source frames the diagnosis
Parents may find the diagnosis difficult to receive. The guideline recommends communicating clearly that ASD is a neurodevelopmental disability, is lifelong, begins in utero, is not caused by vaccines or bad parenting, and varies substantially between individuals.
  • Autism is a neurodevelopmental disability.
  • It is lifelong.
  • It starts in utero.
  • It is not produced by vaccines.
  • It is not caused by bad parenting.
  • All children may not be similar.
  • Early therapy helps.
  • Education may not be the only aim.
  • Discuss ASD openly.
  • Connect with other parents of children with ASD.
  • The path can be difficult; seek help at each step.
  • Give examples of individuals with ASD.

13 · Special clinical issues

Distress, anxiety & agitation

Assess

  • Communication of needs/desires and comprehension.
  • Ability to process new information and use augmented communication (the figure gives an iPad as an example).
  • Problems with change in routine.
  • Sensory sensitivity to sounds, lights, colours, textures, smells and touch; hypo- or hypersensitivity.
  • Past aggression, agitation or self-harm, including triggers and helpful measures.
  • Behavioural variation between school and home.
  • Comforting strategies, including deep pressure/squeezing/holding.
  • Eye contact.

Non-pharmacological approaches in Figure 3

  1. Maintain a low-stimulus environment.
  2. Limit the number of professionals dealing with the person.
  3. Minimize delays and prioritize procedures.
  4. Liberalize diet.

Pharmacological approaches shown in Figure 3

  • Clonazepam: <10 years up to 0.5 mg; >10 years 0.5–1 mg.
  • Lorazepam: >13 years 0.02–0.05 mg/kg, maximum 2 mg.
  • Risperidone: >5 years; 15–20 kg: 0.25 mg; >20 kg: 0.5 mg.
  • Aripiprazole: up to 10 mg.
  • SSRIs for repetitions/compulsions (off-label in the figure: fluoxetine/citalopram).
  • Stimulants for hyperactivity, e.g. methylphenidate.
Dose principle from Figure 3
Patients with ASD may require much lower or much larger doses than commonly prescribed; the source emphasizes individual titration.
Mood disorders in ASD

The guideline describes increased mood symptoms in high-functioning autistic individuals around adolescence and after environmental changes or negative life events. Social difficulties and repeated experiences of failure may contribute to low self-esteem and depression. In extreme cases, catatonia can occur as an exacerbation of mood symptoms, ranging from marked unresponsiveness to marked agitation.

Objective assessment

  • ASD-Comorbidity for Children (ASD-CC), a 49-item scale for emotional difficulties.
  • Psychopathology in Autism Checklist (PAC), described as helping discriminate autism from psychosis, depression, anxiety and OCD.

Management approaches shown

  • Depression: SSRIs, TCAs, stimulants.
  • Bipolar disorder: lithium, divalproate.
  • CBT particularly for high-functioning individuals with good insight into social impairment/awkwardness.
Feeding disorders

Feeding problems described include poor eating schedules, poor eating skills, poor nutrition, preference for specific foods and weight gain. The paper also notes phenomenological overlap between ASD and anorexia nervosa in girls as an area for further study.

Assess

  • Feeding habits/preferences at baseline
  • Nutritional status
  • Food allergies
  • Sensory issues related to mastication/swallowing, assessed by OT and speech-language pathologist
  • Mealtime behaviour

Intervene

  • Behaviour therapy
  • Sensory integration
  • Speech/language therapy for oromotor issues
Sleep disorders

Sleep problems include initial, middle or terminal insomnia and sleep-wake cycle abnormalities. The paper links circadian impairment with melatonin functioning and states that melatonin has been effective in numerous studies.

Source dosing statement
The paper gives a melatonin dose requirement of 1–10 mg at bedtime and notes very few mild/transient side effects and low seizure-precipitation risk. It also states that risperidone, clonazepam and clonidine may benefit sleep disorders in individuals with ASD.
Personality disorders & schizophrenia

Personality disorders: DSM-5 makes diagnosis possible in adolescence, facilitating earlier recognition/intervention. The paper says personality disorders may be comorbid with ASD but the relationship is complex; psychotherapies and medication as necessary are mentioned.

Schizophrenia: Both ASD and schizophrenia are discussed as neurodevelopmental disorders with overlapping phenotypic patterns. The source states reported co-occurrence rates are highly variable and calls for better diagnostic tools and intervention strategies.

Gender dysphoria and ASD

The article reports that studies had found greater reporting of gender-dysphoric symptoms among individuals with ASD than in typically developing individuals. It then discusses possible hormonal/developmental hypotheses involving fetal testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone.

Historical hypothesis — not established causation
The hormonal explanations in this 2019 paper are presented here as the authors’ discussion of potential mechanisms and research directions, not as established causal explanations.

14 · Certification, insurance, travel & reproductive counselling

Certification and insurance

  • Disability assessment should follow clinical diagnosis and use the INCLEN tools and India Scale for Assessment of Autism (ISAA), according to the guideline.
  • The paper names Niramaya (insurance), Aspiration (early intervention) and GyanPrabha (scholarship) as schemes available to autistic individuals.
  • The article describes an Autism Certification Medical Board comprising a pediatrician/pediatric neurologist, clinical or rehabilitation psychologist, and psychiatrist.
  • It states that certificates should be valid for 5 years for individuals under 18 with temporary disability and permanently valid for acquired permanent disability.
Source-era administrative point
Certification rules and government schemes can change. The above reproduces the 2019 paper’s description and should not be treated as current legal/administrative advice without checking the applicable present rules.

Travel

  • Certification may be required for concessions on buses, trains and air travel.
  • Because change may be poorly tolerated, trips should be planned thoroughly and the child repeatedly prepared in advance.
  • Consider food and clothing specifics.
  • Pictures or a scrapbook can help prepare the child.
  • The source mentions that for long journeys or altitude changes, a small dose of an anxiolytic or antihistaminic such as promethazine may sometimes be prescribed.

Planning another child

The paper states that robust genetic evidence was lacking, while cited studies estimated recurrence risk at 3%–10% and, more recently, up to 20% for a second child when the first child has ASD. It recommends infant screening and genetic counselling for such families.

15 · Care after parents & lifespan support

The guideline recognizes the long-term concern of parents about what will happen to an autistic child in adulthood and after the parents’ death. It lists several newer concepts intended to address this.

Lifespan carers

People well versed in ASD across the lifespan who can support individuals at crucial points and across levels of functioning.

Positive behaviour support

Based on applied behaviour analysis, inclusion movement and personalized values.

Multidisciplinary/community network

Professionals, family members, friends, neighbours, social workers and volunteers can contribute to ongoing care/support.

Parent support groups

The paper names groups such as Forum for Autism and Action for Autism and notes their role in reducing caregiver distress/burnout through support networks.

Residential / assisted living
The article notes the emergence of assisted-living centres for autistic individuals in places including Gurgaon, Hyderabad and Bengaluru as a source of hope for families concerned about long-term care after parents are gone.

The authors conclude that continued efforts are needed in a multicultural society such as India to provide long-term care for individuals with ASD.

16 · Non-evidence-based / non-recommended treatments

Do not mistake claims of cure for evidence
The guideline cites the FDA position that there is no “cure” for autism and states that therapies claiming to be curative should be treated as false/misleading. ASD is described as lifelong and responsive to intervention over time rather than disappearing.

Parent counselling points

  1. Be suspicious of products claiming to treat a wide range of diseases.
  2. Do not substitute personal testimonials for scientific evidence.
  3. Be suspicious of “quick fixes”.
  4. “Miracle cures” claiming breakthroughs or secret ingredients are usually a hoax.

  4. Lacks fear of danger
  5. Excited/agitated without apparent reason

Domain 3 — Speech-language communication

  1. Acquired speech and lost it
  2. Difficulty using non-verbal language/gestures
  3. Stereotyped/repetitive language
  4. Echolalic speech
  5. Infantile squeals/unusual noises
  6. Unable to initiate or sustain conversation
  7. Jargon or meaningless speech
  8. Pronoun reversal
  9. Unable to grasp pragmatics of communication

Domain 4 — Behavioural patterns

  1. Stereotyped/repetitive motor mechanisms
  2. No attachment to inanimate objects
  3. Hyperactivity/restlessness
  4. Aggressive behaviour
  5. Temper tantrums
  6. Self-injurious acts
  7. Insistence on sameness

Domain 5 — Sensory aspects

  1. Unusually sensitive to stimuli
  2. Stares into space for a long time
  3. Difficulty tracking objects
  4. Unusual vision
  5. Insensitive to pain
  6. Responds unusually to objects/people by smiling, touching or tasting

Domain 6 — Cognitive component

  1. Inconsistent attention/concentration
  2. Delay in responding
  3. Unusual memory
  4. Savant ability

One-page takeaway
Think ASD care as a longitudinal developmental programme: detect early → confirm clinically with multidisciplinary assessment → characterize cognition/language/comorbidity and functional needs → start individualized developmental/behavioural/educational/communication interventions → treat associated symptoms when they impair functioning → monitor adverse effects → support family and school/community participation → plan for adolescence, adulthood and lifespan care.

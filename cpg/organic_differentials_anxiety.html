Psychiatry • Differential Diagnosis

Medical / Organic Differentials of Anxiety

A structured clinical approach to recognizing medical causes and mimics of anxiety before labeling symptoms as a primary anxiety disorder.

System-based differential Red-flag driven Targeted investigations Psychiatry-focused
Core clinical principle

Anxiety is a non-specific syndrome and can represent a final common pathway of endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, metabolic, toxic, medication-related and other medical disorders.

The practical objective is not to investigate every patient indiscriminately, but to identify clinical cues that increase the pre-test probability of an organic cause and then investigate accordingly.

01 • Learning objectives

What should you be able to do?

01

Recognize

Identify clinical cues that raise suspicion for an organic or medical cause of anxiety symptoms.

02

Differentiate

Distinguish primary anxiety disorders from anxiety secondary to other medical conditions.

03

Investigate

Select investigations according to history, age of onset, physical findings and system-specific clinical cues.

02 • Clinical context

Why do medical differentials matter?

Anxiety is non-specific

Anxiety symptoms have one of the longest differential lists in psychiatry because autonomic arousal, subjective fear, restlessness, insomnia and impaired concentration occur across multiple medical conditions.

Medical disease may masquerade as anxiety

Endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory and neurological disorders can produce panic-like episodes, palpitations, dyspnoea, fear of impending death and agitation.

Late-onset symptoms matter

New anxiety symptoms appearing later in life, particularly without a previous anxiety phenotype, should increase consideration of a medical cause.

Treatment implications

Treating the underlying medical disorder may substantially reduce or resolve the anxiety symptoms. Psychotropic treatment alone may mask progression of the underlying condition.

03 • Clinical red flags

When should you suspect organic anxiety?

High-yield principle

Late onset + atypical phenomenology + abnormal examination should trigger reconsideration of a primary anxiety diagnosis.

Age of onset

First presentation after approximately 35–40 years is atypical for primary anxiety and should prompt consideration of medical causes.

No previous anxiety phenotype

No personal or family history of anxiety, phobias, childhood separation anxiety or similar symptoms.

No avoidance pattern

Absence of classical avoidance behaviour or anticipatory anxiety characteristic of panic/agoraphobia.

No clear psychosocial trigger

Symptoms have no clear temporal relationship with stressors or psychological precipitants.

Altered consciousness

Fluctuating awareness, staring, unresponsiveness, confusion or post-event amnesia.

Focal neurological signs

Weakness, sensory deficits, visual symptoms, ataxia, bladder dysfunction or other focal findings.

Abnormal vital signs

Persistent tachycardia, severe hypertension, hypoxaemia, orthostatic abnormalities or fever.

Physical disease clues

Goitre, tremor, weight change, clubbing, cyanosis, oedema, abnormal cardiac examination or other systemic findings.

04 • System-based differential

Endocrine & metabolic causes

The endocrine system contains several important and potentially reversible mimics of anxiety.

01

Hyperthyroidism / Thyrotoxicosis

Graves disease or toxic thyroid disease
Key message

Anxiety associated with thyrotoxicosis usually occurs with prominent autonomic and metabolic signs.

Psychiatric manifestations

  • Nervousness
  • Inner tension
  • Irritability
  • Emotional lability
  • Restlessness
  • Hypervigilance
  • Impaired concentration
  • Insomnia
  • Fatigue
  • Panic-like episodes in severe cases
  • Hypomania or psychosis in severe cases

Autonomic / metabolic clues

  • Tachycardia
  • Palpitations
  • Fine tremor
  • Sweating
  • Heat intolerance
  • Weight loss despite increased appetite
  • Increased bowel frequency

Thyroid / eye signs

  • Diffuse goitre
  • Thyroid bruit
  • Lid lag
  • Exophthalmos
  • Proximal muscle weakness
  • Menstrual irregularities

When to investigate

  • New-onset or worsening anxiety
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Heat intolerance
  • Tremor
  • Persistent tachycardia
  • No clear psychological trigger
  • Poor or paradoxical response to psychotropics
Investigations

TSH + free T4 / free T3 are the initial thyroid function assessment. TRAb/TSI can be considered when Graves disease is suspected.

Consider ECG when significant tachycardia or palpitations are present.

Practical rule

Anxiety + weight loss + heat intolerance + tremor + persistent tachycardia → evaluate thyroid function.

02

Pheochromocytoma

Episodic catecholamine excess mimicking panic attacks
Classic clinical triad
Headache Severe / episodic
Palpitations Often with tachycardia
Sweating Often profuse

Psychiatric presentation

  • Intense subjective anxiety
  • Sense of impending doom
  • Panic-like episodes
  • Restlessness
  • Irritability
  • Tremulousness

Red flags

  • Paroxysmal symptoms
  • Marked hypertension during attacks
  • Severe headache
  • Drenching sweats
  • Pallor
  • Episodes triggered by specific drugs/procedures
Diagnostic approach

First-line biochemical testing: plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines.

If biochemical testing is positive, proceed to localisation with CT/MRI of the abdomen and pelvis.

Practical rule

A panic-like episode accompanied by severe hypertension + headache + drenching sweating should prompt evaluation for pheochromocytoma before labeling the episode as primary panic.

03

Hypoglycaemia / Insulinoma

Autonomic and neuroglycopenic symptoms mimicking panic
Think of Whipple's triad
  1. Symptoms consistent with hypoglycaemia.
  2. Documented low plasma glucose during symptoms.
  3. Resolution of symptoms after glucose correction.

Autonomic symptoms

  • Palpitations
  • Tremor
  • Sweating
  • Pallor
  • Hunger
  • Anxiety / sense of doom

Neuroglycopenic symptoms

  • Dizziness
  • Blurred / double vision
  • Headache
  • Weakness
  • Slurred speech
  • Ataxia
  • Confusion
  • Seizures
  • Coma in severe cases
Timing is diagnostically important

Episodes may occur during fasting, early morning, after prolonged exercise, following a missed meal, or in some cases after a carbohydrate-heavy meal.

Investigations during symptoms
  • Plasma glucose
  • Insulin
  • C-peptide
  • Proinsulin
  • β-hydroxybutyrate
  • Supervised prolonged fast when indicated
  • CT/MRI after biochemical confirmation when insulinoma is suspected
Feature Primary panic/anxiety Hypoglycaemia
Timing Often variable / psychological context Fasting, early morning, exercise or meal-related
Hunger Usually not prominent Often prominent
Neuroglycopenia Unusual Confusion, diplopia, slurred speech, ataxia, seizures
Skin Usually no specific change Pallor, cold clammy skin
Response to carbohydrate Little / inconsistent Symptoms improve after glucose correction
Blood glucose Normal Low during symptoms
04

Primary Hyperparathyroidism

Hypercalcaemia-associated neuropsychiatric syndrome
Clinical concept

Hypercalcaemia can produce anxiety, irritability, fatigue, cognitive complaints, sleep disturbance and mood symptoms.

Psychiatric / cognitive

  • Anxiety
  • Nervousness
  • Irritability
  • Depression
  • Apathy
  • Anhedonia
  • Emotional lability
  • Poor concentration
  • Memory complaints
  • Fatigue

Systemic clues

  • Polyuria
  • Polydipsia
  • Nephrolithiasis
  • Constipation
  • Nausea
  • Abdominal pain
  • Bone pain
  • Fractures / osteoporosis
  • Proximal muscle weakness
Classic mnemonic

Stones • Bones • Groans • Psychiatric overtones

Investigations
  • Total or ionized serum calcium
  • PTH
  • Phosphate
  • 24-hour urinary calcium
  • Vitamin D

Localisation studies such as neck ultrasound or sestamibi scanning can follow biochemical confirmation when indicated.

05 • System-based differential

Cardiovascular mimics

Palpitations, chest discomfort, dyspnoea and presyncope are common presentations shared by anxiety and cardiovascular disease.

Cardiac safety rule

New palpitations with syncope / presyncope, irregular pulse, exertional symptoms or significant cardiovascular risk factors should not be assumed to be anxiety.

01

Arrhythmias

SVT, atrial fibrillation and premature beats

SVT

  • Sudden-onset racing heart
  • Palpitations
  • Chest discomfort
  • Dyspnoea
  • Light-headedness
  • Anxiety / panic-like symptoms

Atrial fibrillation

  • Irregularly irregular pulse
  • Palpitations
  • Fatigue
  • Dyspnoea
  • Reduced exercise tolerance

PACs / PVCs

  • Skipped beat sensation
  • Extra beat sensation
  • Brief pauses
  • Fear that the heart is stopping
  • Sense of doom

History to obtain

  • Exact onset
  • Duration
  • Exertional relationship
  • Caffeine / alcohol
  • Postural relationship
  • Syncope / presyncope
  • Family history
Investigations
  • 12-lead ECG
  • Electrolytes
  • TSH
  • Haemoglobin
  • Ambulatory ECG / Holter when episodes are intermittent
  • Echocardiography when structural disease is suspected
Feature Panic attack Arrhythmia
Rhythm Usually sinus tachycardia Depends on arrhythmia
Onset Often builds over minutes May be abrupt
Triggers Stress, crowds, phobic cues Exertion, caffeine, alcohol, posture or none
Vagal manoeuvres Usually minimal effect May terminate some SVTs
Documentation ECG generally normal between attacks ECG/ambulatory monitoring may capture rhythm abnormality
02

Mitral Valve Prolapse

Palpitations, atypical chest pain and anxiety symptoms

Clinical features

  • Atypical chest pain
  • Palpitations
  • Dyspnoea on exertion
  • Fatigue
  • Anxiety / panic symptoms in some patients

Examination

  • Mid-systolic click
  • Possible late systolic murmur
  • Dynamic manoeuvres can alter the murmur
Important nuance

The relationship between mitral valve prolapse and panic disorder has been inconsistent across studies. It should therefore be approached as a clinical differential rather than assumed to explain anxiety symptoms.

03

Angina / Acute Coronary Syndrome

Anxiety or sense of doom can accompany cardiac ischaemia
Red flags for ACS
  • Age >40–45 years
  • Smoking
  • Diabetes
  • Hypertension
  • Dyslipidaemia
  • Family history
  • Exertional chest discomfort
  • Radiation to arm, jaw or back
  • Profuse sweating
  • Nausea
  • Syncope
Initial approach
  • Immediate ECG when ACS is suspected
  • Serial cardiac troponins
  • Risk stratification as appropriate
  • Early cardiology involvement for high-risk presentations
Feature Panic attack ACS
Chest sensation Often sharp/stabbing or variable Pressure, heaviness, tightness or discomfort
Duration Often peaks within minutes and resolves Persistent or prolonged discomfort may occur
Relation to exertion Often at rest or during emotional distress Classically exertional, although atypical patterns occur
Associated symptoms Tremor, paraesthesia, derealisation Sweating, nausea, hypotension, syncope
ECG / biomarkers Usually normal May show ischaemic changes and/or elevated troponin
04

Heart Failure

Dyspnoea and fear of suffocation can resemble panic

Key symptoms

  • Exertional dyspnoea
  • Fatigue
  • Reduced exercise tolerance
  • Orthopnoea
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea
  • Fear of suffocation

Physical clues

  • Peripheral oedema
  • Raised JVP
  • Crackles
  • S3
  • Hepatomegaly
Investigations

BNP/NT-proBNP, chest imaging and echocardiography can support evaluation when heart failure is suspected.

Practical rule

Dyspnoea + orthopnoea / PND ± oedema should prompt evaluation for heart failure rather than assuming panic disorder.

05

Severe Hypertension

Anxiety may accompany markedly elevated blood pressure
Clinical distinction

Anxiety can transiently increase blood pressure, whereas sustained severe elevation requires assessment for hypertensive disease and, importantly, target-organ damage.

Pattern Likely consideration Clinical approach
Mild/moderate transient elevation during anxiety Autonomic response to anxiety Repeat measurement and assess context
Sustained marked elevation Hypertension / secondary cause Assess history, medications, renal/endocrine causes
Severe BP elevation + neurological/cardiac/renal symptoms Possible hypertensive emergency Urgent assessment for target-organ injury
06 • System-based differential

Respiratory mimics

Dyspnoea, chest tightness and the sensation of being unable to breathe are central symptoms of panic—but also major manifestations of respiratory disease.

01

Asthma / COPD Exacerbation

Airflow limitation with secondary anxiety and fear of suffocation

Asthma

  • Episodic dyspnoea
  • Chest tightness
  • Wheeze
  • Cough
  • Nocturnal / early morning symptoms
  • Allergen or exercise triggers
  • Atopy, eczema or allergic rhinitis history

COPD

  • Chronic progressive dyspnoea
  • Chronic cough
  • Sputum production
  • Exacerbations
  • Smoking history
  • Biomass exposure
Practical rule

Dyspnoea with wheeze or cough should prompt consideration of asthma/COPD rather than being automatically labelled as panic.

02

Pulmonary Embolism

Potentially life-threatening cause of sudden dyspnoea and anxiety
Think PE when anxiety accompanies:
  • Sudden unexplained dyspnoea
  • Tachycardia
  • Tachypnoea
  • Pleuritic chest pain
  • Haemoptysis
  • Syncope
  • Hypoxaemia
  • Sense of impending doom

Risk factors

  • Recent surgery
  • Recent trauma
  • Prolonged immobilisation
  • Pregnancy
  • Oestrogen-containing contraception
  • Previous DVT / PE
  • Thrombophilia

Important examination clues

  • Low SpO₂
  • Tachycardia
  • Tachypnoea
  • Leg swelling
  • Pleuritic pain
Feature Panic attack Pulmonary embolism
Dyspnoea Subjective sensation prominent Objective respiratory abnormality may be present
SpO₂ Usually normal May be reduced
Chest pain Variable / atypical Pleuritic and worse with inspiration
Risk factors Psychological triggers VTE risk factors
Leg findings Usually absent Possible unilateral swelling/pain
Safety rule

New dyspnoea + tachycardia ± pleuritic chest pain or VTE risk factors warrants assessment for pulmonary embolism.

03

Hyperventilation Syndrome

Ventilation exceeding metabolic demand → hypocapnia

Symptoms

  • Dyspnoea
  • Chest tightness
  • Frequent yawning
  • Dizziness
  • Light-headedness
  • Blurred vision
  • Perioral/digital paraesthesia
  • Palpitations
  • Sense of suffocation

Clinical examination

  • Carpopedal spasm may occur
  • Chvostek/Trousseau phenomena may be demonstrable
  • Symptoms may occur during an episode
Possible investigations
  • ABG/VBG where clinically appropriate
  • Low PCO₂
  • Respiratory alkalosis
  • Capnography may show reduced end-tidal CO₂ during symptoms
Clinical cue

Recurrent panic-like episodes with prominent paraesthesia, spasms and hypocapnia should raise consideration of hyperventilation syndrome.

04

Obstructive Sleep Apnoea

Nocturnal airway obstruction, hypoxia and sympathetic activation
Core concept

Repeated upper-airway collapse during sleep causes intermittent hypoxia, arousals and sympathetic activation. Nocturnal choking can be misinterpreted as panic.

Sleep symptoms

  • Loud snoring
  • Witnessed apnoeas
  • Nocturnal choking/gasping
  • Fragmented sleep

Daytime symptoms

  • Excessive daytime sleepiness
  • Unrefreshing sleep
  • Morning headache
  • Nocturia
  • Dry mouth
  • Irritability
  • Cognitive complaints

Associated conditions

  • Obesity
  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Depression
Assessment

Screening tools may identify risk. Definitive evaluation is with sleep study / polysomnography.

The source material notes an AHI threshold of ≥5 with symptoms or ≥15 irrespective of symptoms.

Feature Primary anxiety OSA-related symptoms/anxiety
Sleep Difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep Snoring, witnessed apnoea, choking awakenings
Daytime state Worry, tension, restlessness Sleepiness, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache
Pattern of anxiety Persistent worry ± panic Often linked to poor sleep/nocturnal symptoms
Physical clues Usually absent Obesity, crowded oropharynx, hypertension may occur
Practical rule

Anxiety + excessive daytime sleepiness + loud snoring + witnessed apnoea → screen for OSA.

05

Chronic Hypoxia / Advanced Lung Disease

COPD, ILD and other chronic respiratory conditions

Possible manifestations

  • Persistent dyspnoea
  • Fatigue
  • Exercise intolerance
  • Cognitive slowing
  • Irritability
  • Sleep disturbance
  • Secondary polycythaemia
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Cor pulmonale

Physical clues

  • Clubbing
  • Cyanosis
  • Crackles
  • Wheeze
  • Signs of right-heart failure
  • Abnormal lung imaging
Practical rule

Dyspnoea with resting/exertional hypoxaemia or abnormal lung imaging should prompt evaluation for underlying lung disease.

07 • System-based differential

Neurological causes of anxiety

Neurological disorders can present with anxiety, panic-like episodes, mood lability or behavioural change, sometimes before the classical neurological syndrome becomes obvious.

Neurological red flag

Anxiety accompanied by altered awareness, focal neurological deficits, stereotyped brief episodes, seizures, progressive cognitive change, movement disorder or visual/sensory abnormalities should prompt neurological evaluation.

01

Temporal Lobe Epilepsy

Ictal fear / panic as a classic mimic of panic disorder
Why can TLE mimic anxiety?

Mesial temporal structures, including the amygdala, are involved in generation of intense fear and autonomic experiences.

High-yield clinical cues

  • Highly stereotyped episodes
  • Very brief duration
  • No obvious psychological trigger
  • Nocturnal episodes
  • Déjà vu / jamais vu
  • Rising epigastric sensation
  • Olfactory/gustatory aura

Altered awareness / automatisms

  • Staring
  • Unresponsiveness
  • Lip smacking
  • Chewing
  • Fumbling/picking
  • Post-event confusion
  • Amnesia
  • Post-event fatigue/sleep
Major differentiator: duration

A stereotyped episode lasting seconds to less than approximately 2 minutes with aura or impaired awareness should raise suspicion for focal seizures rather than primary panic.

Investigations
  • EEG
  • Sleep-deprived EEG where appropriate
  • Video-EEG for definitive event capture
  • MRI brain with attention to mesial temporal structures
  • Neurology referral
Feature Temporal lobe seizure Primary panic attack
Duration Often seconds to <2 minutes Typically peaks over minutes
Consciousness May be impaired Usually preserved
Aura Déjà vu, rising epigastric, olfactory/gustatory Absent
Automatisms May occur Not typical
Post-event state Confusion/amnesia/fatigue possible Residual anxiety but no typical postictal state
Trigger May occur out of context or during sleep May be situational/anticipatory
02

Migraine / Vestibular Migraine

Vertigo, dizziness and anxiety may coexist

Migraine features

  • Unilateral throbbing headache
  • Photophobia
  • Phonophobia
  • Visual aura
  • Motion sensitivity
  • Specific triggers

Vestibular migraine

  • Recurrent spontaneous vertigo
  • Dizziness lasting minutes to hours
  • History of migraine may or may not include headache during attack
  • Motion sensitivity
  • Visual triggers
  • Fear of falling / avoidance behaviour
Feature Panic disorder Vestibular migraine
Dizziness Light-headedness / floating sensation True vertigo / spinning
Headache May be absent or tension-type May be unilateral/throbbing
Associated symptoms Fear, paraesthesia, autonomic symptoms Photophobia, phonophobia, motion sensitivity
Duration Peak within minutes; generally shorter Minutes to hours; migraine headache may last longer
Triggers Stress, crowded places, phobic cues Motion, visual patterns, foods, sleep changes
Practical rule

Recurrent true vertigo + migraine history + anxiety → consider vestibular migraine.

03

Multiple Sclerosis

Demyelinating disease with neuropsychiatric manifestations

Psychiatric manifestations

  • Anxiety
  • Depression
  • Irritability
  • Emotional lability
  • Apathy
  • Disinhibition
  • Rarely psychosis

Neurological clues

  • Optic neuritis
  • Diplopia
  • Sensory symptoms
  • Motor weakness
  • Ataxia
  • Gait disturbance
  • Bladder dysfunction
  • Fatigue
Diagnostic approach
  • MRI brain and spine when clinically indicated
  • CSF assessment including oligoclonal bands where appropriate
  • Evoked potentials in selected cases
  • Neurology assessment
Practical rule

Anxiety + focal neurological, visual or sensory symptoms warrants evaluation for neurological disease.

04

Wilson Disease

Treatable copper metabolism disorder with psychiatric presentations
Do not miss this diagnosis

A young patient with new psychiatric symptoms accompanied by movement disorder, hepatic disease or characteristic ocular findings should be evaluated for Wilson disease.

Psychiatric manifestations

  • Anxiety
  • Depression
  • Irritability
  • Personality change
  • Psychosis
  • Mania
  • Cognitive impairment
  • Sleep disturbance

Neurological / hepatic clues

  • Tremor
  • Dystonia
  • Parkinsonism
  • Ataxia
  • Dysarthria
  • Dysphagia
  • Gait disturbance
  • Liver disease
Additional clues
  • Young age, particularly psychiatric onset before 40 years
  • Family history
  • Kayser–Fleischer rings
  • Low ceruloplasmin
  • Abnormal hepatic profile
Diagnostic assessment
  • Serum ceruloplasmin
  • 24-hour urinary copper
  • Slit-lamp examination for Kayser–Fleischer rings
  • Hepatic copper assessment when indicated
  • MRI brain for characteristic basal ganglia involvement
  • ATP7B genetic testing in selected cases

Brain tumours can initially present with psychiatric symptoms
Red flags
  • New-onset anxiety or depression

  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Seizures
  • Focal neurological deficits
  • Visual field disturbance
  • Aphasia
  • Progressive cognitive/personality change

TIA / Cerebrovascular Disease

Sudden focal neurological symptoms can trigger acute anxiety
Stroke/TIA rule

A sudden focal neurological deficit with or without anxiety should be treated as a cerebrovascular event until appropriately evaluated.

Possible focal deficits

  • Unilateral weakness
  • Unilateral numbness
  • Facial droop
  • Aphasia / dysphasia
  • Visual field loss
  • Ataxia
  • Vertigo

Vascular risk factors

  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Tobacco use
  • Previous vascular disease
Possible assessment
  • ECG
  • Brain MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging where indicated
  • Vascular imaging
  • Carotid evaluation where appropriate
  • Echocardiography when indicated
  • Neurology/stroke service evaluation

07

Post-Concussive Syndrome

Persistent symptoms after mild traumatic brain injury

Somatic symptoms

  • Headache
  • Dizziness
  • Fatigue
  • Photophobia
  • Phonophobia

Cognitive / emotional

  • Poor concentration
  • Memory complaints
  • Slowed processing
  • Irritability
  • Anxiety
  • Depression
  • Emotional lability
  • Sleep disturbance
Clinical clue

Anxiety emerging after head injury together with headache, dizziness, sensory sensitivity or cognitive complaints should prompt consideration of post-concussive syndrome.

Management principle

Neurodegenerative Disorders

Anxiety can precede or accompany cognitive and motor syndromes
Disorder Psychiatric manifestations Important accompanying clues
Parkinson disease Anxiety, depression, apathy; later psychosis may occur Motor symptoms, dopamine/serotonin-related neuropsychiatric changes

08 • Clinical workflow

Stepwise diagnostic approach

The goal is a focused, probability-based evaluation rather than indiscriminate investigation.

1

Establish the phenomenology

Clarify exactly what the patient means by "anxiety": fear, autonomic arousal, dyspnoea, palpitations, dizziness, choking, impending doom, worry or behavioural change.

2

Define onset and course

Determine age at first onset, duration, episodic versus continuous pattern, progression and whether episodes are stereotyped.

3

Identify triggers and temporal relationships

Ask about psychological stress, exertion, meals, fasting, sleep, posture, caffeine, alcohol, medications, substances and specific environmental triggers.

4

Look for an established anxiety phenotype

Ask about childhood anxiety, separation anxiety, phobias, avoidance, anticipatory anxiety, previous panic attacks and family history.

5

Perform focused physical examination

Include vital signs, orthostatic measurements when indicated, thyroid examination, cardiovascular examination, respiratory examination and a complete neurological examination.

6

Review medications and substances

Consider medication/substance-induced symptoms and recent changes in medications, stimulants, decongestants and other relevant agents.

7

Order basic investigations according to context

The source framework proposes CBC, chemistry panel, electrolytes, glucose, calcium, renal function, liver function, thyroid function and urine drug screening, with pregnancy testing where relevant.

8

Escalate selectively

Add ECG, endocrine tests, imaging, EEG, sleep study, cardiac monitoring or other targeted investigations according to the positive findings and pre-test probability.

9

Treat the identified medical cause

Where a medical cause is identified, management should address the underlying condition rather than treating anxiety in isolation.

10

Reassess residual anxiety

After addressing the medical condition, reassess whether clinically significant anxiety remains and whether it meets criteria for a primary anxiety disorder or requires separate psychiatric management.

09 • Investigations

Basic laboratory framework

Haematology

Metabolic panel

Electrolytes, glucose, calcium and renal function.

LFT

Liver function

Useful where systemic, metabolic, medication-related or hepatic disease is suspected.

TFT

Thyroid function

Particularly important in new-onset anxiety with autonomic or metabolic features.

β-hCG

Pregnancy test

When relevant to the patient's clinical context and medication/investigation decisions.

10 • Investigation strategy

When should testing be escalated?

Clinical suspicion Typical profile Suggested approach
Low Younger patient with clear psychological triggers, established anxiety phenotype, family history and no abnormal physical findings. Focused history and examination with limited/basic investigations according to clinical context.
Moderate Age approximately 35–40+, atypical symptoms, new medication/substance exposure or mild examination abnormalities. Basic laboratory assessment plus targeted testing such as ECG, thyroid studies or other system-specific tests.
High Late-onset symptoms, focal neurological signs, cognitive fluctuation, unexplained weight change, abnormal vital signs or other strong medical clues. Full targeted work-up, including endocrine studies, imaging, EEG, cardiac monitoring or other specialist investigations according to the suspected disorder.
Principle: probability-based testing

Start with history and examination, obtain basic investigations where appropriate, and escalate according to pre-test probability rather than ordering an extensive panel routinely in every patient.

13 • Take-home framework

Before calling it primary anxiety

Ask yourself five questions:
  1. Is the age of onset typical?
  2. Does the patient have an established anxiety phenotype?
  3. Are the episodes stereotyped, unusually brief, nocturnal or associated with altered consciousness?
  4. Are there abnormal vital signs or physical/neurological findings?
  5. Is there a clear medical, substance, medication, metabolic or endocrine clue?

The overarching diagnostic strategy

1

Phenomenology

Define exactly what the patient is experiencing.

2

History

Onset, course, triggers, childhood history, family history, substances and medications.

Examination

Vitals, thyroid, cardiovascular, respiratory and neurological examination.

Basic tests

CBC, metabolic profile, glucose, calcium, renal/liver function, thyroid function and targeted tests.

5

Targeted escalation

ECG, Holter, endocrine tests, imaging, EEG, sleep study or specialist evaluation according to clinical suspicion.

6

Treat and reassess

Treat the underlying medical condition and then reassess residual anxiety before concluding that a primary anxiety disorder is present.

Final take-home message

Anxiety should be viewed as a clinical syndrome, not automatically as a psychiatric diagnosis.

Late onset, atypical features, absence of a previous anxiety phenotype, abnormal physical findings and neurological or autonomic abnormalities are the major signals to broaden the differential.

Start with a focused history and examination, use basic investigations appropriately, and escalate testing according to the clinical probability of a specific medical disorder.

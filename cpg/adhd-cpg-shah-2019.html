1. Scope, definition & overall framework

The guideline describes ADHD as one of the most common childhood psychiatric disorders, affecting around 5%–7% of school-aged children. It is described as a neurodevelopmental disorder with a chronic course that can impair peer/social, academic and occupational functioning across the lifespan and can substantially affect caregivers.

Core symptom domains
Inattention · hyperactivity · impulsivity
Guideline population
Children and adolescents up to 18 years; preschoolers are <6 years; adult ADHD treatment is outside the intended scope.
Diagnostic principle
Diagnosis is clinical and should use prevailing nosological criteria plus developmental history, persistence, duration, cross-setting information and functional impairment.
Management principle
Individualized, needs-based treatment combining environmental/psychosocial interventions and medication when indicated.
Important framingThe authors explicitly state that recommendations must be tailored to individual patients. The guideline should be read with the earlier 2007 Indian Psychiatric Society ADHD treatment guideline.

2. Comprehensive assessment & evaluation

Assessment requires a detailed history, mental state examination and physical examination to establish diagnosis and estimate severity and impact on the child and caregivers. Developmental, psychiatric and medical comorbidity and relevant psychosocial/environmental factors should be assessed.

2.1 Multi-informant, multi-setting assessment

  • Interview parents and the child/adolescent.
  • Obtain information from preschool/daycare/school where possible.
  • Teacher reports may be unstructured observations or structured rating scales.
  • Teacher information should preferably be requested through parents after explaining its purpose and obtaining consent/assent.
  • If school liaison is difficult, use parent/child accounts, school work and report cards.
  • In Indian joint/extended families, grandparents and other significant family members can provide important contextual information.
  • Different informants may perceive/tolerate behaviors differently; their accounts are complementary.
  • Assessment may require multiple visits and should remain an ongoing, multi-informant process.
Clinical anchorMulti-informant interviews across multiple settings are central. A child may look much better in a structured one-to-one clinic interaction than in school or during monotonous tasks.

2.3 Clinical questions to answer

  1. Are symptoms of inattention, overactivity and/or impulsivity present?
  2. What is their onset and course?
  3. Are they developmentally inappropriate?
  4. If global developmental delay exists, are symptoms inappropriate for developmental/mental age?
  5. Are symptoms persistent and present more often than not?
  6. Do they cause impairment in more than one setting?
  7. How severe are symptoms and impairment?
  8. Are developmental, psychiatric or medical problems associated?
  9. What genetic/environmental risk factors are present?
  10. What psychosocial, parenting and parenting-stress factors are relevant?
  11. What strengths/assets and protective environmental factors exist?

3. Clinical manifestations across development

The same core syndrome can look different with age. The guideline emphasizes tracing symptoms back to childhood when evaluating adolescents.

Developmental stageTypical manifestations described in the guideline
PreschoolMarked overactivity (“whirlwind”), does not listen when called, little sense of danger, short play sequences (<3 min), frequently leaves activities incomplete.
Primary schoolDifficulty remaining still/calm where expected; brief activities (<10 min), forgetfulness, disorganization, distractibility; interrupting, acting out of turn, rule breaking, intrusions and accidents.
AdolescenceOveractivity may become inner restlessness/fidgetiness; inattention may present as poor organization, procrastination and missed deadlines; impulsivity may appear as risk-taking and substance use.
Cross-setting requirementSymptoms may occur at home, school/preschool, with family or friends and can vary substantially by context. The guideline requires functional impairment to be assessed across domains.

Factors modifying symptom visibility

ADHD signs may be less evidentInattention is more evident
One-to-one interaction; novel/clinic setting; close supervision; frequent rewards; highly interesting activity; consistent external stimulation such as TV/video games.Lengthy, boring or monotonous tasks; high attentional demands; fatigue; particular times of day; reduced external motivation.

Associated problems — not diagnostic by themselves

Defiant/noncompliant behavior, irritability, mood swings, aggression, temper tantrums, sleep difficulties, clumsiness, mild speech/language delay and substance abuse/high-risk behavior may accompany ADHD and should inform management but should not by themselves be used as evidence for ADHD.

Developmental consequences to assess

  • Cognitive/academic: chronic inattention can produce inadequate learning and impair later learning.
  • Social: poor attention to social cues, impulsive peer interactions and peer rejection can contribute to social immaturity.
  • Self-esteem/identity: repeated rejection and criticism can adversely affect self-esteem despite a seemingly boisterous presentation.

4. Differential diagnosis & comorbidity

4.1 Common comorbidities

Developmental
Intellectual disability, speech/language disorders, learning disorders, motor coordination disorders, ASD, tic disorders.
Psychiatric
ODD, conduct disorder, mood and anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders, substance use disorders.
Medical
Epilepsy, tics, seizures, malnutrition, thyroid abnormalities.

4.2 Differentiation table

ConditionClinical distinction emphasized by guideline
ODD / conduct disorderADHD usually predates defiant symptoms; do not diagnose ADHD when defiance occurs without core ADHD symptoms.
Intellectual disabilityInattention/difficulty following instructions may reflect poor comprehension; ADHD symptoms must be clearly developmentally inappropriate.
Specific learning disorderAcademic difficulties are persistent in SLD; ADHD performance may be strikingly variable. The guideline suggests treating ADHD first and reassessing for SLD if indicated.
ASDIncreased activity may be repetitive/stereotyped; sensory and social-communication problems may mimic ADHD symptoms. Avoid overdiagnosis.
Tic disordersProlonged observation can distinguish complex motor tics from ADHD-related motor activity/fidgetiness.
ManiaRecent onset, definite premorbid change, goal-directed activity, inflated self-esteem/grandiosity.
DepressionRecent onset/premorbid change and loss of interest/pleasure beyond irritability/agitation.
PsychosisRecent onset/premorbid change plus delusions/hallucinations.
AnxietyInattention/concentration problems and hyperarousal accompanied by worry/autonomic symptoms.
OCDRepetitive compulsions are goal-directed/ritualistic and may be accompanied by obsessions.
Diagnostic overshadowing / overdiagnosisDo not attribute developmentally inappropriate hyperactivity/impulsivity automatically to ID. Conversely, do not label ASD-related inattention/hyperactivity as ADHD when symptoms are primarily explained by social-communication or sensory issues. Focused interviewing across settings is essential.

Medical mimics and contributors

Rare ADHD-like presentations can occur with head injury, thyroid disorders and absence seizures; genetic syndromes such as fragile X may be relevant. Medication-related mimics include bronchodilators, thyroxine, neuroleptics causing akathisia and isoniazid. Epilepsy may be comorbid, particularly with other neurodevelopmental disorders.

5. Rating scales, psychological testing & investigations

Key ruleRating scales assist diagnosis and monitoring; they are not necessary and cannot replace a good clinical assessment.
Tool / approachRole in guideline
Conners parent/teacher scalesStructured information from different informants; treatment monitoring.
Vanderbilt parent/teacher scalesStructured symptom information; monitoring.
ADHD Rating Scale-IVStructured assessment and monitoring.
SDQ / Achenbach Child Behavior ChecklistMore generic instruments that may be more accessible.
CGI / CGASBaseline and follow-up assessment of overall clinical status/functioning.

The guideline notes that many ADHD scales are expensive, lack Indian norms and can over-/under-diagnose when ID or ASD is present.

Psychological testing

  • Routine psychological testing is not indicated.
  • IQ testing is appropriate if ID is suspected; severe ADHD symptoms can lower scores, so repeat testing after adequate symptom control may be needed.
  • If SLD is suspected, assessment should preferably occur after adequate ADHD symptom control.
  • Playroom observation can assist an experienced clinician but is neither mandatory nor a replacement for clinical evaluation.
  • Routine neuropsychological/continuous performance testing is not indicated.

Physical investigations

  • No specific investigation is required to diagnose ADHD.
  • CT/MRI/PET/SPECT and EEG should not be performed without a specific clinical indication.
  • Check lead levels when exposure/toxicity is suspected.
  • Check thyroid profile only when clinical features suggest thyroid disease.

6. Formulating the treatment plan

The plan should account for age/developmental stage, symptom severity, domains of impairment, everyday-life impact, developmental/psychiatric/medical comorbidity, psychosocial risk/protective factors, child/family goals, resources and strengths.

PrincipleGuideline emphasis
ObjectivesAlleviate symptoms and improve functioning across domains.
IndividualizationNeeds-based and individualized; may change over time.
ComorbidityAccount for developmental, psychiatric and medical disorders.
ContextConsider psychosocial/environmental risks and protective factors.
PreferencesConsider child/family perceptions, treatment preferences and resources including finances.
Therapeutic alliance firstBuild alliance with both child/adolescent and family, validate concerns, address guilt/blame and misconceptions, discuss realistic goals, and involve the family in informed treatment decisions.

Communicating the diagnosis

  • Explore family beliefs about causation before communicating the diagnosis.
  • The guideline suggests explaining ADHD as a brain/neurodevelopmental disorder with genetic and environmental influences.
  • Address guilt and blame; parental factors can influence manifestation/course but symptoms are not simply the child’s or parents’ “fault.”
  • Communicate comorbid diagnoses because they affect treatment and course.
  • Respect stigma concerns and obtain consent before communicating the diagnosis to school.

Psychoeducation

The guideline suggests that psychoeducation may occur over 2–3 sessions and be tailored to the child/family. It should cover core symptoms, organization/time-management difficulties, etiological factors, misconceptions, treatment options, benefits/risks, comorbidities and long-term course.

7. Psychosocial interventions

7.1 Environmental modifications

Structure
Predictable routines; advance warning for changes; fixed places for belongings.
Time management
Daily/weekly planners, labels, verbal/visual prompts, transition warnings 3–5 min beforehand.
Attention
Minimize distractions, adult supervision, simple prompts, highlighters and task-related stimulation.
Breaks
About 5 min after 15–20 min on-task, or earlier in younger children/fidgetiness.
Energy channeling
Brief physical activity; standing while working if helpful; cycling, running, swimming or football.
School support
Extra time, smaller assignments and workload reduction where feasible, with consent-based school liaison.
Practical study environmentQuiet setting; away from TV/traffic; only relevant books/materials on the desk; minimize toys and screen media; supervisor nearby. The source notes that instrumental music may help some children.

7.2 Parent training

Parent training is skills-based and does not imply faulty parenting. It teaches parents behavioral strategies for difficult/extraordinary situations. Other significant family members may need inclusion for consistency.

  • Individual or group programs.
  • Individual training may be particularly useful with conduct problems, family psychopathology, parental ID/language difficulties or engagement difficulties.
  • Source-described duration: 8–10 weekly individual sessions or 10–20 group sessions.
  • Content: psychoeducation, environmental modification, communication, behavioral management/token economy, preparation for unstructured situations, self-care and school liaison.
3 Ps + 4 Cs from the PGIMER manual
3 Ps: patience, persistence, special play time.
4 Cs: clear/specific/short instructions; consistency between what is said and done across adults/time/settings; “catch them good”; cut down critical comments.

7.3 Behavioral principles

  • Clear rules and consistent implementation.
  • Immediate rewards, followed by delayed rewards contingent on desirable behavior.
  • Proportionate withdrawal of rewards/response cost for undesirable behavior.
  • Consistency across time and between caregivers, including grandparents where relevant.
  • Positive feedback and attention to child/family strengths.

7.4 CBT & cognitive training

For older children/adolescents who continue to have domain-specific impairment despite environmental modifications, parent training and/or medication, CBT may target peer/social functioning, time management, problem-solving, emotional expression, self-control and anger management.

Cognitive training may improve working memory, but the guideline states that it is not yet well established whether this translates into improved academic functioning.

Other measures listed by the guideline

Diet and exercise, exclusion/elimination/few-food diets, omega fatty acids/nutritional supplements and neurofeedback are listed among treatment modalities; their evidence should be interpreted in the context of the guideline’s evidence discussion rather than as equivalent first-line treatments.

8. Pharmacological treatment

Before prescribingTailor treatment to severity, impairment, response to nonpharmacological measures, pharmacokinetic/formulation issues, comorbidity, specific needs and misuse/diversion risk.

8.1 Baseline assessment before medication

  • Review diagnosis, current impairment and need for medication.
  • Review mental health and comorbidity.
  • Weight, height and BMI; plot on age/sex growth charts.
  • Baseline appetite, diet and sleep.
  • Ask about exercise intolerance, excessive exertional dyspnea, syncope/fainting, congenital heart disease/surgery, rapid regular palpitations and cardiac chest pain.
  • Ask specifically about family history of sudden cardiac death before age 40.
  • Measure resting heart rate and blood pressure with age-appropriate cuff.
  • Cardiovascular examination; ECG only when indicated. The source does not recommend routine ECG except when a TCA is being considered.
  • History of seizures and tics.
  • ALT/AST at baseline if atomoxetine is to be prescribed.
  • Assess risk of misuse/diversion.

8.2 Stimulants

The guideline identifies methylphenidate, dexamphetamine and lisdexamfetamine as stimulants with substantial evidence; it states that methylphenidate was the stimulant available in India at the time.

FormulationSource-described durationSource-described dosing framework
Methylphenidate IR1–4 h5 mg once or twice daily starting; maximum 60 mg/day.
Methylphenidate intermediate/extended release6–8 h10 mg morning starting; maximum 60 mg/day.
Methylphenidate OROS~12 h18 mg morning starting; maximum 72 mg/day.

IR may require twice/thrice-daily dosing; sustained/extended and OROS preparations permit morning dosing and may improve adherence. Dose-response varies considerably; titration aims for maximal benefit with minimal/acceptable adverse effects.

8.3 Nonstimulants

Agent/classGuideline content
AtomoxetineSelective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor; usually once daily in morning, sometimes divided. CYP2D6 metabolism; poor metabolizers may experience more adverse effects/drug interactions. The guideline describes stronger efficacy evidence than other nonstimulants.
Clonidineα2-noradrenergic agonist; extended-release formulation was not available in India at source publication, so divided dosing was described.
GuanfacineEvidence noted, but extended-release guanfacine was not available in India according to the source.
TCAsThird-line; generally not recommended because of side-effect profile/cardiac concerns.
Modafinil / bupropion / desipramineEvidence noted; modafinil/bupropion/TCAs described as third-line.
Source-era regulatory noteThe 2019 article states that stimulants were Schedule X drugs in India and describes prescription/record-retention requirements. This is reproduced as historical guideline content and should be checked against current Indian regulations before operational use.

8.4 Initiation principles

  • Start low and monitor improvement and adverse effects after each increment.
  • Explain expected symptom benefits, limitations, adverse effects, monitoring and precautions.
  • Stimulants/atomoxetine may cause appetite suppression and sleep difficulties early in treatment; discuss meals and sleep hygiene.
  • Do not abruptly stop clonidine because of risk of rebound hypertension.
  • Discuss and incorporate patient/family preferences.

9. Monitoring, adequate trials, adherence, drug holidays & discontinuation

Initial monitoring

  • During titration: preferably weekly or every 2 weeks.
  • Assess improvement versus side effects; CGI or structured scale may be used.
  • Teacher ratings, if feasible, every 2–4 weeks.
  • Determine onset and duration of medication effect; afternoon worsening may reflect fatigue or wearing off.
  • Ask both parent and child about adverse effects, sleep and appetite.
  • Measure weight at every titration visit.

Continued monitoring

ParameterSource-described interval
WeightEvery visit during titration; thereafter every 3 months.
HeightEvery 3–6 months.
Heart rate/BPEvery visit.
Liver enzymesPeriodically if atomoxetine is used.
FunctioningAcross settings/domains, with multiple informants.

Adequate medication trial

Methylphenidate
Source: ~6 weeks at an adequate dose with good adherence may constitute an adequate trial.
Atomoxetine
Source: slower response; ~12 weeks at an adequate dose with good adherence may constitute an adequate trial.

Drug holidays

Planned breaks may be used during lower-demand periods. The guideline notes potential reduction of adverse effects and opportunities for appetite/catch-up growth and reassessment of continuing need. Symptoms may or may not worsen. It cautions that drug holidays can also increase nonadherence. The source considers weekend holidays more suitable for stimulants than clonidine because of the latter's rebound-hypertension risk.

Discontinuation

Need for continued medication should be reviewed at least annually. Consider response, functional impact, adverse effects, missed-dose effects, planned dose reductions, periods without treatment, psychosocial interventions and patient/family preferences. Trial reduction/withdrawal may be considered when benefit-risk assessment supports it, preferably during lower-stress periods such as vacations, with continued follow-up.

10. Adverse effects: monitoring & source-described management

Adverse effectCommonly associatedMonitoring / initial response in source
Appetite suppressionMethylphenidate, atomoxetineWeight every titration visit/3-monthly thereafter; distinguish baseline eating problem; take with/after meals; calorie-dense breakfast/late meal; dose reduction/change class if persistent.
Growth retardationMethylphenidate; possibly atomoxetinePlot growth. Consider dose reduction, drug holidays/change class; stop if persistent/severe, with pediatric endocrinology referral when indicated.
Tachycardia/BP riseMethylphenidate, atomoxetineHR/BP every titration visit/3-monthly thereafter; source threshold: sustained HR >120 or BP >95th percentile on two occasions → dose reduction and cardiology referral.
Orthostatic hypotensionClonidineCheck orthostatic BP; general fall prevention; dose reduction/division/change/stop if needed.
InsomniaMethylphenidateAssess sleep onset/duration/quality; sleep hygiene; source advises stimulant before 3 PM; dose reduction/change or low-dose clonidine/melatonin at night may be considered.
SedationClonidineAssess daytime sedation; shift higher dose to night; reduce/stop/change if persistent.
TicsMethylphenidateConsider natural waxing/waning and coincidence; observe where possible; weigh tic vs ADHD impairment; reduce/stop/change to atomoxetine/clonidine if indicated.
Mania/psychosisMethylphenidateMonitor; source recommends stopping medication if these adverse effects emerge.
Prescribing noteThese are the 2019 guideline's monitoring thresholds and management suggestions. Current product labeling, regulatory requirements and contemporary pediatric practice should be checked before applying them clinically.

11. Sleep problems associated with ADHD

  • Assess baseline sleep and bedtime behavior before treatment.
  • Bedtime resistance may reflect difficulties with limit-setting/behavior management.
  • Use sleep hygiene and behavioral interventions: regular bedtime, calming routine, dark/comfortable room, avoidance of loud noise, avoidance of evening naps and avoidance of evening electronic media/screens.
  • The guideline notes that adults should also avoid screen use in the bedroom.
  • Source-described medication options for sleep-onset delay include low-dose clonidine or melatonin: 3 mg in children and 3–6 mg in adolescents.
  • Polysomnography is reserved for specific sleep disorders and is not routine.

12. Evidence summary from the guideline

Population/interventionGuideline's evidence interpretation
Preschoolers — stimulantsSome short-term efficacy, but important concerns about appetite and growth suppression.
>6 years/adolescents — stimulantsSignificant efficacy vs placebo; greater short-term efficacy than nonstimulants, while long-term efficacy evidence was considered inadequate.
AtomoxetineEfficacy evidence stronger than other nonstimulants, but effect sizes lower than stimulants.
ClonidineEfficacy evidence, with smaller effect sizes than atomoxetine.
Parent trainingEvidence of efficacy in preschoolers and in children/adolescents.
Medication vs psychosocialNo robust direct comparison; medication may be more efficacious for core symptoms while psychosocial interventions may have greater effects on associated behavior/functioning.
CombinationNo evidence that two medications are superior to one; more adverse effects make medication combinations undesirable in the guideline. Medication plus psychosocial intervention may provide additional benefit over psychosocial intervention alone.
Clinical interpretation of the 2019 frameworkMedication and psychosocial interventions address partly different outcome domains. The guideline therefore emphasizes individualized combination rather than treating symptom reduction as the only endpoint.

14. Special circumstances & comorbid ADHD

Comorbidity/contextGuideline approach
Tic disorderSame medication classes can be considered, but titrate more slowly and monitor adverse effects closely. Clonidine or atomoxetine may be useful options.
Anxiety disorderAtomoxetine may be a useful option; guideline states there is no good evidence that methylphenidate must be avoided.
OCD / ASDSame medication approach may be used, with slower titration and close attention to tolerability.
ID / ASD with irritability, withdrawal or agitationRevisit ADHD diagnosis; if apparent worsening occurs, stop the medication and consider a nonstimulant.
Psychosis / bipolar disorderTreat psychosis/mood disorder first; after resolution, reassess ADHD diagnosis and then weigh stimulant/nonstimulant treatment and interactions.
DepressionAssess severity, suicidality and contribution to global severity. Severe depression/suicidality → treat depression first. In milder depression where ADHD contributes more to overall severity, ADHD may be treated first.
Substance dependenceTreat ADHD and substance dependence simultaneously; assess misuse/diversion risk; use nonstimulant if risk is high.
ODD / conduct disorderParent training should address behavioral problems as well as ADHD; assess misuse/diversion risk. Source notes low-dose risperidone particularly for aggression and refers to conduct-disorder guidance.
Safety principleIn comorbid psychosis/bipolar disorder, the guideline prioritizes stabilization of the acute disorder before revisiting ADHD treatment. In ASD/ID, unexpected irritability, withdrawal, agitation or apparent worsening should trigger diagnostic and tolerability reassessment.

15. High-yield clinical pearls

1. Clinic observation ≠ absence of ADHD
Children can perform much better during structured one-to-one interaction or interesting tasks.
2. Think developmentally
Symptoms must be inappropriate for developmental/mental age, not merely inconvenient.
3. Cross settings matter
Use multiple informants and multiple environments.
4. Impairment matters
Assess academic, social, family, interpersonal, occupational and legal consequences.
5. Avoid diagnostic overshadowing
ID, ASD, anxiety, depression, mania, psychosis and SLD can mimic ADHD.
6. Scales supplement assessment
They do not replace clinical diagnosis.
7. No routine neuroimaging/EEG
Investigate only when a separate clinical indication exists.
8. Treat the child, not only the score
Target functioning, developmental needs and family goals.
9. Parent training ≠ blaming parents
It is skills training to improve behavioral management and consistency.
10. Monitor growth and cardiovascular parameters
Baseline and longitudinal measurement are integral to medication treatment.
11. Annual medication review
Reassess continuing need, response, adverse effects and function.
12. School liaison needs consent
Collaborate with teachers where possible while respecting child/family consent.
One-line assessment formulation
ADHD diagnosis = developmentally inappropriate persistent symptoms + cross-setting evidence + clinically relevant impairment + exclusion/consideration of mimics/comorbidity + multi-informant formulation.
One-line management formulation
Individualize → psychoeducate → modify environment → parent train → liaise with school → add/titrate medication when indicated → monitor response, growth, cardiovascular parameters, sleep/appetite and adverse effects → reassess annually.

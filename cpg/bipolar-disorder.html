Clinical Practice Guideline

Bipolar Disorder

Detailed, clinically oriented notes based on the Clinical Practice Guidelines for Management of Bipolar Disorder published by the Indian Psychiatric Society in 2017.

Source: Indian Psychiatric Society / Indian Journal of Psychiatry Year: 2017 Population: Primarily adults; special populations discussed separately
Clinical overview

A phase-based approach to bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder is characterized by episodes of depression, hypomania/mania and mixed episodes, with variable interepisodic recovery. The guideline emphasizes that assessment and treatment should be longitudinal: current polarity, previous episodes, treatment response, comorbidity, risk, functioning and caregiver factors all influence treatment selection.

Assess longitudinally Identify current polarity Assess suicide risk Treat comorbidity Protect sleep & rhythms Plan maintenance early
Important source note

This page is a detailed paraphrased summary of the 2017 Indian Psychiatric Society guideline supplied for this project. It intentionally preserves the source's 2017 recommendations rather than silently replacing them with newer guideline positions. Current prescribing information and newer evidence should be checked separately when making clinical decisions.

1. Introduction and Scope

The guideline describes bipolar affective disorder as a serious mental disorder characterized by episodes of depression, hypomania/mania and mixed episodes, with interepisodic recovery in many patients but residual symptoms in some. It highlights substantial educational, occupational, interpersonal and psychosocial consequences, as well as suicide risk, medication adverse effects and psychiatric and physical comorbidity.

The Indian Psychiatric Society had previously published bipolar disorder guidelines in 2005. The 2017 document was intended to update those recommendations in view of developments in the evidence base. The source also emphasizes the practical challenges created by limited resources, limited knowledge and inadequate access to health services in India.

Primary source

Shah N, Grover S, Rao GP. Clinical Practice Guidelines for Management of Bipolar Disorder. Indian Journal of Psychiatry. 2017;59:51–66. DOI: 10.4103/0019-5545.196974.

2. Assessment and Evaluation

Assessment is an ongoing process. The guideline recommends assessing both the patient and caregivers, obtaining history from all available sources and performing a careful mental-status examination. Diagnosis should be based on current diagnostic criteria because a reliable diagnosis improves communication and facilitates evidence-based management.

Longitudinal illness history

  • Number of previous episodes.
  • Type of first lifetime episode.
  • Predominant polarity.
  • Duration and severity of episodes.
  • Degree of interepisodic recovery.
  • Rapid or ultra-rapid cycling features.
  • Seasonal pattern where relevant.

Current episode

  • Current polarity: depression, hypomania/mania or mixed state.
  • Severity and symptom dimensions.
  • Psychotic symptoms.
  • Agitation, aggression and behavioural disturbance.
  • Suicidality and self-harm risk.

Comorbidity

  • Alcohol and other substance-use disorders.
  • Anxiety and other psychiatric disorders.
  • Physical and neurological disorders.
  • Medication-related contributors.
  • Conditions that may become clearer after the acute episode settles.

Function and psychosocial context

  • Occupational and educational functioning.
  • Interpersonal and family functioning.
  • Caregiver burden and distress.
  • Stigma and social resources.
  • Psychosocial stressors and biological-rhythm disruption.

Risk assessment

Suicide risk should be explicitly evaluated. The guideline lists suicidal ideation, intent, plans, access to means, potential lethality, commanding hallucinations, severe anxiety, substance use, previous attempts or non-suicidal self-harm and family history of self-harm or completed suicide among relevant areas of assessment.

Do not omit

Suicide risk is not confined to bipolar depression. The source notes suicidal behaviour during depressive, mixed and manic episodes and even during euthymic periods.

Physical examination and investigations

Assessment domain Source recommendation / consideration
Physical examination Thorough physical examination to identify physical illness and secondary affective syndromes.
Anthropometry Blood pressure and weight; BMI and waist circumference where indicated.
Laboratory Haemogram, blood sugar, lipid profile, liver and renal function tests as appropriate.
Cardiac Electrocardiogram where indicated.
Cognition Detailed cognitive testing when clinically required.
Neuroimaging Consider in atypical presentations, neurological signs, treatment non-response, later age at onset and elderly patients.

Caregiver assessment

The caregiver assessment should cover knowledge about the illness and treatment, attitudes and beliefs, caregiver burden and distress, stigma, marital and social impact and available personal and social resources.

Clinical pearl

Reassess over time. The guideline specifically highlights changing needs such as treatment response, adverse effects, adherence, marriage, pregnancy, disability, access to the treatment team and therapeutic alliance.

3. Clinical Formulation and Treatment Planning

Treatment planning should be collaborative, practical, feasible and flexible. Decisions should cover the treatment setting, pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, safety, caregiver needs and longer term prophylaxis. The plan should be reviewed and modified as clinical needs change.

1
Define the episode Identify current polarity, severity, psychosis, agitation and functional impairment.
2
Assess safety Evaluate suicide, violence, self-neglect, medical instability and need for admission.
3
Review the course Use previous polarity, recurrence, rapid cycling and past response to guide selection.
4
Select treatment Consider mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, ECT and psychosocial interventions according to phase.
5
Monitor Follow response, adverse effects, serum levels where relevant and physical health.
6
Plan maintenance Choose a sustainable long-term regimen and relapse-prevention strategy early.

Factors influencing pharmacological choice

Illness factors

Current polarity, severity, psychotic symptoms, previous episodes, predominant polarity, rapid cycling and treatment resistance.

Patient factors

Previous response, adverse effects, adherence, metabolic profile, comorbidity, preference and preferred route of administration.

Practical factors

Cost, availability, concomitant medication, access to monitoring and the feasibility of the proposed treatment.

4. Treatment Setting and Indications for Inpatient Care

Most patients can be managed in outpatient settings. When inpatient treatment is required, the source recommends involving family caregivers whenever possible and facilitating referral if appropriate inpatient services are not locally available.

Mania / mixed episodes

  • Severe agitation or violence creating risk to others.
  • Medical or psychiatric comorbidity making outpatient care unsafe or ineffective.
  • Failure to respond to combinations of first-line agents.
  • Treatment-refractory mania or mixed episode.

Bipolar depression

  • Suicidal behaviour placing life at risk.
  • Severe agitation or violence.
  • Refusal to eat or severe malnutrition.
  • Inability to care for self requiring constant support.
  • Catatonia.
  • Significant medical/psychiatric comorbidity.
  • Failure to respond to appropriate medication combinations.

5. Pharmacological Treatment

The guideline broadly classifies treatment options into mood stabilizers, antidepressants, antipsychotic medications, somatic treatments such as ECT, adjunctive medications and psychosocial interventions.

Mood stabilizers

Lithium, divalproex/valproate, carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine and other anticonvulsant agents discussed in the source.

Antipsychotics

First- and second-generation antipsychotics, including oral, parenteral and depot/long-acting preparations.

Somatic treatments

ECT, with preliminary discussion of rTMS and tDCS in selected bipolar-depression contexts.

6. Lithium

High-yield

The guideline describes lithium as effective for acute episodes of either polarity, relapse prevention for both polarities and reduction of suicide risk.

Before starting lithium

Clinical review

  • Renal dysfunction.
  • Thyroid dysfunction.
  • Cardiac conduction abnormalities.
  • Relevant dermatological illness.
  • Pregnancy status where relevant.

Baseline investigations

  • Urea and creatinine.
  • Serum electrolytes.
  • Thyroid function.
  • Haemogram.
  • ECG.
  • Fasting blood sugar and lipid profile.
  • Anthropometry.

Lithium education

  • Explain common and serious adverse effects.
  • Emphasize avoidance of dehydration.
  • Discuss relevant dietary considerations.
  • Review medications that can increase lithium concentrations, including NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and diuretics.
  • Explain symptoms of toxicity and the need for prompt medical review.

Serum lithium monitoring

Parameter 2017 source recommendation
Acute episode serum level Usually 0.6–1.0 mEq/L.
Prophylaxis Usually 0.6–0.8 mEq/L; the source also discusses lower levels in selected patients.
After initiation/dose change Check after approximately 5 days once steady state is expected; check earlier if toxicity is suspected.
Sampling Source recommends a sample approximately 12–14 hours after the last dose.
Stable treatment Serum levels approximately every 3–4 months, with more frequent monitoring in higher-risk situations.
Renal monitoring More frequent during the first 6 months; subsequently approximately every 6–12 months in stable patients, according to clinical need.
Thyroid monitoring One or two assessments during the first 6 months, then approximately every 6–12 months in stable patients, or more frequently when indicated.

These intervals and ranges are presented as the 2017 source recommendations. Verify current laboratory-monitoring standards and product information before clinical use.

Clinical situations in which the source describes lithium as particularly useful

The guideline lists classical mania, bipolar depression predominance, episodic illness, a manic-depressive-euthymic course, non-rapid-cycling illness, absence of mixed episodes, later age of onset and family history of bipolar disorder or lithium response among situations in which lithium may be particularly useful.

7. Valproate / Divalproex

The guideline describes valproate as useful for acute mania and mixed episodes and for maintenance prevention of both mania and depression. Evidence for acute bipolar depression was described as less robust than that for lithium.

Before starting

Clinical review

  • Hepatic disease.
  • Haematological or bleeding problems.
  • Pregnancy status where relevant.
  • Metabolic and weight-related risk.

Baseline investigations

  • Haemogram.
  • Liver function tests.
  • Pregnancy testing where relevant.
  • Fasting glucose.
  • Lipid profile.
  • Anthropometry.

Serum level and monitoring

  • Source therapeutic serum range: 50–100 µg/mL.
  • During initial titration, level after approximately 5 days of a stable dose.
  • Immediate-release formulation: source recommends sampling approximately 12 hours after the last dose.
  • Extended-release formulation: source discusses sampling approximately 21–24 hours after the last dose.
  • Long-term haemogram and liver-function monitoring may be performed approximately every 6–12 months according to clinical need.
Important

The source specifically emphasizes hepatic, haematological, bleeding, weight and metabolic considerations before and during valproate treatment.

8. Lamotrigine

The guideline describes lamotrigine as useful for bipolar depression and prevention of depressive relapse. It emphasizes that the major serious adverse effect is severe skin rash, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis.

Rash warning

Risk is greater during the early treatment period and when lamotrigine is used with valproate. The source emphasizes slow titration and immediate clinical attention when a significant rash occurs, particularly when accompanied by systemic symptoms.

The source describes an initial regimen of 25 mg/day for the first 2 weeks, followed by 50 mg/day during weeks 3–4, with subsequent increases according to clinical response.

9. Carbamazepine and Other Anticonvulsants

Carbamazepine is described as effective for acute bipolar mania and prevention of relapse. Before starting it, the clinician should review blood dyscrasias and hepatic dysfunction and educate the patient about haematological, hepatic and skin adverse effects.

Carbamazepine monitoring described by the source

  • Baseline haemogram and liver-function tests.
  • Renal-function assessment; serum electrolytes in elderly patients when indicated.
  • During the first 2 months, haemogram and liver-function tests approximately every 2 weeks.
  • If stable after the first 2 months, approximately every 3 months.
  • Source serum level: 4–12 µg/mL, using the seizure-disorder range because a bipolar-specific therapeutic range was not established.

Evidence for oxcarbazepine was described as limited, while topiramate had limited evidence as an add-on in bipolar depression and gabapentin did not demonstrate useful efficacy for mania in the trials discussed by the guideline.

10. Antipsychotic Medications

The guideline reviews evidence for several first- and second-generation antipsychotics across mania, bipolar depression and maintenance. Treatment choice should be linked to current polarity, severity, psychosis, previous response, adverse effects, metabolic risk, adherence and patient preference.

Acute mania

The source reports evidence for olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, risperidone, paliperidone, ziprasidone and haloperidol, among others.

Bipolar depression

The source describes evidence for quetiapine monotherapy, olanzapine–fluoxetine combination and lurasidone in acute bipolar depression.

Maintenance

Olanzapine and quetiapine are discussed for prevention of both depressive and manic recurrence; long-acting risperidone is also described as beneficial for recurrence prevention.

Combination therapy in mania

Acute mania

The source reports that combining lithium or valproate with an antipsychotic can have greater efficacy and faster onset than monotherapy in acute mania. Combination treatment is therefore considered particularly relevant when mania is severe or markedly disruptive.

11. Antidepressants in Bipolar Disorder

The guideline describes antidepressant use in bipolar depression as a controversial area because of concern about treatment-emergent hypomanic/manic switching.

Core source recommendation

Antidepressants should not be used as monotherapy according to the 2017 guideline. They should be used with a mood stabilizer and/or an atypical antipsychotic when indicated.

  • The source reports benefit from antidepressants when added to mood stabilizer treatment.
  • Bupropion is described as having comparatively lower switch risk among the antidepressants discussed.
  • Antidepressants should be avoided as monotherapy.
  • The source advises against antidepressants in rapid-cycling illness and mixed episodes.
  • Paroxetine is specifically discussed as an agent for which evidence was less supportive.

12. What Counts as an Adequate Trial?

Acute mania

The guideline considers approximately 3–4 weeks the minimum duration for an effective trial.

Bipolar depression

Approximately 6 weeks is described as an adequate trial for bipolar depression.

Apparent non-response should be interpreted in the context of adherence, dose, duration, comorbidity, current polarity and the treatment already being used.

13. Electroconvulsive Therapy

The guideline describes ECT as an evidence-supported treatment for acute mania, mixed episodes and bipolar depression in selected circumstances.

Rapid control / high risk

  • Need for rapid symptom control.
  • Suicidal behaviour placing life at risk.
  • Severe agitation or violence.
  • Refusal to eat threatening life.

Specific clinical situations

  • Catatonia.
  • Severe bipolar depression or mania not responding adequately to medication.
  • Previous good response to ECT.
  • Augmentation of partial response.
  • Treatment-resistant illness.
  • Severe episodes during pregnancy.
Maintenance ECT

The guideline also states that maintenance ECT may be considered in patients who have responded to ECT during an acute episode.

14. Management of Hypomania, Mania and Mixed Episodes

Primary acute goals Reduce aggression, agitation and disruptive behaviour rapidly; ensure safety; reduce symptoms; restore functioning; and establish a plan for longer-term prophylaxis.

If the patient is already receiving treatment

1
Check for antidepressants If the patient has developed hypomanic, manic or mixed symptoms while taking an antidepressant, the source recommends stopping it.
2
Optimize the mood stabilizer Check adherence and optimize the existing mood stabilizer, including serum levels where relevant.
3
Add antipsychotic if required For severe symptoms or inadequate response to optimization, add an antipsychotic as appropriate.

If the patient is not currently receiving treatment

  • Monotherapy with lithium, valproate or an antipsychotic may be considered.
  • Combination treatment with lithium + antipsychotic or valproate + antipsychotic may be considered, particularly when the episode is severe.
  • Short-term benzodiazepines may be used when needed.
  • Depot/long-acting antipsychotic treatment may be considered when oral medication is refused or adherence is problematic.
  • ECT may be considered according to clinical indication.

Failure of an adequate first-line trial

Change strategy

  • Change lithium to valproate or vice versa.
  • Consider carbamazepine.
  • Change the antipsychotic if already used.

Augment / combine

  • Add an antipsychotic if not already used.
  • Change between lithium/antipsychotic and valproate/antipsychotic combinations.
  • Use short-term benzodiazepines where clinically indicated.
  • Consider ECT if not previously considered.
Treatment-refractory mania

The source discusses clozapine as an option in treatment-refractory mania.

15. Management of Bipolar Depression

Primary goals Achieve euthymia and restoration of functioning while avoiding a switch into hypomania or mania.

First-line options described in the source

Mood stabilizer-based

  • Lithium.
  • Lamotrigine.
  • Valproate in selected strategies described by the source.

Antipsychotic-based

  • Quetiapine.
  • Olanzapine–fluoxetine combination.
  • Other combinations according to prior treatment and clinical context.

Breakthrough bipolar depression

The first step is to check adherence and ensure that the existing treatment is being taken adequately. If adherence is not the problem, the guideline recommends optimizing the existing mood stabilizer.

If symptoms persist

  • Add bupropion or an SSRI when an antidepressant is appropriate, with concurrent mood-stabilizing/antipsychotic protection.
  • Add or change to lithium, lamotrigine or quetiapine where appropriate.
  • Consider combinations involving mood stabilizers and antipsychotics.
  • Consider psychosocial treatment when depression is mild to moderate.
  • Consider ECT when indicated.
  • The source also discusses preliminary evidence for rTMS and tDCS.
Avoid antidepressant monotherapy

The guideline specifically advises against using antidepressants without a concurrent mood stabilizer or atypical antipsychotic.

16. Psychosocial Management

Psychosocial treatment is described as an important adjunct to pharmacotherapy, particularly during bipolar depression and maintenance. The source associates appropriate psychosocial interventions with reduced relapse, better functioning and improved treatment adherence.

Psychoeducation

Education about illness, treatment, adverse effects, adherence, early warning signs, relapse and lifestyle.

IPSRT

Regularize sleep/wake and daily social rhythms while addressing interpersonal problems and role transitions.

CBT

Develop cognitive and behavioural coping skills, improve adherence and monitor symptoms to reduce relapse risk.

Family-focused intervention

Improve communication, problem solving, treatment adherence, stress management and recognition of early relapse.

Psychoeducation: essential content

  • Assess existing knowledge of the patient and caregivers.
  • Explain the diagnosis and symptoms of depression, mania, hypomania and mixed episodes.
  • Discuss treatment options, efficacy, adverse effects and expected duration.
  • Explain the importance of medication adherence.
  • Teach early warning signs of a new episode.
  • Discuss stress management and regular habits.
  • Address substance use, marriage and other psychosocial issues.
  • Improve communication and problem solving.
  • Address stigma, disability and available benefits/support.
  • Provide lifestyle and dietary advice.

Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy

IPSRT is based on the concept that disruption of social and circadian rhythms can precipitate mood episodes. The guideline emphasizes establishing regular daily routines, stabilizing sleep/wake cycles, identifying interpersonal problems and teaching strategies to minimize their impact.

1
Understand the pattern Relate previous episodes to interpersonal problems and changes in social routine.
2
Stabilize rhythms Establish regular daily routines and address problematic communication patterns.
3
Maintain gains Continue use of learned skills during continuation and maintenance treatment.

Family-focused intervention

The guideline considers family involvement particularly important in the Indian setting. Family-focused treatment addresses psychoeducation, communication skills, acceptance of vulnerability to future episodes, stress recognition, problem solving and establishment of a functional relationship.

Family principle

Family involvement can be protective, but high expressed emotion, persistent criticism, poor supervision or over-involvement may contribute to relapse and poor outcomes.

17. Treatment Adherence

The guideline reports substantial non-adherence/dropout in bipolar disorder and recommends active measures to improve treatment adherence.

Simplify

Explain when and how often medicines should be taken and, where appropriate, prefer simpler once-daily regimens.

Educate

Explain expected benefits, adverse effects and what to do if adverse effects occur.

Review

Ask about adherence at follow-up and advise patients to consult before stopping medication independently.

Practical adherence checklist from the source

  • Explain when and how often to take medicines.
  • Prefer once-daily dosing where feasible.
  • Minimize unnecessary numbers of tablets and medications.
  • Ask about preferred formulation.
  • Check the entire prescription for duplication.
  • Explain that benefits may take days to weeks to appear.
  • Explain why treatment may need to continue after the patient feels better.
  • Discuss adverse effects and what to do if they occur.
  • Encourage reporting of adverse effects.
  • Advise against stopping medication without consulting the psychiatrist.

18. Lifestyle, Diet and Rehabilitation

Patients with bipolar disorder have increased cardiovascular and metabolic vulnerability, including medication-related metabolic effects. The guideline recommends physical exercise, dietary modification and abstinence from smoking, alcohol and other substances.

Rehabilitation programmes should be culturally appropriate and adapted to the needs of the patient and family.

19. Rapid Cycling Affective Disorder

Definition in the 2017 source

Rapid cycling is defined as four or more episodes of depression, mania, hypomania or mixed episodes in one year, with episodes separated by at least 2 months of partial/full remission or by a switch to the opposite polarity.

Risk factors described in the guideline

  • Hypothyroidism.
  • Long-term/aggressive antidepressant use.
  • Comorbid substance use.
  • Minor tranquilizer, stimulant or caffeine abuse.
  • Cyclothymic or hyperthymic temperament.
  • Female sex and menopause.
  • Temporal-lobe dysrhythmias.
  • Multiple episodes/stressors and possible kindling effects.
  • Depression as the onset polarity.
  • Frequent or predominantly depressive episodes.
  • Possible biological-rhythm disturbances.

Management

The first step is to evaluate for medical contributors and review medications, particularly antidepressants. The guideline describes lithium or valproate as first-line pharmacological options, with lamotrigine as another consideration. If monotherapy is insufficient, combinations of mood stabilizers or a mood stabilizer plus an antipsychotic may be considered.

20. Maintenance Treatment

Maintenance goals Prevent recurrence of either polarity, reduce residual symptoms and improve overall functioning.

The guideline's general principle is to continue the medication that was effective during the acute phase, taking into account long-term tolerability, previous polarity, recurrence pattern and patient preference.

Agents with strong maintenance evidence in the source

  • Lithium.
  • Valproate.

Other agents discussed

  • Olanzapine.
  • Quetiapine.
  • Long-acting risperidone.
  • Lamotrigine for depressive relapse prevention.
  • Other antipsychotics for selected polarity patterns.

Maintenance selection according to longitudinal course

Clinical pattern Agents discussed in the source
Predominantly manic recurrence Lithium, valproate or carbamazepine may be considered.
Predominantly depressive recurrence Lithium, lamotrigine or quetiapine may be considered.
Severe/recurrent illness Combination of lithium and valproate may be considered.
Long-term antipsychotic review

The source emphasizes that people with mood disorders may be particularly vulnerable to tardive dyskinesia with long-term antipsychotic exposure, especially typical antipsychotics. Continued need for an antipsychotic should therefore be reassessed according to the longitudinal course.

Similarly, discontinuation of antidepressants should be considered after remission of bipolar depression, according to the source.

21. Special Clinical Situations

Situation Key considerations from the 2017 guideline
Suicidality Assess ideation, plans and attempts at every follow-up. Use high-risk management and consider inpatient care when indicated. Educate patient/family about warning signs. Lithium is described as reducing suicide risk; ECT is an important option during high-risk acute episodes.
Catatonia Evaluate differential diagnoses and organic causes. Lorazepam is described as an initial treatment; ECT is considered when benzodiazepines fail or symptoms recur after withdrawal.
Psychotic features Antipsychotic treatment is warranted when psychotic symptoms are present.
Violence/aggression Assess dangerousness, especially during mania. Serious violence risk may warrant inpatient care. Injectable antipsychotics or lorazepam may be used for acute management.
Alcohol/substance use Treat bipolar disorder and substance-use disorder concurrently. Substance use may precipitate episodes, increase cycling and suicide risk, worsen response and alter medication tolerability.
Comorbid psychiatric illness Anxiety disorders, ADHD and other comorbidities may become more evident after the acute episode. Assess and treat them alongside bipolar disorder.
Physical illness Conduct thorough medical assessment and consider comorbid illness and concomitant medication when choosing treatment.
Perioperative period The source recommends discontinuing lithium before surgery because of potential haemodynamic, electrolyte and renal-excretion issues; restart postoperatively when clinically stable and able to drink, taking interacting drugs into account.

22. Pregnancy, Postpartum and Breastfeeding

Source-specific recommendation

The 2017 guideline considers lithium, valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine to have important reproductive considerations and advises that pregnancy should be planned whenever possible. Medication decisions require individualized risk–benefit discussion.

Pregnancy

  • Plan pregnancy whenever possible.
  • Discuss risks and benefits of continuing or stopping medication.
  • Where feasible, avoid exposure to mood stabilizers during pregnancy as described by the source.
  • If medication is required, use the minimum effective regimen and dose appropriate to the clinical situation.
  • The source discusses lithium use in the second and third trimesters when required.
  • Antipsychotics, particularly high-potency typical antipsychotics, are discussed as possible options in selected situations.
  • Use folic acid during pregnancy.
  • If mood stabilizer exposure occurs, the source discusses fetal monitoring including alpha-fetoprotein at 20 weeks.

Postpartum and breastfeeding

  • The postpartum period carries a high risk of relapse.
  • Newborns should be evaluated when the mother received psychotropic medication during pregnancy.
  • Lithium requires particular caution postpartum.
  • If valproate is used postpartum, the source recommends appropriate neonatal liver-function and haemogram monitoring.

23. Elderly Patients

  • Higher rates of medical comorbidity and substance-use disorders.
  • Greater sensitivity to medication adverse effects.
  • Higher risk of falls and hip fracture.
  • Potential cognitive adverse effects with lithium or benzodiazepines.
  • Start low and go slow.

24. Children and Adolescents

The guideline notes high rates of comorbidity, including ADHD, conduct disorder and substance-use disorders. Distinguishing mania from ADHD may be difficult, and stabilization may take longer.

Source-specific age statement

The guideline states that data on lithium before age 12 were not available at the time and did not recommend lithium before this age. It recommends lower starting doses and slow titration in younger patients.

25. Adverse Effects and Their Management

Lithium

Adverse effect Management described in the source
Polyuria / polydipsia Reduce dose or consider once-daily dosing; source discusses diuretic strategies in selected cases.
Tremor Reduce dose; beta-blockers may be considered.
GI symptoms Give after meals; formulation changes may be considered.
Weight gain Address dose and lifestyle factors; consider alternatives if clinically necessary.
Hypothyroidism The source states that hypothyroidism is not by itself an indication to stop lithium; thyroid replacement may be used.
Cardiac conduction abnormality Reduce dose and consider discontinuation in significant abnormalities in consultation with appropriate medical specialists.
Renal adverse effects Monitor renal function; selected treatments for impaired concentrating capacity are discussed.
Dermatological effects Reduce dose and treat dermatological disease; discontinuation may be considered when severe/refractory.

Valproate

  • GI symptoms: dose reduction or formulation changes may help.
  • Tremor: beta-blockers are discussed.
  • Hepatotoxicity: discontinue valproate.
  • Leukopenia/thrombocytopenia: discontinue when clinically significant.
  • Weight gain/increased appetite: dietary measures are discussed.
  • Polycystic ovarian disease: discontinuation may need consideration if alternatives exist.
  • Severe hepatic failure, haemorrhagic pancreatitis or agranulocytosis require discontinuation and urgent medical management.

Carbamazepine

  • Diplopia/blurred vision: dose reduction.
  • Nausea: dose reduction.
  • Ataxia: dose reduction.
  • Skin rash: reduce or stop depending on severity.
  • Severe leukopenia/thrombocytopenia: stop the medication.
  • Hyponatraemia may require discontinuation.

26. Toxicity and Acute Toxicological Management

Medical emergency

Lithium and carbamazepine toxicity can be fatal and are medical emergencies. Suspected significant toxicity requires immediate medical assessment rather than routine outpatient adjustment.

Lithium toxicity

The source notes that toxic effects are usually seen when serum lithium exceeds approximately 1.5 mEq/L, with potentially life-threatening effects at higher levels. Management includes stopping lithium, maintaining airway and intravenous access, supportive management and haemodialysis in selected severe cases.

Valproate toxicity

The source describes haemodialysis as a possible intervention in severe valproate toxicity, while noting that clear guidance for this is less established.

Carbamazepine toxicity

The source discusses gastric lavage, haemoperfusion and supportive measures in carbamazepine intoxication.

27. Monitoring and Follow-up

Monitoring should extend beyond symptom reduction. The guideline emphasizes response, adverse effects, treatment adherence, serum drug levels where relevant, physical health, pregnancy-related issues, disability, access to care and therapeutic alliance.

Every clinical review

  • Current mood polarity and symptom severity.
  • Suicidal and violence risk.
  • Adherence and barriers.
  • Adverse effects.
  • Substance use.
  • Functioning and psychosocial stressors.

Longitudinal review

  • Number and type of episodes.
  • Predominant polarity.
  • Rapid cycling.
  • Residual symptoms.
  • Comorbid psychiatric/medical disorders.
  • Need for continued medication combinations.

Metabolic monitoring with long-term antipsychotics

The guideline states that the metabolic-monitoring recommendations used for atypical antipsychotics in schizophrenia may also be followed when long-term antipsychotic treatment is used in bipolar disorder.

  • Weight, BMI and/or waist circumference.
  • Blood pressure.
  • Fasting glucose.
  • Fasting lipid profile.
  • Lifestyle and dietary intervention.

28. Practical Clinical Algorithm

1
Affective symptoms present Consider organic, substance-induced, psychotic and other differential diagnoses.
2
Establish bipolar diagnosis Use current diagnostic criteria and longitudinal collateral information.
3
Assess current polarity Depression, hypomania/mania, mixed state or remission.
4
Assess risk Suicide, violence, self-neglect, psychosis, substance use and medical instability.
5
Select setting Outpatient when safe; inpatient when risk or severity requires it.
6
Choose phase-specific treatment Use mood stabilizer/antipsychotic/ECT strategies according to polarity and severity.
7
Add psychosocial care Psychoeducation, family intervention, CBT and/or IPSRT as appropriate.
8
Monitor Response, adverse effects, serum levels, metabolic health and adherence.
9
Plan maintenance Use longitudinal polarity and recurrence pattern to select long-term prophylaxis.

29. Key Clinical Points

01 · Think longitudinally

The current episode is only one part of the formulation. Previous episodes, predominant polarity, treatment response, cycling pattern and interepisodic recovery matter.

02 · Identify polarity before prescribing

Treatment choice is strongly influenced by whether the patient is manic, depressed, mixed or in remission.

03 · Suicide risk is phase-independent

Actively assess suicidality during depression, mixed states, mania and euthymia rather than assuming risk is limited to depression.

04 · Lithium requires structured monitoring

Check renal, thyroid and relevant physical parameters before treatment and monitor serum levels and toxicity throughout therapy.

05 · Antidepressants require caution

The source does not recommend antidepressant monotherapy and advises against their use in rapid-cycling illness and mixed episodes.

06 · Psychosocial treatment is integral

Psychoeducation, family-focused interventions, CBT and IPSRT support relapse prevention, functioning and adherence.

07 · Maintenance starts early

The treatment selected during an acute episode should be considered in light of its suitability for long-term prophylaxis and tolerability.

08 · Monitor the patient, not only the mood

Physical health, metabolic risk, adverse effects, adherence, substance use, functioning and caregiver burden all require ongoing attention.

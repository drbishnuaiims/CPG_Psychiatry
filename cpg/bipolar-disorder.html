CLINICAL PRACTICE GUIDELINE

Clinical Practice Guidelines for Management of Bipolar Disorder

A detailed, structured clinical note based on Shah N, Grover S, Rao GP. Indian Journal of Psychiatry. 2017;59:51–66.

Source: Indian Psychiatric Society CPG update Published: 2017 DOI: 10.4103/0019-5545.196974
Source scope

This page is a structured clinical note derived from the supplied 2017 article. It preserves the article's recommendations, tables, terminology and evidence classifications rather than silently updating them with newer guidelines. Drug doses, serum ranges and monitoring intervals should therefore be cross-checked against current prescribing information and contemporary guidelines before clinical use.

Important source caveats
  • The article states that the 2017 guidelines are an update to the IPS 2005 CPGs and should be read along with the earlier version.
  • The original article contains apparent caption/context errors: its Figure 1 is labelled “Initial evaluation and management plan for schizophrenia” despite describing bipolar disorder, and Table 6 is similarly captioned for schizophrenia while listing bipolar ECT indications. The content has been retained as bipolar-specific where the surrounding text clearly identifies it as such.
  • The source text gives some medication-dose wording that may reflect publication/typographical limitations. Do not treat the page as a current prescribing formulary.

1. Overview

Bipolar disorder (BPAD) is described as a serious mental disorder characterized by episodes of depression, hypomania/mania and mixed episodes, with interepisodic recovery. The article notes that many patients continue to have residual symptoms between episodes. The illness commonly begins in adolescence or early adulthood and can substantially affect the patient and caregivers.

Clinical burden

Educational and occupational difficulties, interpersonal dysfunction, marital problems, disability, suicidal behaviour and medication adverse effects are highlighted.

Comorbidity

Patients have substantial rates of both physical and psychiatric comorbidity, which must be actively assessed and considered in treatment selection.

Indian context

The authors emphasize limited resources, inadequate knowledge about illness/treatment and limited access to healthcare as practical challenges in India.

Why the CPG was updated

  • IPS had previously formulated CPGs for BPAD in 2005.
  • The 2017 article was intended to update the earlier guideline because of developments in evidence, particularly pharmacological evidence.
  • The authors explicitly recommend reading the 2017 guideline together with the earlier IPS 2005 version.
Core principle: Management is phase-specific. The current polarity, severity, past episodes, previous response, adverse effects, comorbidity, treatment adherence and clinical course all influence treatment selection.

2. Assessment and Evaluation

Assessment is presented as an ongoing process involving both the patient and caregivers. History from the patient and available collateral sources, mental status examination, diagnostic formulation and longitudinal reassessment are central.

2.1 Diagnostic assessment

  • Establish the diagnosis using current diagnostic criteria.
  • Consider diagnostic uncertainty early in the illness because manic symptoms can resemble schizophrenia, acute/transient psychosis and other psychiatric syndromes.
  • Consider substance-induced and organic/secondary affective disorders when symptoms are atypical or when substance/medical causes are possible.
  • Determine the current polarity: hypomania/mania, depression, mixed presentation or remission.

2.2 Longitudinal illness history

  • Number of previous episodes.
  • Type of first lifetime episode.
  • Predominant polarity.
  • Duration and severity of episodes.
  • Degree of interepisodic recovery.
  • History of suicidal behaviour.
  • Violence and agitation.
  • Seasonal variation in onset.
  • Rapid cycling and ultra-rapid cycling features.
  • Relationship of episodes to psychosocial stressors and disturbances in biological rhythms.

2.3 Current episode and risk assessment

  • Severity and dimensions of current symptoms.
  • Suicide risk: ideation, intent, plan, access to means, potential lethality, command hallucinations, severe anxiety, substance use, previous attempts/self-harm and family history of suicide/self-harm.
  • Risk of harm to others, aggression and violence.
  • Agitation and behavioural disturbance.
  • Functional impairment.
  • Psychiatric and medical comorbidity.
  • Socio-cultural context, support, stigma and coping.
Do not miss: Suicide risk must be assessed longitudinally. The article emphasizes assessment at every follow-up and not only during depressive episodes; suicidal behaviour may also occur during mixed, manic and euthymic phases.

2.4 Physical and laboratory assessment

A thorough physical examination is recommended to identify physical illness and possible secondary mood syndromes. Investigations should be used judiciously according to clinical need.

2.5 Caregiver assessment

  • Knowledge about the illness.
  • Knowledge about treatment.
  • Attitudes and beliefs regarding illness and treatment.
  • Impact of illness on the caregiver.
  • Burden and distress.
  • Stigma.
  • Personal and marital impact.
  • Social and personal resources.

2.6 Past treatment history

  • Medication previously used.
  • Clinical response.
  • Duration of treatment.
  • Adverse effects.
  • Reasons for discontinuation.
  • Adherence/compliance.

2.7 Ongoing reassessment

The guideline emphasizes that assessment does not end after diagnosis. During treatment, reassess response, adverse effects, adherence, marriage/pregnancy, disability, healthcare needs, accessibility of the treatment team and therapeutic alliance.

Assessment checklist
  • Patient + caregiver
  • Diagnosis + differential diagnosis
  • Current polarity + severity
  • Suicide / violence / self-care risk
  • Substance use
  • Physical and psychiatric comorbidity
  • Functioning and psychosocial context
  • Past treatment response and adverse effects
  • Baseline investigations
  • Standardized rating scales where appropriate
  • Longitudinal reassessment

3. Formulating the Treatment Plan

The treatment plan should be formulated collaboratively with the patient, caregivers and relevant members of the treatment team. It should be practical, feasible and flexible, and periodically revised according to changing needs.

3.1 Decide the treatment setting

Most patients can be managed as outpatients. Inpatient care is required for selected patients depending on safety, severity, functional capacity and medical/psychiatric complexity. Where inpatient facilities are unavailable, the patient/family should be informed and referral to the nearest available facility facilitated.

3.2 Decide the treatment package

DomainOptions / considerations
PharmacologicalMood stabilizers, antipsychotics, antidepressants in selected circumstances, adjunctive medicines.
SomaticECT; the article also discusses rTMS and tDCS as treatment options.
PsychosocialPsychoeducation, CBT, IPSRT, family-focused intervention and other psychosocial/rehabilitative approaches.
LifestyleExercise, dietary measures and avoidance of smoking, alcohol and other substances.

3.3 Factors influencing medication selection

  • Current polarity.
  • Psychotic symptoms.
  • Previous treatment response.
  • Previous adverse effects.
  • Cost.
  • Medical and psychiatric comorbidity.
  • Patient/family preference.
  • Preferred route of administration.
  • Medication availability.
  • Current metabolic profile.
  • Previous adherence.
  • Treatment resistance.
Clinical decision rule: The guideline does not advocate selecting a drug in isolation from the patient. Polarity + longitudinal history + safety + comorbidity + previous response + tolerability + adherence + preference + feasibility should drive the decision.

4.2 Lithium

The article describes lithium as the oldest mood stabilizer and reports efficacy in acute episodes of either polarity and prevention of relapse of both polarities. It also cites evidence for a role in suicide prevention.

Before starting lithium
  • Review renal dysfunction, thyroid dysfunction and cardiac conduction abnormalities.
  • Assess dermatological disease.
  • In women, ascertain last menstrual period and perform pregnancy testing when required.
  • Educate regarding adverse effects and dehydration.
  • Review medicines that can raise lithium levels, including diuretics, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors.

Starting and monitoring

  • Start at a low dose, preferably in divided doses, and titrate upward with serum-level monitoring.
  • Steady state is described as occurring at about 5 days after initiation or a dose increase; the article recommends checking the level at that point, or earlier if toxicity is suspected.
  • Acute treatment serum range stated: 0.6–1.0 mEq/L.
  • Prophylaxis range stated: 0.6–0.8 mEq/L; the article notes that 0.4–0.8 mEq/L may sometimes be sufficient but may carry higher relapse risk.
  • Once stable, once-daily administration may reduce adverse effects and improve adherence.
  • Stable serum levels: every 3–4 months.
  • Renal function: every 2–3 months during the first 6 months, then 6–12 monthly in stable patients.
  • Thyroid function: once or twice during the first 6 months, then 6–12 monthly in stable patients, with more frequent testing when indicated.

4.3 Valproate / divalproex

Valproate is described as useful in acute mania and mixed episodes and for maintenance prevention of mania and depression. Evidence for acute depression is described as less robust than for lithium.

Before starting valproate
  • Review hepatic, haematological and bleeding problems.
  • Obtain liver function tests and haemogram.
  • In young women, ascertain menstrual history and perform pregnancy testing when required.
  • Because of weight gain/metabolic effects, assess fasting glucose, lipid profile and anthropometry.
  • The article describes low-dose initiation (e.g. 250 mg BD or 250 mg TID) followed by titration.
  • The source text states a “maximum daily dose” of 60 mg/day; because this wording may represent a publication/unit issue, it should not be used as a standalone current dosing instruction.
  • Therapeutic serum level stated: 50–100 µg/mL.
  • Serum level after approximately 5 days of a stable dose during titration.
  • Immediate-release sample: approximately 12 h after the dose.
  • Extended-release sample: approximately 21–24 h after the last dose.
  • Once stabilized, OD or BD dosing may be used to reduce adverse effects and improve adherence.
  • The article notes approximately 15% lower bioavailability of extended-release formulations compared with immediate-release preparations.

Clinical features associated with reported response to valproate

  • Bipolar II disorder.
  • Dysphoric/mixed mania.
  • Rapid cycling.
  • Later age of onset.
  • Shorter illness duration.
  • Long/severe course.
  • Comorbid alcohol use disorder.
  • Anxiety/panic attacks.
  • PTSD.
  • Marked sleep disturbance.
  • Explosive dyscontrol/aggression.
  • Comorbid migraine.

4.4 Lamotrigine

Lamotrigine is described as useful for bipolar depression and prevention of depressive relapse. The major safety issue emphasized is rash, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis.

  • Risk is particularly relevant early in treatment and when combined with valproate.
  • Slow titration is emphasized to reduce rash risk.
  • Patients should be instructed to report rash promptly.
  • Widespread/diffuse rash or rash with fever/sore throat/systemic symptoms is a reason described in the article to stop lamotrigine.
  • Source titration: 25 mg/day for the first 2 weeks, then 50 mg/day during weeks 3–4, followed by increases of 50 mg/day per week depending on response.
Lamotrigine safety: A new or progressive rash during titration should be treated as a potentially serious adverse reaction until evaluated. The source specifically highlights SJS/TEN risk.

4.5 Carbamazepine

  • Useful for acute bipolar mania and relapse prevention.
  • Before treatment, review blood dyscrasias and hepatic dysfunction.
  • Educate about hepatic, haematological and skin reactions.
  • Baseline: haemogram, liver function and renal function; serum electrolytes may be particularly relevant in older patients because of hyponatraemia risk.
  • Source starting dose: 200 mg/day in divided doses, with slow titration.
  • Usual maintenance described around 1000 mg/day, with an individual range of 200–1600 mg/day in the article.
  • Serum range of 4–12 µg/mL is cited, although the article notes that a specific BPAD therapeutic range has not been established and this range is commonly borrowed from seizure treatment.
  • Serum levels after about 5 days of initiation/dose increase; sample approximately 12 h after the last dose.

4.6 Other anticonvulsants

  • Oxcarbazepine: the article notes lack of double-blind RCT data and small open-label evidence suggesting possible benefit as monotherapy or add-on treatment in refractory mania.
  • Topiramate: open-label data suggested possible add-on benefit in bipolar depression.
  • Gabapentin: studies in mania were negative.

4.7 Antipsychotics

The article discusses evidence for several antipsychotics across acute mania, bipolar depression and maintenance.

Clinical role described in the articleAgents/examples
Acute maniaOlanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, risperidone, paliperidone, ziprasidone; typical agents such as haloperidol are also discussed.
Bipolar depressionQuetiapine monotherapy; olanzapine–fluoxetine combination; lurasidone is discussed for acute bipolar depression.
Relapse preventionOlanzapine and quetiapine for depressive and manic relapse; long-acting risperidone and ziprasidone for selected maintenance outcomes.

4.8 Combination therapy

The article reports that combining lithium or valproate with an antipsychotic in acute mania may produce greater efficacy and faster onset than monotherapy. Combination treatment is particularly considered with more severe/disruptive mania.

4.9 Benzodiazepines

  • Clonazepam and lorazepam may be used adjunctively during acute episodes.
  • The article notes that apparent antimanic effects are difficult to separate from sedation.
  • They are particularly useful for agitation and catatonia, with lorazepam specifically highlighted.

4.10 ECT

Source-listed ECT indications
  • Bipolar depression not responding to adequate pharmacotherapy.
  • Bipolar mania not responding to adequate pharmacotherapy.
  • Catatonia.
  • Need for rapid symptom control.
  • Life-threatening suicidal behaviour.
  • Severe agitation/violence threatening others.
  • Refusal to eat with risk to life.
  • Previous good response to ECT.
  • Augmentation of partial pharmacological response.
  • Inability to tolerate pharmacotherapy.
  • Treatment-resistant illness.
  • Severe depression or mania during pregnancy.

4.11 Antidepressants

  • Antidepressants in bipolar depression are presented as controversial because of the risk of manic/hypomanic switch.
  • The article reports meta-analytic evidence that adding antidepressants to a mood stabilizer may be superior to mood stabilizer plus placebo and was not associated with increased switch risk in the evidence reviewed.
  • Paroxetine accounted for many negative studies; the authors state that if avoiding antidepressant treatment, paroxetine may be avoided.
  • Bupropion is described as having the lowest switch risk among the antidepressants discussed.
  • Antidepressant monotherapy is not recommended in the source.
  • Antidepressants should not be used as monotherapy and should be avoided in rapid cycling and mixed episodes according to the article.
High-yield antidepressant principle: In this 2017 CPG, antidepressants are positioned as adjunctive treatment, not standalone treatment, in bipolar depression.

5. Non-pharmacological and Psychosocial Management

Psychosocial treatment is described as an important adjunct to pharmacotherapy, particularly during bipolar depression and maintenance. The article supports psychoeducation, IPSRT, CBT and family-focused interventions, with reported benefits in relapse prevention, functioning and adherence.

5.1 Psychoeducation

  • Assess patient/caregiver knowledge of aetiology, treatment and prognosis.
  • Explain the diagnosis.
  • Discuss depression, mania, hypomania and mixed symptoms.
  • Explain treatment choices, effectiveness, adverse effects and expected duration.
  • Discuss adherence and early relapse recognition.
  • Address substance use, marriage and other psychosocial problems.
  • Teach communication and problem-solving.
  • Improve insight and adaptive coping.
  • Address expressed emotion.
  • Advise lifestyle and dietary modification.
Psychoeducation is not a one-time intervention. The article recommends feedback from previous sessions and tailoring subsequent psychoeducation to patient and caregiver needs.

5.2 Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT)

The rationale is that circadian/social rhythm disruption can precipitate episodes. IPSRT therefore targets routine stabilization and interpersonal functioning.

StepCore components
1Explain rationale; assess relationship between episodes, interpersonal problems and social routine; educate about BPAD; identify grief/role transition/role dispute; agree on target; initiate Social Rhythm Metric.
2Establish regular daily routine; resolve interpersonal problems; analyse communication; role-play alternative communication; maintain social rhythms.
3During continuation/maintenance, reinforce use of previously learned IPSRT skills.
4Termination.

5.3 CBT

  • Useful in bipolar depression and maintenance.
  • Educates about the illness.
  • Builds cognitive-behavioural coping skills.
  • Addresses psychosocial stressors and associated problems.
  • Supports medication/treatment adherence.
  • Monitors symptoms and helps prevent relapse.

5.4 Family-focused intervention

The article emphasizes the importance of family involvement in the Indian setting while also recognizing that criticism, poor supervision and over-involvement can increase relapse risk through high expressed emotion.

  • Family assessment.
  • Psychoeducation about symptoms, early recognition, aetiology, treatment and adherence.
  • Communication skills training with behavioural rehearsal.
  • Acceptance of vulnerability to future episodes.
  • Differentiate the patient's personality from manifestations of BPAD.
  • Stress recognition and coping.
  • Problem-solving skills.
  • Establish and maintain functional relationships.

5.5 Lifestyle and rehabilitation

  • Physical exercise.
  • Dietary modification.
  • Abstinence from smoking, alcohol and other substances.
  • Culturally adapted rehabilitation programmes for patients and families.

5.6 Treatment adherence

The article cites a substantial non-adherence/dropout burden, reporting that approximately 20–60% of patients may become non-compliant with medication and drop out of treatment.

Practical adherence strategy
Explain exactly when and how often medicines should be taken.
Prefer once-daily dosing where appropriate.
Minimize number of tablets and medicines where feasible.
Ask which formulation the patient prefers.
Check the complete prescription for duplication.
Explain that benefit may take days to weeks.
Explain the need to continue treatment even after improvement.
Explain expected adverse effects and what to do if they occur.
Encourage reporting of adverse effects.
Advise patients to consult the psychiatrist before stopping medication themselves.

6. Management of Hypomania / Mania / Mixed Episode

Immediate priorities
  1. Assess severity, safety, suicidality, violence/aggression, substance use and medical/psychiatric comorbidity.
  2. Determine whether inpatient care is required.
  3. If the patient is taking an antidepressant and presents with hypomanic/manic/mixed symptoms, the source recommends stopping it.
  4. Select medication according to polarity, severity, previous response, adverse effects and treatment history.
  5. Initiate psychosocial intervention early.
  6. Consider ECT when indicated.

6.1 First-line pharmacological options described

  • Lithium.
  • Valproate.
  • Olanzapine.
  • Haloperidol.
  • Quetiapine.
  • Aripiprazole.
  • Risperidone.
  • Paliperidone.
  • Ziprasidone.

Typical antipsychotics such as haloperidol, trifluoperazine and chlorpromazine are also discussed in the Indian setting, particularly for irritability, aggression, impulsivity and psychotic features, although the article notes a higher incidence of extrapyramidal reactions.

6.2 Monotherapy versus combination

  • Less severe mania: monotherapy may be considered.
  • Severe/disruptive mania: combination treatment with lithium or valproate plus an antipsychotic may be considered.
  • Short-term adjunctive benzodiazepines may be required.
  • For patients refusing medication and becoming unmanageable, depot/long-acting antipsychotic treatment may be considered.

6.3 Breakthrough mania/hypomania

  1. Optimize the current treatment.
  2. Check serum levels when relevant, particularly lithium and valproate.
  3. If needed, add antipsychotic and/or benzodiazepine according to severity.
  4. If an optimized first-line agent provides no significant clinical benefit after approximately 2 weeks, the article suggests adding another first-line agent.
  5. Alternative strategies include switching lithium ↔ valproate, changing to carbamazepine, adding an antipsychotic or switching the antipsychotic.
  6. Consider ECT for marked disruption, non-response to combination treatment, previous good ECT response, pregnancy or mixed episodes.
  7. Start psychoeducation/family-focused treatment early; CBT or IPSRT can be considered when the patient is cooperative.

6.4 Inpatient indications

  • Severe agitation or violence posing risk to others.
  • Medical/psychiatric comorbidity making outpatient care unsafe or ineffective.
  • Failure to respond to a combination of first-line agents.
  • Treatment-refractory mania/mixed episode.

6.5 Source algorithm: escalation

SituationSource-described strategy
Not on treatmentFirst-line monotherapy with lithium/valproate/antipsychotic, or lithium/valproate + antipsychotic combination depending on severity.
Breakthrough while on treatmentStop antidepressant if relevant; optimize mood stabilizer; add antipsychotic if inadequate/severe; benzodiazepine as needed.
Failure of adequate first-line trialSwitch lithium ↔ valproate; add/change antipsychotic; consider carbamazepine; consider ECT.
Treatment-refractory maniaConsider clozapine and other agents; ECT is also an option.

7. Management of Bipolar Depression

The goals are euthymia, restoration of normal functioning and prevention of a switch to hypomania/mania.

7.1 First-line options described in the article

  • Lithium.
  • Lamotrigine.
  • Quetiapine.
  • Olanzapine–fluoxetine combination.
  • Lithium + valproate in selected combinations.
  • Valproate + antidepressant in selected circumstances.
  • Mood stabilizer/antipsychotic + antidepressant combinations where appropriate.

7.2 If already receiving treatment

  1. First check adherence.
  2. If adherence is adequate, optimize the current mood stabilizer.
  3. Consider adding bupropion or an SSRI as an adjunct where appropriate.
  4. Consider adding or changing to lithium, lamotrigine or quetiapine.
  5. Consider valproate in selected combinations.
  6. Use psychosocial intervention when depression is mild to moderate.
  7. Consider ECT when indicated.
  8. rTMS/tDCS are described as having preliminary evidence.
Avoid antidepressant monotherapy. The article specifically recommends against antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar depression and emphasizes the switch risk.

7.3 ECT in bipolar depression

  • Severe depression.
  • Suicidality.
  • Risk of harm to others.
  • Catatonia.
  • Psychotic symptoms.
  • Pregnancy.
  • Concurrent medical illness limiting pharmacotherapy.
  • Failure of adequate pharmacotherapy.

8. Rapid Cycling Affective Disorder

The article defines rapid cycling affective disorder (RCAD) as four or more episodes of depression, mania, hypomania or mixed episodes in a single year, with episodes separated by partial/full remission for at least 2 months or by a switch to an episode of opposite polarity.

Risk factors listed in the article

  • Hypothyroidism.
  • Long-term aggressive antidepressant use.
  • Comorbid substance use.
  • Minor tranquilizer, stimulant or caffeine abuse.
  • Cyclothymic/hyperthymic temperament.
  • Female sex.
  • Menopause.
  • Temporal lobe dysrhythmias.
  • Several episodes/stressors and possible kindling effects.
  • Illness beginning as depression.
  • Frequent and more depressive episodes.
  • Possible COMT/BDNF abnormalities.
  • Possible biological rhythm disturbances, including wakefulness/light exposure.

Management approach

  1. Look for underlying medical conditions contributing to rapid cycling.
  2. Review antidepressant exposure because the article associates antidepressants with increased cycling.
  3. Lithium or valproate are described as first-line options.
  4. Lamotrigine may also be considered.
  5. If monotherapy is ineffective, consider combinations of mood stabilizers or mood stabilizer + antipsychotic.

9. Maintenance Treatment

The maintenance goals are to prevent recurrence of either polarity, reduce/eliminate residual symptoms and improve overall functioning.

General maintenance principle

The article recommends continuing the medication that was started during the acute phase in many cases. Therefore, acute treatment selection should already consider long-term tolerability, patient preference and recurrence pattern.

  • More manic episodes: lithium, valproate or carbamazepine may be preferred according to the source.
  • More depressive episodes: lithium, lamotrigine or quetiapine may be considered.
  • Severe illness: lithium + valproate may be considered.
  • Long-term antipsychotic need should be periodically reassessed, especially because mood-disorder patients may be vulnerable to extrapyramidal adverse effects/tardive dyskinesia with long-term antipsychotic exposure.
  • Antidepressant discontinuation should be considered after bipolar depression remits.
  • Maintenance combination therapy is suggested for patients who have not responded adequately to optimal monotherapy.

10. Special Situations

10.1 Suicidality

  • Suicide risk is high in BPAD.
  • Suicidal behaviour may occur in depressive, mixed and manic episodes and has also been reported in euthymia.
  • Assess ideation, plans and attempts at every follow-up.
  • Institute high-risk management and consider inpatient care when risk is high.
  • Psychoeducation should address warning signs.
  • Provide psychological support for severe distress related to loss, stigma and other factors.
  • Lithium is described as reducing suicide risk.
  • ECT is highlighted for high-risk suicidal patients during an acute episode.

10.2 Catatonia

  • May occur during both depressive and manic phases.
  • Consider the full differential diagnosis of catatonia.
  • Investigate for organic causes.
  • Initial management may include benzodiazepines, especially lorazepam.
  • ECT should be considered when benzodiazepines fail or catatonia relapses after benzodiazepine discontinuation.

10.3 Psychotic features

When psychotic symptoms are present, the source recommends use of antipsychotics.

10.4 Violence and aggression

  • Assess dangerousness at every assessment, particularly during acute mania.
  • Inpatient treatment may be considered with serious threat or actual violence.
  • Injectable antipsychotics such as haloperidol, or lorazepam, are described as options for acute management.

10.5 Comorbid alcohol/substance use

  • May precipitate episodes and increase episode frequency.
  • May be associated with rapid cycling and higher suicide risk.
  • May be associated with poorer treatment response and longer time to remission.
  • Can influence medication selection and adverse-effect vulnerability.
  • BPAD and the substance use disorder should be treated concurrently.
  • Abstinence should be encouraged during acute and maintenance phases.

10.6 Comorbid psychiatric disorders

  • Anxiety disorders and ADHD are specifically mentioned.
  • Comorbidities may become clearer after the acute episode resolves.
  • Evaluate during maintenance and treat concurrently.

10.7 Physical comorbidity

  • Patients require assessment for physical illnesses.
  • Comorbid conditions should be monitored throughout treatment.
  • Physical disease and concomitant medicines should influence treatment setting and medication choice.

10.8 Perioperative period

  • Lithium may contribute to haemodynamic instability, sodium/potassium disturbances and altered renal excretion.
  • The source recommends discontinuing lithium before surgery.
  • Postoperatively, restart gradually once haemodynamically stable, able/allowed to drink and not receiving potentially interfering drugs.

10.9 Pregnancy

Pregnancy requires individualized risk–benefit planning. The 2017 source describes mood stabilizers, including lithium, as associated with teratogenic/congenital-malformation risk and recommends pregnancy planning and minimizing exposure where feasible.
  • Where feasible, attempt to avoid lithium, valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine during pregnancy.
  • If discontinuation is not tolerated, discuss and document risks and benefits, particularly during the first trimester.
  • If required, lithium may be used in the second and third trimesters at the minimal optimal dose according to the source.
  • High-potency typical antipsychotics may be considered when required.
  • Use folic acid.
  • If a mood stabilizer is continued, the article recommends monitoring alpha-fetoprotein at 20 weeks.

10.10 Postpartum and breastfeeding

  • Postpartum relapse risk is high.
  • Newborns exposed to psychotropics during pregnancy should be assessed for malformations, overdose/toxicity and withdrawal.
  • Use lithium cautiously postpartum.
  • When maternal valproate is used postpartum, the source recommends appropriate neonatal LFT and haemogram monitoring.

10.11 Elderly

  • Higher rates of medical comorbidity and substance use disorders.
  • Greater sensitivity to adverse effects.
  • Higher risk of falls and hip fracture.
  • Lithium and benzodiazepines may contribute to cognitive deficits.
  • Start low and go slow.

10.12 Children and adolescents

  • High comorbidity with ADHD, conduct disorder and substance use disorders.
  • Mania can be difficult to distinguish from ADHD.
  • Stabilization may take longer.
  • The article states that data for lithium before age 12 were unavailable and recommends against use before that age.
  • Start at lower doses and titrate slowly.

12. Toxicity and Emergency Management

Lithium and carbamazepine toxicity can be fatal and are medical emergencies. The article describes valproate as having a wider therapeutic window than lithium and carbamazepine.

12.1 Lithium toxicity

The source describes a therapeutic range of approximately 0.4–1.2 mEq/L, with toxic effects usually emerging above approximately 1.5 mEq/L and potentially life-threatening effects when levels exceed 2 mEq/L.

Reported levelClinical features in the source
1.5–2.5 mEq/LFine tremor, apathy, fatigue, muscle weakness, hyperreflexia, incontinence, gait disturbance; nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea; bradycardia, T-wave changes, sinoatrial block and AV block.
>2.5 mEq/LCoarse tremor, slurred speech/dysarthria, ataxia, hypertonia, spasticity, rigidity, myoclonus, seizures, stupor, coma and possible permanent neurological deficits; GI symptoms; cardiovascular abnormalities and hypotension; renal failure.

12.2 Management of lithium toxicity

  • Stop lithium.
  • Maintain airway.
  • Establish and maintain IV access.
  • The article recommends haemodialysis when serum lithium is >2.5 mEq/L.
  • In end-stage renal disease, the source states haemodialysis may be considered at levels below 2.5 mEq/L.

12.3 Valproate toxicity

Features listed include somnolence, heart block and coma. The article states that haemodialysis may be used, although it notes that clear guidelines were not available in the source.

12.4 Carbamazepine toxicity

  • Dizziness and ataxia.
  • Sedation and nystagmus.
  • Ophthalmoplegia.
  • Cerebellar/extrapyramidal signs.
  • Impaired consciousness.
  • Seizures.
  • Respiratory depression.
  • Stupor/coma.
  • Tachycardia and arrhythmia.
  • Cardiac conduction disturbance.
  • Hypotension.

The source describes gastric lavage, haemoperfusion and supportive measures as management approaches.

13. High-Yield Clinical Quick Reference

Before starting a mood stabilizer

  • Confirm diagnosis/current polarity.
  • Assess suicide/violence risk.
  • Review comorbidity and substance use.
  • Review previous treatment response/adverse effects.
  • Obtain drug-specific baseline investigations.
  • Discuss adherence, pregnancy where relevant and patient preference.

Acute mania

  • Safety first.
  • Stop antidepressant if switch/mixed/manic presentation.
  • Consider lithium/valproate or antipsychotic.
  • Severe mania: combination treatment may be required.
  • Benzodiazepine short term if needed.
  • ECT for selected severe/refractory/urgent cases.

Bipolar depression

  • Check adherence first.
  • Optimize existing treatment.
  • Lithium/lamotrigine/quetiapine are key options in the source.
  • Olanzapine–fluoxetine is described.
  • Do not use antidepressant monotherapy.
  • Consider ECT for severe/suicidal/catatonic/psychotic cases.

Maintenance

  • Prevent both poles.
  • Reduce residual symptoms.
  • Improve functioning.
  • Continue effective acute medication when appropriate.
  • Reassess long-term antipsychotic need.
  • Add psychosocial interventions.
One-line framework: Assess → establish polarity/severity → ensure safety → choose setting → choose treatment based on longitudinal history → monitor efficacy/adverse effects → add psychosocial care → reassess continuously → plan maintenance.

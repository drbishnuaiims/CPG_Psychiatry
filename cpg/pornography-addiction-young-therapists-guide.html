Source framing — read this first

The uploaded manual is a historical clinical guide to Internet addiction. It describes cybersexual addiction as one Internet-addiction subtype involving online pornography and adult fantasy/sexual online activity. The source uses older DSM-IV-era and “addiction” terminology; this note preserves that framework rather than silently converting it into a modern diagnostic classification.

Source: Young, A Therapist’s Guide to Assess and Treat Internet Addiction, pp. 1–45.
01 · Scope

What the source means by “pornography addiction”

The manual begins from the proposition that the Internet itself is a neutral tool, while patterns of use can become clinically problematic. Its focus is therefore not on Internet access per se, but on compulsive use of particular online applications and the impairment that follows.

Core concept

The source conceptualizes Internet addiction as a non-intoxicant, impulse-control-type phenomenon modeled on pathological gambling. A person becomes attached to a particular online application that repeatedly provides reinforcement and becomes difficult to control.

Cybersexual subtype

Cybersexual addiction is described as viewing, downloading or trading online pornography and/or participating in adult fantasy role-play chat rooms. The source identifies sexually explicit online material as a particularly prominent clinical presentation.

Clinical terminology
The manual is a historical source. It should be read as a therapeutic framework for compulsive Internet/cybersexual behavior, not as a statement that the terminology or diagnostic thresholds are current DSM/ICD criteria.
Compulsive online behavior Cybersexual activity Pornography Loss of control Functional impairment Escape coping Relapse prevention
02 · Diagnostic features

Eight-item screening framework

Young adapted DSM-IV pathological-gambling concepts into an eight-item Internet-dependency screen. The questions examine preoccupation, increasing use, failed control, affective reactions to cutting down, loss of control over duration, jeopardized functioning, concealment and use for mood escape.

1Preoccupation

Persistent thoughts about previous or future online activity.

2Escalation

Increasing time needed to obtain satisfaction.

3Failed control

Repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut down or stop.

4Withdrawal-like affect

Restlessness, irritability, low mood or dysphoria when reducing use.

Questions 1–4

  1. Are you preoccupied with Internet use or anticipating the next session?
  2. Do you need progressively more time online to obtain satisfaction?
  3. Have repeated attempts to control, reduce or stop failed?
  4. Does cutting down produce restlessness, irritability, depressed mood or similar distress?

Questions 5–8

  1. Do you remain online longer than originally intended?
  2. Has Internet use jeopardized an important relationship, job, education or career opportunity?
  3. Have you lied to others to conceal the extent of use?
  4. Do you use the Internet to escape problems or relieve dysphoric states such as guilt, anxiety, helplessness or depression?
Source threshold
In the manual, 5 or more “yes” responses out of 8, provided the behavior is not better explained by a manic episode, is considered the source's “addicted” range. The author explicitly notes that further work was needed regarding the scale’s construct validity and clinical utility.
Do not overread the number
The manual itself describes this as a workable measure requiring further validation. A positive screen should therefore trigger clinical assessment of pattern, context, impairment and comorbidity, not function as a standalone modern diagnosis.
03 · Associated features

What accompanies heavy compulsive use?

Sleep

Late-night sessions may progressively shift bedtime several hours later, producing sleep deprivation and daytime fatigue.

Body

Back strain, eyestrain and carpal-tunnel-type complaints are described with prolonged computer use and poor ergonomics.

Function

Compulsive Internet use may be associated with social isolation, depression, family discord, divorce, academic failure, job loss and financial problems.

Source observation
The manual also describes some heavy users consuming caffeine products to remain awake and extend online sessions.
04 · Subtypes

Internet addiction is application-specific

The source emphasizes that “Internet addiction” is broad: different applications can function as the trigger for excessive use. It describes five subtypes.

SubtypeSource descriptionTypical consequence highlighted
Cybersexual addictionViewing, downloading or trading online pornography; adult fantasy role-play chat rooms and related sexual online activity.Sexual compulsivity, high relapse vulnerability and disruption of functioning.
Cyber-relational addictionOver-involvement in online relationships, including virtual affairs, with online friends becoming more important than real-life relationships.Marital discord, family instability and withdrawal from offline relationships.
Net-compulsionsCompulsive online gambling, shopping, stock trading, auctions or related activities.Financial loss and interference with work or relationships.
Information overloadCompulsive web surfing, searching, collecting and organizing information.Reduced productivity and obsessive-compulsive tendencies.
Computer addictionProblematic computer-game use and other repetitive computer activities.Loss of productive time and occupational/organizational impairment.
Clinical pearl
For a pornography-focused presentation, assess the specific sexual application or behavior that functions as the trigger, rather than treating all Internet use as equally problematic.
05 · Case formulation

Case vignette: Amanda

The pattern

The manual presents Amanda, a 43-year-old homemaker, who progressed from initial Internet exploration to approximately 70 hours/week in online chat rooms. Her use displaced household responsibilities and family relationships. She concealed the extent of her use, resisted counseling, isolated herself in the computer room and denied that the behavior was problematic. Her marriage subsequently deteriorated and she moved in with an online acquaintance.

Behavioral markers
Concealment → escalating time → neglect of responsibilities → interpersonal disruption → denial/minimization → continued use despite consequences.
Formulation lesson
The clinically important signal is not simply “many hours online”; it is the interaction between reinforcement, loss of control, concealment and impairment.

A second part of the case describes Amanda's online relationship with “Paul,” an online acquaintance. The relationship offered romance, excitement and an escape from a dull routine. Anonymity allowed her to present an older photograph and temporarily experience herself as more attractive. The source uses this case to demonstrate how anonymity, convenience and escape can interact.

06 · Etiology / reinforcement

The ACE model

The source proposes the ACE model: Anonymity + Convenience + Escape. These three properties of cyberspace are presented as mechanisms that reward and reinforce problematic online behavior.

A · ANONYMITY

Reduced accountability

Online identities can conceal personal information and create a sense of control over how one is perceived. The source links this to increased confidence, alternative online personas and greater willingness to explore sexual material.

C · CONVENIENCE

Immediate access

Online pornography and other sexual material can be accessed continuously and privately. The source emphasizes instant gratification and anonymous availability as important to online sexual compulsivity.

E · ESCAPE

Emotional avoidance

Online stimulation may temporarily reduce awareness of stress, depression, anxiety, burnout, academic problems, unemployment, marital discord or other distress.

Mechanistic summary
Distress / unmet need → online access → immediate relief or stimulation → temporary improvement in affect → reinforcement → repeated use → consequences → renewed distress → further use.

Anonymity: three functions described

  • May reduce social inhibition and increase confidence in online interaction.
  • May permit an online persona that differs from the person's offline identity.
  • May facilitate sexual experimentation or exploration with less perceived social consequence.

Convenience

The manual emphasizes 24-hour availability, immediate gratification and the ease with which pornography and sexual chat environments can be accessed. It particularly notes the vulnerability of people with previous sexual compulsivity when these resources become readily available.

Escape

The behavior can temporarily “switch off” awareness of problems. However, because the underlying stressor remains unchanged, avoidance can increase the problem and subsequently become a trigger for renewed use.

07 · Gender differences

Application preference in the source

The manual discusses gender differences in terms of the online applications that users may preferentially select. These observations are explicitly presented in the historical source and should not be treated as universal contemporary rules.

Source observationDescription
MenThe source describes greater preference for online pornography, stock trading and high-end online purchasing.
WomenThe source describes greater preference for chat rooms for friendship/romance and more frequent lower-value online purchases.
CybersexThe source states that both men and women may engage in cybersex but describes men as more likely to gravitate toward visual stimuli and women toward text-driven fantasy role-play.
Interpret with caution
These are the author's historical observations, not diagnostic criteria or fixed biological rules. Individual assessment should remain behavior- and person-specific.
08 · Triggers & cognition

What initiates the binge cycle?

1Emotional state

Depression, hopelessness, pessimism, anxiety or distress.

2Cognition

Overgeneralization, selective abstraction, magnification or personalization.

3Life event

Marital problems, job dissatisfaction, unemployment, illness or academic instability.

4Online escape

Temporary relief reinforces the behavior.

Emotional states

The source describes excitement, euphoria and exhilaration during online activity, contrasted with unpleasant offline affect. Online behavior may temporarily generate feelings of calm, security, self-worth, accomplishment, control, intimacy or belonging.

Affect shift
Offline state describedOnline state described
Frustrated / worried / angryExcited / happy / thrilled
Anxious / depressedSupported / desirable
Low self-worthMore confident / attractive

Maladaptive cognitions

  • Catastrophic anticipation.
  • Overgeneralization.
  • All-or-nothing thinking.
  • Magnification of personal problems.
  • Selective abstraction of negative events.
  • Low self-reinforcement and high self-punishment.
  • Core beliefs such as “I am no good” or “I am a failure,” temporarily countered by an enhanced online self-evaluation.

Life events and unmet needs

The source identifies vulnerability when a person experiences dissatisfaction, lack of intimacy or social connection, low confidence, few compelling interests or loss of hope. Marital dissatisfaction, job dissatisfaction, illness, unemployment and academic instability may become contexts in which online use substitutes for more direct coping.

09 · Clinical assessment

Assessment must look beyond the presenting complaint

The manual emphasizes that comorbid psychiatric conditions are common and that self-referral is uncommon. People may present primarily with depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety-related symptoms or obsessive-compulsive tendencies while minimizing or concealing their Internet use.

Always ask directly
A routine psychiatric intake should include assessment of problematic Internet use and the specific applications involved, especially when there is unexplained functional deterioration, sleep disruption, relationship conflict or recurrent mood symptoms.

Psychiatric

Depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety-related disorders, obsessive-compulsive tendencies and other addictive behaviors.

Addiction context

Alcohol/drug dependence, sexual compulsivity and other repetitive or compulsive behaviors.

Function

Relationships, work, education, sleep, responsibilities, finances and daily activities.

Assessment principle

The source recommends a comprehensive intake plus targeted assessment of Internet-triggered problems. Two structured tools are proposed: the Internet Dependency Intake Evaluation and the Internet Addiction Impairment Index.

10 · Structured intake

Internet Dependency Intake Evaluation

The source's intake form is designed for outpatient and inpatient settings and covers Internet usage, impulse control, cravings/withdrawal, concealment, impairment and prior treatment.

A. Internet usage profile
  1. When did Internet use begin?
  2. How many hours per week are currently spent online for non-essential purposes?
  3. Which applications/sites are used?
  4. Which specific sites, newsgroups or areas are visited?
  5. How many hours are spent on each application?
  6. Rank applications by importance.
  7. What is most liked about each application?
  8. If recall is difficult, use a prospective usage log for the following week.
EmailChat / IRCGames / MUDsNewsgroupsE-auctionsE-brokeragesVirtual casinosAdult websitesGeneral sitesOther
B. Antecedent → affect/belief → consequence analysis

For each application, identify:

  • Antecedent: what happened immediately before logging on—e.g., relationship conflict, depressed mood, work stress or poor academic performance.
  • Affect/belief: what the person felt after going online and how they justified the behavior.
  • Consequence: what negative result followed—e.g., more family anger, return of distress after logging off, persistent work problems or academic consequences.
C. Craving and withdrawal-like experiences
  • Preoccupation with the Internet.
  • Past attempts to control, reduce or stop.
  • Frequency of thoughts about going online.
  • Frequency of talking about going online.
  • Planning online use.
  • Forgoing responsibilities to go online.
  • Emotional/physical reactions when access is unavailable.
D. Affective response when access is blocked

The source asks clinicians to record whether the following occur when Internet access is unavailable or prohibited, together with the application involved and the date/time:

AnxietyDepressionMood swingsIrritabilityInsomniaPanic attacksRestlessness
E. Escape motives

Assess whether Internet use has been used to escape:

HelplessnessGuiltSadnessDepressionLonelinessRemorseAngerHopelessness

Also ask whether Internet use has been used to escape situational difficulties, and obtain examples.

F. Control attempts and abstinence history
  • Date of first attempt to control/cut down/stop.
  • Date of most recent attempt.
  • Longest period of abstinence from the Internet.
G. Concealment and impairment
  • Has the person been honest about the habit with friends, family, therapists or others?
  • Has an online persona or multiple online identities been created?
  • Which online activities were kept secret or believed others would disapprove of?
  • Have online friends disrupted significant real-life relationships?
  • Which relationships were affected and how?
  • Has Internet use disrupted work or job performance?
  • What other areas of life have been affected?
H. Prior treatment
  • Previous counseling for Internet addiction.
  • Timing and perceived effectiveness.
  • Previous support-group participation and effectiveness.
  • Current medications.

How the source recommends using the score
The higher the score, the greater the level of addiction/Internet-related problems in the source's framework. After categorizing the total score, inspect individual items scored 4 or 5 to identify the specific domains requiring intervention.
Item-level interpretation
The manual gives examples such as severe sleep loss from late-night Internet use and fear of life without the Internet. The score is therefore intended to be followed by qualitative clinical review, not treated as a substitute for formulation.
12 · Treatment framework

Moderation of Internet use + abstinence from the trigger application

Central treatment principle
Because Internet use is legitimate for work, banking, communication and other ordinary activities, the source argues that total Internet abstinence is usually impractical. The main goal is controlled/moderated Internet use, with abstinence from the specific problematic application when moderation of that application repeatedly fails.
1Map use

Identify applications, times, settings and triggers.

2Control behavior

Use structured behavioral techniques.

3Treat drivers

Address comorbid and psychosocial problems.

4Prevent relapse

Build alternatives, support and high-risk plans.

The manual combines behavioral exercises with psychotherapy approaches to address the online behavior and associated psychosocial problems such as social phobia, mood disorders, marital dissatisfaction, job burnout and childhood sexual abuse.

Behavioral control

Practicing the opposite, external stoppers, scheduled time limits, task priorities, reminder cards and personal inventories.

Underlying problems

Psychiatric illness, interpersonal difficulties, multiple addictions, marital problems and occupational burnout.

Support

Individual therapy, interpersonal work, insight-oriented therapy, support groups and couples counseling when indicated.

13 · Initial recovery techniques

Six structured behavioral interventions

1. Practicing the Opposite

First map the person's current routine: days used, start time, session duration and location. Then deliberately reorganize the schedule to disrupt the established habit.

  • Replace an automatic morning log-in with shower/breakfast first.
  • Delay a habitual evening session until after dinner or another planned activity.
  • Change the days on which the Internet is used when appropriate.
  • Introduce regular breaks.
  • Change the usual physical location of use.
2. External Stoppers

Use concrete external events as stop cues. When natural cues are ignored, the source suggests an alarm clock or timer placed near the computer. The preset signal marks the end of the planned session.

3. Set Time Limits

Replace vague intentions (“I will use less”) with a measurable schedule. Determine a reasonable weekly target, allocate the time into specific calendar slots and make the schedule visible.

The source illustrates the principle with scheduled evening and weekend blocks and emphasizes that a tangible schedule restores a sense of control.

4. Set Task Priorities

Before each legitimate Internet session, write down exactly what needs to be completed and where it will be done online.

  • Improves adherence to the predetermined time limit.
  • Reduces random surfing and exposure to temptations.
  • Supports relapse prevention.
5. Reminder Cards

Create two parallel lists: the five major problems caused by the addiction and the five major benefits expected from reducing use or abstaining from the problematic application.

Keep the list on a small card at the workstation or in a wallet/pocket and review it at choice points when the urge to use appears.

Problems
Relationship conflict · family conflict · reduced productivity · financial/occupational consequences · possible job loss
Benefits
More intimacy · greater attention to family · improved relationships · better work performance · occupational gains
6. Personal Inventory

Identify activities that were reduced or abandoned because of excessive Internet use—exercise, hobbies, dating, socializing, volunteering, visiting friends or other valued activities.

Rank each neglected activity as very important, important or not very important. Explore how the “very important” activities previously improved quality of life and actively rekindle them.

Behavioral principle
The aim is not simply to remove screen time. It is to change the cue–behavior–consequence pattern and restore rewarding offline activities that were displaced by compulsive online use.
14 · Advanced recovery

Treat what the Internet behavior is doing for the person

Advanced techniques target the underlying factors maintaining Internet addiction. The manual specifically discusses clinical depression, multiple addiction, psychiatric illness, marital discord, social phobia and job burnout.

Reality Therapy

The source draws on Glasser's model of basic needs—belonging, power, freedom, fun and survival. Therapy aims to identify responsible ways of meeting needs, using role-play, systematic planning and exploration of wants, needs and perceptions.

Interpersonal Therapy

Focuses on limited social support, interpersonal deficits, role disputes and role transitions. Techniques include affect encouragement, communication analysis, modeling and role-play.

Insight-oriented therapy

Particularly relevant when compulsive Internet use is part of a broader addictive pattern. The aim is to understand deeper drivers of chronic compulsivity and identify warning signs that sobriety/recovery is at risk.

Warning signs that recovery is becoming vulnerable

  • Going online when no one else is home.
  • Using work Internet access for non-work activity.
  • Keeping Internet use secret.
  • Losing sleep or rest because of Internet use.
  • Skipping support-group meetings or therapy.
Expected adjustment
The manual states that early recovery may involve missing the Internet and feeling the loss of a previously pleasurable activity. This is presented as an expected adjustment challenge rather than evidence that recovery is failing.
15 · Support systems

Social recovery and relationship repair

Twelve-Step support groups

The source recommends appropriate support-group participation as a way to build offline relationships, increase social support, reality-test interpersonal behavior and process relapse and triggers. The model may incorporate a Higher Power, which the manual notes need not necessarily be interpreted as religious. Sponsorship is described as an accountability/support mechanism.

Couples counseling

Couples work is presented as particularly relevant when Internet addiction has disrupted marital or family relationships, including situations involving virtual adultery or online infidelity.

Four goals for couples work

  1. Educate the couple about the addictive potential of Internet use.
  2. Reduce blame directed at the person with the addictive behavior.
  3. Improve communication about pre-existing family problems contributing to the pattern.
  4. Enable the partner to provide supportive accountability during high-risk moments.

Pharmacological assessment

The source does not present a specific medication for Internet/cybersexual addiction. Instead, it recommends assessment for pharmacological treatment of untreated underlying psychiatric disorders.

16 · Recovery checklist

Seven domains of recovery

  1. Assess current Internet-use practices.
    • Examine the full use cycle and associated problems.
    • Identify problematic applications and triggers.
    • Identify situations in which vulnerability is greatest.
  2. Make measurable behavioral changes.
    • Maintain a log of changes in Internet use.
    • Consider more public/visible locations for Internet use.
    • Use technology-based strategies to maintain abstinence from cybersex when cravings occur.
  3. Plan for abstinence/restriction.
    • Identify emotional and physical difficulties expected during restraint.
    • Make proactive lifestyle changes that reduce time around the computer.
    • Address the underlying drivers of the addiction.
  4. Understand the sexual needs served by the behavior.
    • Identify unmet needs previously filled by cybersex.
    • Develop alternative coping strategies.
    • Transfer positive qualities into healthier sexual outlets.
    • Explore unresolved sexual feelings before they become relapse triggers.
  5. Develop a proactive high-risk plan.
    • Avoid predictable high-risk situations.
    • Plan how unavoidable situations will be handled.
    • Minimize deliberate exposure to cybersex-related triggers.
    • Prepare for accidental exposure.
  6. Correct relapse rationalizations.
    • Challenge “I already slipped, so I might as well continue” thinking.
    • Watch for entitlement thoughts such as “I had a hard day, I deserve this.”
    • Treat slips as isolated learning opportunities rather than proof of failure.
  7. Build sponsorship and continuing support.
    • Identify how family/friends can help.
    • Repair old relationships and develop new ones.
    • Establish accountability rather than attempting recovery entirely alone.
17 · Outcome markers

Signs of healthier Internet / cybersexual use

The manual ends by encouraging patients to recognize progress even when setbacks occur. Because legitimate computer use may remain necessary, improvement may be more visible in behavior and relationships than in simple total screen-time elimination.

  1. Sticking to the planned Internet schedule and weekly limit.
  2. Close others noticing meaningful improvement in Internet habits and interpersonal behavior.
  3. Keeping online-service spending within budget.
  4. Returning to timely work performance resembling the pre-addiction pattern.
  5. Rediscovering previously enjoyed hobbies and activities.
  1. Investing more attention in people physically present than in strangers online.
  2. Recognizing problematic Internet use in oneself and others more realistically.
  3. Feeling progressively less tempted to resume old habits during legitimate or limited entertainment use.
  4. Wanting more offline social and family interaction and accepting more invitations.
  5. Looking back on the addictive period as a distinct earlier phase rather than one's current way of living.
Recovery marker
Healthy recovery is represented by regained control, restored offline relationships and activities, improved functioning, reduced cue-driven temptation and a sustainable relationship with legitimate Internet use.
18 · High-yield clinical synthesis

Practical formulation for psychiatry

Ask

What application is problematic? When does use occur? How long? What precedes it? What does the person feel immediately afterward? What does it cost them?

Formulate

Map the cycle: trigger → urge → online behavior → short-term reward/escape → consequence → distress → renewed urge.

Intervene

Control the trigger application, structure Internet use, rebuild offline reinforcement, treat comorbidity, strengthen relationships and plan for relapse.

Avoid a simplistic formulation
The source does not reduce the problem to pornography exposure alone. Its formulation repeatedly returns to reinforcement, emotional escape, cognitive patterns, interpersonal difficulties, life dissatisfaction, functional impairment and co-occurring psychiatric/addictive problems.

One-page algorithm

01Detect

Ask about Internet use routinely.

02Specify

Identify cybersexual/pornographic application and triggers.

03Measure

Assess loss of control, impairment and affective response.

04Treat

Moderate Internet use + abstain from the trigger + address drivers.

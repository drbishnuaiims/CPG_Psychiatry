1. Definition & clinical frame

START HERE

Diagnostic pattern

  • Difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or early-morning awakening.
  • Occurs despite adequate opportunity for sleep.
  • At least 3 nights/week.
  • Associated with clinically significant daytime distress/impairment.
  • ≥1 month and <3 months = episodic; ≥3 months = persistent insomnia.

Do not frame it as simply “secondary insomnia”

Insomnia can coexist with psychiatric and medical disorders; it should not be reduced to a symptom of the associated condition.

Core treatment concept: insomnia is associated with hyperarousal. Treatment therefore targets sleep-related hyperarousal, dysfunctional beliefs and maladaptive sleep behaviours.

2. OPD assessment: a 5-minute structured screen

DO NOT SKIP

A. Characterise the night

B. Characterise the day

C. Predisposing → precipitating → perpetuating

  • Predisposing: vulnerability to insomnia.
  • Precipitating: recent stressor or illness.
  • Perpetuating: dysfunctional beliefs, excessive time in bed, compensatory behaviours, screens while awake, altered daytime behaviour, caffeine/smoking patterns.

The perpetuating factors are particularly useful because they tell you what to target in CBT-I.

D. Medication / substance / environment audit

  • Every current medication: does it alter sleep?
  • Alcohol, caffeine, nicotine and other substances: type, timing, frequency, intoxication/withdrawal effects.
  • Bedroom/environmental factors interfering with sleep.
  • Pre-bedtime behaviour and mental state.
  • Behaviour and thoughts during nocturnal awakenings.

3. Rule out insomnia mimics before prescribing a hypnotic

HIGH-YIELD

Sleep-related breathing

  • Obstructive sleep apnea
  • Central sleep apnea

Movement disorders

  • Restless legs syndrome
  • Nocturnal myoclonus / sleep starts
  • Leg cramps
  • Bruxism

Circadian disorders

  • Delayed sleep-wake phase
  • Advanced sleep-wake phase
  • Shift-work disorder
  • Irregular sleep-wake rhythm

Parasomnias / nocturnal phenomena

  • Sleepwalking
  • Sleep terrors
  • Nightmare disorder
  • Abnormal activity at night → consider seizures / parasomnia

Medical / neurological contributors

  • Cardiac: arrhythmias, heart failure, myocardial infarction, varicose veins
  • Pulmonary: COPD, interstitial lung disease, scoliosis
  • GI: peptic ulcer disease, GERD, chronic constipation
  • CNS: dementia, seizures, stroke, Parkinson disease, intracranial lesions
  • GU: CKD, BPH
  • Musculoskeletal: rheumatoid arthritis, connective-tissue disease
Clinical rule: if a sleep disorder mimicking insomnia is identified, treat that disorder. If insomnia is comorbid with it, treat both. Do not simply escalate sedatives.

4. Phenotype + formulation

OPD DECISION

Initial / sleep-onset

  • Predominant difficulty initiating sleep.
  • Consider clinically significant sleep-onset latency >30 min on >3 nights/week.

Middle / maintenance

  • Repeated nocturnal awakenings.
  • Prolonged wakefulness after sleep onset.

Terminal / early awakening

  • Early-morning awakening with reduced total sleep time.
  • Exclude advanced sleep-wake phase disorder if sleep is otherwise adequate at the patient's natural timing.

Use a sleep diary before assuming “insomnia”

A sleep diary can distinguish insomnia from circadian misalignment. A patient may report a long sleep-onset latency yet obtain normal sleep when allowed to follow their preferred delayed schedule.

If the patient sleeps adequately when allowed to sleep at their preferred timing, reconsider insomnia and assess circadian misalignment.

5. CBT-I: the mainstay for chronic insomnia

CORE TREATMENT

Core CBT-I components

Sleep educationSleep hygiene Dysfunctional-belief workStimulus control Sleep restrictionRelaxation

Common objective: reduce hyperarousal through education, cognitive restructuring and relaxation.

What to tell the patient

  • Do not chase sleep by spending excessive time in bed.
  • Do not turn the bed into a place for prolonged worrying or wakeful activity.
  • Track the actual sleep pattern rather than relying only on a subjective impression.
  • Expect CBT-I to take longer than a sedative to show its full effect; benefits may be longer lasting and relapse may be reduced.

Stimulus control

  • Break the learned association between bed and wakefulness.
  • Use the sleep diary to review adherence and response.

Sleep restriction

  • Reduce excessive time in bed to consolidate sleep.
  • Caution: avoid in bipolar disorder and epilepsy because sleep restriction may worsen the comorbid condition.

Relaxation / problem solving

  • Use relaxation techniques to reduce hyperarousal.
  • Add problem-solving when daytime worries are prominent.
  • Mindfulness-based stress reduction can be incorporated as a CBT-I component.

Exercise + sleep hygiene

  • Aerobic exercise plus sleep hygiene reduced sleep-onset time and improved sleep efficiency in trials.
  • Do not impose a blanket ban on exercise before bedtime.
  • Assess the individual patient's sleep reactivity to exercise.

Sleep hygiene alone is not enough

  • Sleep education / sleep hygiene alone has limited value.
  • Combine it with active CBT-I components.
  • Do not present “sleep hygiene” as the complete treatment.

6. Pharmacotherapy: use strategically, not reflexively

SHORT-TERM FOCUS
Practical prescribing position: pharmacotherapy is mainly for short-term insomnia and is not routinely recommended for chronic insomnia. For chronic insomnia, CBT-I is preferred.

How to choose a drug

  1. Age and risks associated with sedation
  2. Psychiatric and medical comorbidity
  3. Pharmacokinetic properties
  4. Drug interactions
  5. Short- and long-term adverse effects
  6. Availability
  7. Cost
  8. Allergy

Benzodiazepines / BzRAs: phenotype-based principle

  • Short-acting: generally preferred when sleep initiation is the dominant problem.
  • Intermediate-acting: may be selected when sleep maintenance is the dominant problem.
  • Long-acting: may be selected for early-morning awakening.
  • Intermediate/long-acting agents can produce residual daytime effects and somnolence.

Sedating antidepressants

Trazodone, mirtazapine and tricyclics may be considered when there is a comorbid psychiatric disorder that warrants their use, or when benzodiazepine/BzRA adverse effects are not tolerated. The guideline notes insufficient evidence for their efficacy in insomnia.

Antihistamines

Commonly used for self-medication, but efficacy data are limited. Low-dose doxepin (10 mg) is described as primarily acting at H1 receptors.

Antipsychotics

Chlorpromazine, clozapine, olanzapine and quetiapine are described as off-label insomnia treatments, but efficacy data as hypnotics are lacking. Adverse effects include metabolic syndrome and extrapyramidal symptoms.

Prescribing safety: brand availability and price are procurement information, not a reason to choose a hypnotic. For chronic insomnia, the therapeutic decision remains driven by diagnosis, CBT-I, comorbidity, age, interaction burden, pharmacokinetics and adverse-effect profile.

7. Insomnia in the elderly

SAFETY FIRST

Elderly: key safety point

Pharmacotherapy should be used cautiously in elderly patients because of drug interactions and the adverse-effect profile.
  • Daytime sedation, cognitive problems, ataxia and falls can outweigh the benefit of “more sleep”.
  • Review the indication and risk–benefit balance before escalating sedatives.

Geriatric OPD safety screen

Why long-acting sedatives are problematic

  • Residual daytime effects are more likely with intermediate/long-acting agents.
  • Watch for memory lapses, daytime sleepiness, ataxia and falls.

Practical geriatric principle

  • First ask: “Why is this older patient awake?”
  • Look for medical disease, nocturia, pain, RLS, OSA, medication effects, circadian change and neurocognitive disorder.
  • Address reversible contributors before adding sedatives.

CBT-I feasibility

  • CBT-I may be less feasible with neurocognitive deficits or poor adherence.
  • Assess motivation and adapt the behavioural plan.
  • Use caregiver support when appropriate.
Important source limitation: the 2017 CPG gives a general “use pharmacotherapy cautiously in the elderly” statement but does not provide a dedicated geriatric drug hierarchy, geriatric dose table, or modern Beers/STOPP-START-based prescribing recommendations. Do not interpret this page as a geriatric prescribing guideline beyond what the CPG supports.

8. One-page treatment algorithm

CLINIC FLOW
1
Confirm insomnia phenotype
Adequate opportunity + nighttime sleep complaint + daytime symptoms/impairment + ≥3 nights/week.
2
Exclude mimics and contributors
OSA/CSA, RLS, parasomnia, seizures, circadian disorder, medical illness, substances, medications and environmental factors.
3
Determine duration
<3 months → short-term pattern. ≥3 months → chronic/persistent insomnia.
4
Start behavioural treatment
For chronic insomnia, CBT-I is the preferred treatment. Do not prescribe “sleep hygiene alone” as the entire intervention.
5
If medication is needed, choose deliberately
Match phenotype, age, comorbidity, pharmacokinetics, interactions and adverse-effect burden. Keep pharmacotherapy short-term when possible.
6
Review with sleep diary
Assess sleep-onset latency, awakenings, total sleep time, daytime symptoms, adherence and adverse effects.
7
Move toward CBT-I maintenance
When medication is used alongside CBT-I, taper gradually while maintaining CBT-I.
8
Escalate investigation if refractory
Use actigraphy when objective data are needed; for refractory chronic insomnia, consider video-synchronised 24-channel polysomnography.

9. Follow-up: what to measure

OUTCOME TRACKING

Night

  • Sleep-onset latency
  • Number/duration of awakenings
  • Wake time after sleep onset
  • Total sleep time
  • Sleep efficiency

Day

  • Fatigue vs sleepiness
  • Mood
  • Daytime functioning
  • Unintended sleep
  • Quality of sleep

Treatment

  • CBT-I adherence
  • Sleep-diary completion
  • Medication benefit
  • Residual sedation
  • Adverse effects / interactions

When to stop

  • For short-term insomnia, intermittent drug holidays may be used; restart treatment if symptoms recur.
  • CBT-I may be discontinued after sleep remains stable for ≥4 weeks.
  • The 4-week threshold is acknowledged as arbitrary.

10. OPD prescription checklist

COPY INTO CASE SHEET

Quick clinical pearls

REMEMBER

“More sedation” ≠ “better insomnia treatment”

The treatment target is improved sleep and daytime function with sustained benefit—not simply unconsciousness.

Chronic insomnia → think CBT-I first

CBT-I is the mainstay. Pharmacotherapy is not routinely recommended as the primary treatment for chronic insomnia.

Older patient + insomnia = investigate before sedating

Age increases the importance of interactions and adverse-effect burden. Use sedating medication cautiously and investigate reversible contributors first.

Use the sleep diary as your objective anchor

It helps identify sleep pattern, behavioural perpetuators and circadian misalignment, and provides a practical way to track response.

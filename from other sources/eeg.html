Understanding EEG

A practical, step-by-step guide to EEG waveforms, montages, normal and abnormal findings, seizures, EEG reports, artifacts, limitations and clinical correlation.

How to use this module

Think of EEG interpretation as a sequence of questions:

  1. What exactly is the EEG recording?
  2. Where are the electrodes and what does each channel represent?
  3. What does the normal background look like?
  4. How fast, how tall and how sharp are the waves?
  5. Does the patient remain awake or enter sleep?
  6. What happens during activation procedures?
  7. Are there epileptiform abnormalities?
  8. Is there an actual seizure?
  9. Could what we are seeing be artifact?
  10. How should all of this be translated into an EEG report?
  11. What can—and importantly, what cannot—the EEG tell us?
  12. How does the EEG fit with the clinical picture and imaging?
Core principle: EEG is not interpreted in isolation. The patient's clinical symptoms and the EEG findings have to be considered together.
01

What exactly is an EEG?

Start with the fundamental question: what is being measured?

Scalp electrodes recording electrical activity generated by neurons

Electroencephalogram = electrical activity of the brain

EEG stands for electroencephalogram.

The term encephalo / encephal refers to the brain.

EEG therefore records the electrical activity of the brain.

Two major things EEG can show:
  • Electrical activity during a seizure.
  • The brain's electrical background between seizures.

A seizure can be thought of as an abnormal burst of electrical activity, involving excessive or overly synchronous electrical activity.

Important: EEG is not only about finding seizures. The background brain activity between seizures can provide important diagnostic information.
EEG Electrical activity Neurons Seizure Background
Electrical activity travels from neurons to scalp electrodes

What is the EEG actually detecting?

The EEG electrodes sit on the surface of the scalp, but the electrical activity of interest originates much deeper, at the level of brain cells called neurons.

The electrical signal therefore has to pass through multiple structures before being detected by the scalp electrodes.

  • Skin
  • Skull / bone
  • Lining of the brain
  • Normal fluid-filled spaces / CSF
  • Blood vessels
  • Brain tissue containing neurons
Conceptual takeaway: EEG is a surface recording of electrical activity generated much deeper inside the brain.

This becomes extremely important later when understanding why a seizure can occasionally occur without producing a clear scalp EEG abnormality.

Squiggly Lines 101: How to read a waveform

Three basic characteristics are repeatedly assessed: frequency, amplitude and morphology.

Normal awake background

The three questions

  1. How fast are the waves? → Frequency
  2. How tall are the waves? → Amplitude
  3. What is their shape? → Morphology / sharpness
When looking at an EEG, constantly ask:

How fast?
How tall?
How sharp?
Posterior dominant alpha activity

Frequency: count the waves

Frequency describes how many waves occur in a second.

In the normal awake example, the occipital leads provide an easy place to count the waves.

The example contains approximately 11–12 waves per second.

This is approximately an 11 Hz alpha frequency.

Alpha frequency: approximately 8–13 Hz, represented in the example by a well-formed posterior rhythm.

Other frequency ranges include:

Pattern Relative speed
Beta Faster activity
Alpha Normal posterior awake rhythm in the example
Theta Slower activity
Delta Even slower activity
Frequency comparison

Amplitude and symmetry

Amplitude refers to the height of the waves.

One important comparison is between the left and right sides of the brain.

In a normal background, the amplitude is generally reasonably symmetric between the two sides.

Look for asymmetry: Does one side consistently look very different from the other?

In addition to frequency and amplitude, morphology matters.

A waveform may have a smooth appearance or may contain distinctly sharp or spiky components.

Sharp and spiky morphology

What does "spiky" actually mean?

When EEG readers describe a waveform as sharp or spiky, they are describing its morphology.

Literally imagine something that looks pointed, sharp and pokey.

Sharp waves and spikes can be abnormal epileptiform findings, but a sharp-looking waveform is not automatically a seizure.
04

Normal awake EEG

Establish the background before looking for abnormalities.

Awake, relaxed background

What does a normal relaxed EEG look like?

A patient who is awake, relaxed and lying still should have an organized background.

The posterior region is particularly useful for appreciating the normal alpha rhythm.

  • Reasonably organized background
  • Posterior alpha activity
  • Symmetry between hemispheres
  • No prominent pathological spikes or sharps
The background is not merely "filler". It is itself an important part of EEG interpretation.
05

How the EEG changes during sleep

Normal sleep produces major changes in the EEG background. Some normal sleep waveforms can even look sharp.

Transition from wakefulness to drowsiness

Drowsiness

As the patient becomes drowsy, the EEG becomes calmer and less active.

The patient stops blinking because the eyes are closing.

The EEG may develop a slower, undulating pattern corresponding to roving eye movements.

Do not overcall sleep: Some waveforms that appear sharp during sleep are normal sleep patterns.
Deeper / slow-wave sleep

Deeper sleep

As sleep becomes deeper, the background becomes progressively slower.

Slow-wave sleep is characterized by prominent slow activity.

These are normal physiological sleep patterns.

REM sleep with rapid eye movements

REM sleep

During REM sleep (rapid eye movement sleep), the EEG begins to look more like wakefulness or drowsiness.

Some apparently spiky-looking activity in the frontal leads may actually represent rapid eye movements.

Normal EEG changes dramatically across sleep stages. Interpretation therefore requires knowing whether the patient is awake, drowsy or asleep.
06

Activation procedures

EEG is not always recorded passively. Certain procedures deliberately challenge the brain's electrical system.

Flashing-light / photic stimulation

Photic stimulation

Photic stimulation is the flashing-light test.

In the example, the lights flash at approximately 6 times per second.

A normal response is for the occipital EEG activity to show a rhythm that follows or matches the frequency of the flashing light.

This is a normal photic driving response.

Why perform photic stimulation?

An EEG may appear otherwise normal, but flashing lights can sometimes provoke epileptiform activity.

It may reveal:

  • Abnormal sharp/spiky activity
  • Photosensitivity
  • Electrical activity associated with seizures
  • Markers suggesting increased seizure risk
The flashing light test therefore serves as an activation procedure designed to expose abnormalities that may not be obvious in the resting EEG.
Hyperventilation / deep breathing

Hyperventilation

Hyperventilation is another activation procedure.

  • Younger children may blow on a pinwheel.
  • Older children can take deep breaths for approximately two minutes.

During hyperventilation, the EEG can become substantially slower.

In the example, only around 3–4 waves per second are visible, corresponding to delta-range slowing.

This can be a normal response to hyperventilation.

The slowing is particularly reassuring when it is generalized and symmetric.

The important distinction is not simply "there is slowing".

Ask: Is the slowing generalized and symmetric, or is one side disproportionately slow?

The example contains no epileptiform spikes or sharps.

07

Epileptiform abnormalities

Spikes and sharp waves can indicate increased seizure risk.

Example of an epileptiform spike

What does "epileptiform" mean?

Epileptiform refers to EEG findings suggesting increased risk for seizures.

These findings represent abnormal electrical activity that could potentially develop into a seizure.

Epileptiform discharge ≠ seizure.

It is a marker of increased seizure risk, not necessarily an actual seizure occurring at that moment.

The most common epileptiform abnormalities discussed are:

  • Spikes
  • Sharp waves
Left frontal epileptiform discharge

Localizing an epileptiform discharge

The electrode naming convention can tell us where the abnormal activity is most prominent.

For example, an epileptiform spike that is best formed over the left frontal region suggests increased electrical activity in that region.

The right frontal region may look comparatively less sharp and more normal.

EEG localization provides a clue about where seizures may be originating, particularly when the finding is clearly focal.
Frequent right temporal sharp waves

Sleep can make epileptiform abnormalities more obvious

In the example, the patient is asleep.

Sharp waves appear repeatedly in the right temporal region.

They may occur every second or two.

Despite the frequency of these sharp waves, this is not itself a seizure.

It represents frequent evidence of increased electrical activity and increased seizure risk, particularly in the right temporal region.

Spike versus sharp wave

Spike vs sharp wave

The distinction is primarily descriptive.

Finding Traditional duration
Spike Less than 200 microseconds
Sharp wave More than 200 microseconds
Clinical point: The distinction between spike and sharp wave generally does not substantially change what it means for the patient or treatment.

The terminology is useful because it helps EEG specialists describe the waveform morphology to each other.

08

When does an epileptiform discharge become a seizure?

This is one of the most important distinctions in EEG interpretation.

Generalized ~3 Hz spike-wave seizure

A seizure is more than a spike

Spike waves can occur without an actual seizure.

A seizure occurs when abnormal electrical activity becomes sufficiently organized, rhythmic, persistent and clinically relevant.

Think:

Organized + rhythmic + sufficiently sustained electrical activity → seizure pattern

A seizure can occasionally be visible on EEG without obvious clinical symptoms, but there are specific criteria for interpreting such events.

EEG change occurring with behavioral arrest

Classic absence seizure example

The example represents a common seizure type in children: an absence seizure.

Clinically, the patient suddenly:

  • Stops
  • Stares
  • Pauses activity
  • May show subtle behavioral changes

On EEG, the normal background suddenly changes to a highly rhythmic spike-wave pattern.

The example is approximately 3 waves per second.

The pattern lasts for several seconds and then ends, followed by return to the normal background and clinical alertness.

The EEG change and the clinical behavioral change occur together.
Evolution of seizure activity

Evolution is a key feature of seizures

EEG readers often use the term evolve.

A seizure does not simply remain a static isolated spike. It may:

  • Spread into additional electrodes / regions
  • Become faster or slower
  • Become more rhythmic
  • Change morphology
  • Develop a progressively different waveform pattern
Static sharp wave: may represent an interictal epileptiform discharge.

Rhythmic evolving activity: raises concern for an ictal seizure pattern.
EEG severity and clinical severity are not always proportional

EEG appearance does not always equal clinical severity

A very dramatic EEG can occur with relatively subtle clinical symptoms.

Conversely, a clinically concerning event may not necessarily produce a dramatic scalp EEG pattern.

How "scary" an EEG looks does not always correspond directly to how severe the patient's symptoms are.
09

How to understand an EEG report

The raw EEG is translated into a standardized written report.

Structure of an EEG report

1. Background

The report usually begins by describing the EEG background.

The reader asks:

  • Is the background organized?
  • Is the rhythm normal?
  • Is there appropriate symmetry?
  • Does one side look different?
  • Is there generalized slowing?
The first part of the report describes what the EEG looks like before focusing on abnormalities .
Abnormal findings and localization

2. Abnormal findings

After the background, the report describes what stands out as abnormal.

This may include:

  • Spikes
  • Sharp waves
  • Other epileptiform discharges
  • Focal slowing
  • Generalized slowing
  • Other abnormalities

The report should describe where abnormalities occur.

Activation procedures

3. Activation procedures

The report may describe the response to:

  • Photic stimulation
  • Hyperventilation
ECG recorded simultaneously with EEG

4. ECG / cardiac rhythm

A single-lead ECG is commonly recorded simultaneously.

This provides information about the heart rhythm during the EEG.

It can also be extremely useful when determining whether an apparent EEG waveform may actually represent a pulse artifact.

Clinical interpretation

5. Interpretation / clinical correlation

After the technical description, the report provides a summary or interpretation.

It generally addresses:

  • Is the EEG normal or abnormal?
  • Was the patient awake or asleep?
  • What did the background look like?
  • Were there epileptiform abnormalities?
  • Where were they located?
  • Is there evidence suggesting increased seizure risk?
Clinical correlation is critical. The technical EEG language is primarily designed for communication between epilepsy specialists and neurophysiology professionals.
10

What EEG cannot tell you

A major part of good EEG interpretation is understanding its limitations.

A normal routine EEG does not exclude epilepsy

Normal EEG does NOT rule out epilepsy

This is one of the most important clinical points.

A normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy.

A patient with definite, documented epilepsy can have a portion of their EEG that looks completely normal.

This is especially relevant when the EEG is short.

A routine EEG may only last approximately 30–60 minutes.

A person with epilepsy can certainly go 30–60 minutes without showing an abnormality.

A routine EEG is therefore a useful screening / initial investigation, but its sensitivity is limited by the duration and nature of the recording.
Abnormal EEG does not automatically equal epilepsy

Abnormal EEG does NOT automatically mean epilepsy

A person can have spikes or sharp waves suggesting increased seizure risk without ever having a clinical seizure.

Do not diagnose or treat solely from the EEG.

In most circumstances, treatment decisions are based on the EEG together with the clinical presentation.

Scalp EEG samples electrical activity indirectly

Why can EEG miss abnormalities?

Remember the basic anatomy: the electrodes are on the scalp, while the electrical activity is generated deep inside the brain.

EEG may miss activity because:

  • The electrical activity is too deep.
  • The affected region is too small.
  • The electrical signal does not propagate strongly enough to the scalp.
  • The abnormal activity occurs too infrequently to be captured during the recording.
  • Recording duration may simply be insufficient.
Small deep seizure potentially missed by scalp electrodes

Even an actual seizure may occasionally be invisible on scalp EEG

The brain contains many folds and deep crevices.

If seizure activity remains extremely small and confined to a deep fold, its electrical signal may not reach the surface electrodes sufficiently to be detected.

"Normal scalp EEG during an event" does not automatically prove that the event is non-epileptic.

Clinical characterization of the event therefore remains essential.

11

EEG artifacts: things that mimic abnormalities

Not every sharp-looking or rhythmic waveform comes from the brain.

Frontal eye-blink artifact

Eye-blink artifact

The eyeballs themselves have an electrical field.

When the eyes blink and roll upward, this electrical activity is detected by the frontal electrodes.

The result can be large sharp deflections in the frontal leads.

Experienced EEG readers recognize this characteristic pattern as an eye-blink artifact rather than epileptiform activity.

The remainder of the EEG background may remain normal.

Pulse artifact synchronized with heartbeat

Pulse artifact

Another fascinating artifact occurs when an EEG electrode picks up the effects associated with blood pulsing through nearby vessels.

It can appear:

  • Rhythmic
  • Sharp-looking
  • Potentially seizure-like

However, the waveform may line up perfectly with the heartbeat on the simultaneous ECG channel.

If an apparent rhythmic EEG abnormality is synchronized with the ECG, consider pulse artifact.

Repositioning the head/electrode may sometimes reduce or eliminate the artifact.

Common sources of non-cerebral EEG signal

Why artifacts matter

EEG is an electrical recording, so the electrodes can pick up signals that are not generated by pathological brain activity.

Potential influences include:

  • Eye movements / blinking
  • Muscle activity
  • Cardiac pulsation
  • Electrode-related effects
  • Skull defects
  • Extra fluid
  • Blood vessels
The EEG reader must distinguish true cerebral abnormalities from artifacts introduced by the recording environment and anatomy.
12

What can we actually do with EEG information?

Despite its limitations, EEG provides clinically valuable information.

EEG as part of initial epilepsy evaluation

1. Initial diagnosis of epilepsy

Consider someone who has experienced their first seizure-like event.

The event might instead represent:

  • A true epileptic seizure
  • Syncope / fainting
  • An abnormal movement
  • Another non-epileptic event

EEG can provide evidence of increased seizure risk through epileptiform abnormalities.

Clinical event + EEG evidence of increased seizure risk can strengthen the diagnosis of epilepsy and help guide management.
Focal versus generalized electrical activity

2. Help determine seizure / epilepsy type

EEG can provide clues regarding whether the electrical abnormality is:

Focal Activity begins in or is most prominent in one region.
Generalized Abnormal activity involves the brain more broadly at the same time.

For example, focal right temporal or frontal sharp waves may suggest a focal epilepsy.

Generalized approximately 3-Hz spike-wave activity is characteristic of the absence-seizure example discussed earlier.

EEG in longitudinal epilepsy management

3. Follow-up after treatment

Repeat EEGs may be performed after epilepsy treatment has started.

The timing is individualized depending on:

  • Type of epilepsy
  • Response to treatment
  • Clinical course
  • Other circumstances

EEG can potentially show whether electrical abnormalities appear more active or less active.

It may also identify subtle electrographic seizures that are not clinically obvious.

The patient's clinical response remains more important than EEG normalization alone. Fewer seizures, improved function and tolerability of treatment remain central clinical outcomes.
Localizing the epileptogenic region

4. Localizing seizure onset

If seizures are focal, an important question becomes:

Where are the seizures coming from?

This becomes particularly important when medications are no longer adequately controlling seizures.

If a consistent seizure-producing region can be identified and it is safe to remove, surgery may become a treatment option.

In carefully selected cases, removal of the epileptogenic region can potentially cure the epilepsy.

13

EEG + MRI + PET: localization becomes powerful

EEG can become much more informative when several independent modalities point toward the same brain region.

Subtle right temporal asymmetry

MRI correlation

Consider the example with frequent right temporal sharp waves.

MRI may show a subtle structural asymmetry in the same region.

The deep right temporal region may appear:

  • A little larger
  • Somewhat chunkier
  • Brighter compared with the opposite side

Such differences can be subtle enough that they may not be immediately obvious even to a trained observer.

The finding becomes much more meaningful when it matches the EEG localization.
PET showing reduced glucose metabolism

PET scan: looking at brain metabolism

PET provides a different type of information.

It can assess the brain's resting metabolic activity.

The brain requires substantial energy even when it is simply resting, and this energy is largely derived from glucose.

Areas with reduced metabolic activity may therefore show reduced glucose uptake.

In the example, the right temporal region shows reduced glucose uptake compared with the opposite side.

This can provide another clue regarding where seizures may be originating.

EEG + MRI + PET convergence

Convergence of evidence

In the illustrative case, three findings point toward the same region:

  1. EEG: excessive electrical activity in the right temporal region.
  2. MRI: subtle structural asymmetry in the right temporal region.
  3. PET: reduced glucose metabolism in that same region.
When EEG, structural imaging and metabolic imaging converge on the same region, localization becomes considerably more compelling.

If the region is determined to be safely removable, this information can help guide epilepsy surgery.

PET is not always abnormal in epilepsy. The example represents a particularly useful case rather than a universal pattern.
14

Clinical events with no EEG correlate: epilepsy vs PNES

Video-EEG is particularly valuable when the clinical event and EEG need to be correlated in real time.

Simultaneous video and EEG recording

Why video-EEG matters

The most useful tool for evaluating an event is often simultaneous:

  • Clinical observation
  • Video recording
  • EEG recording

This allows the clinician to ask:

What is the patient doing clinically at the exact moment the EEG changes?

This temporal correlation is critical when evaluating possible epileptic and non-epileptic events.

Clinical patterns expected to produce scalp EEG changes

When a normal scalp EEG during an event is highly informative

Some clinical patterns are expected to produce a detectable scalp EEG abnormality.

For example, if a patient has bilateral rhythmic jerking with tonic-clonic movements , one would generally expect a corresponding surface EEG change.

If the scalp EEG remains completely normal during such an event, confidence increases that the event is non-epileptic.

Such events may be termed psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES) or non-epileptic spells.

However, this reasoning is not applicable in the same way to every subtle clinical event, because small deep seizures can occasionally escape scalp EEG detection.
Small focal events may be difficult to classify

The difficult cases

The problem becomes more complicated when the clinical symptoms are subtle.

Examples include:

  • A strange sensation / aura
  • Brief staring
  • Lip smacking
  • Subtle behavioral change

A small seizure could theoretically remain confined to a small deep region and fail to produce a clear surface EEG correlate.

The clinical pattern therefore has to be considered alongside the EEG rather than simply saying "no EEG change = PNES."
Prolonged monitoring can capture multiple event types

Why prolonged monitoring helps

Longer recordings increase the opportunity to capture events and observe whether an initially subtle event eventually develops a detectable EEG pattern.

Monitoring may extend to:

  • Several hours
  • Overnight recordings
  • Several days
  • Approximately seven days in some situations

A patient may have multiple event types: one type may remain too small to detect, while another may spread and become clearly visible on scalp EEG.

Longer monitoring can therefore help capture both the clinical event and its electrical evolution.
15

Interictal EEG: what happens between seizures?

EEG abnormalities outside an actual seizure can still carry important clinical information.

Interictal epileptiform discharges

Interictal epileptiform discharges

"Interictal" means occurring between seizures.

Spikes and sharp waves between seizures can indicate an increased tendency toward seizures.

Interictal epileptiform discharges are markers of seizure risk, not seizures themselves.
Diffuse slowing / encephalopathy pattern

Diffuse background slowing

Sometimes the EEG does not primarily show spikes and sharps.

Instead, the entire brain may appear too slow.

There may be excessive theta and delta activity and insufficient normal alpha activity.

Technical terminology used in the report may include:

  • Encephalopathy
  • Diffuse cerebral dysfunction

Such findings can fit with a clinical picture involving global developmental problems, including developmental delay or autism spectrum disorder.

The EEG finding itself does not necessarily establish that one condition is causing another. It may instead be consistent with the broader clinical picture.
Focal slowing and structural correlation

Focal slowing

If one region of the brain is slower than the other, this is called focal slowing.

For example, the right hemisphere may show substantially slower waves than the left.

Focal slowing suggests the possibility of a structural abnormality in that region.

Causes can include:

  • Tumor
  • Previous brain injury
  • Scarring
  • Other structural abnormalities

Therefore, focal slowing generally prompts consideration of MRI brain imaging.

Markedly increased spike activity during sleep

Extreme sleep activation: when spikes themselves may cause symptoms

A particularly interesting phenomenon occurs when epileptiform discharges become dramatically more frequent during sleep.

The EEG may look relatively acceptable during wakefulness, but after the patient falls asleep, there may be continuous or near-continuous spiking.

This is the type of phenomenon discussed in relation to ESES / sleep-activated epileptiform activity.

In some cases, excessive sleep-related epileptiform activity can be associated with:

  • Language regression
  • Other developmental regression
  • Clinical cognitive / behavioral consequences
This represents a relatively unusual situation where the EEG abnormality itself may become a treatment target.

The concern is that the enormous amount of epileptiform activity during sleep may itself contribute to clinical symptoms even though it is not necessarily producing conventional clinical seizures.

16

When a patient's event may or may not appear on EEG

Clinical correlation becomes especially important for subtle symptoms.

Marking subtle clinical events during monitoring

Why the event button matters

If a patient or family member notices a subtle event that may represent a seizure, it is useful to mark the time of the event.

Examples include:

  • Lip quivering
  • Tongue movements
  • Zoning out
  • Brief behavioral change
Marking the event allows the EEG reader to go back to that exact moment and determine whether there is an electrical correlate.

This can be particularly important because a subtle clinical event may be impossible to identify reliably by visually scanning hours of EEG without a time marker.

Events range from subtle focal symptoms to obvious seizures

There is a huge spectrum

EEG abnormalities can range from:

  • A tiny focal seizure / aura
  • A subtle staring episode
  • Lip smacking
  • More obvious focal seizures
  • Generalized seizures with striking EEG changes

The electrical appearance and the clinical appearance do not always scale together.

How long does it take to read an EEG?

Interpretation time depends heavily on the duration and complexity of the recording.

From routine EEG to prolonged monitoring

Recording duration determines workload

Recording Approximate reading burden
Normal routine EEG May take only a few minutes to interpret
Routine EEG with uncertainty May take longer because normal variants and artifacts need careful assessment
Several-hour EEG May take substantially longer, especially if events or abnormalities occur
~12-hour segment Can require at least approximately 30 minutes per patient in the example workflow
Overnight / prolonged EEG Potentially much longer depending on abnormalities and events

A normal EEG is not necessarily always easy to read. A reader must avoid both:

  • Calling normal activity abnormal
  • Missing subtle abnormalities
Computer-assisted EEG interpretation

Can machine learning help?

Software can assist with:

  • Identifying spikes
  • Identifying sharp waves
  • Highlighting asymmetry
  • Drawing attention to potentially abnormal regions
Automated EEG detection is not perfect.

A practical workflow described in the transcript is for the EEG specialist to first review the EEG independently and then use software as an additional tool to investigate specific questions or double-check suspicious findings.

This is particularly important in children because pediatric EEGs can look substantially different from adult EEGs.

Some software has been developed predominantly using adult data and may therefore overcall normal pediatric sleep patterns as abnormal .

The EEG interpretation framework

A practical mental checklist for approaching an EEG.

1. Orientation Left vs right; frontal vs posterior.
2. State Awake, drowsy, NREM sleep or REM sleep?
3. Background Organization, rhythm and symmetry.
4. Frequency Alpha, beta, theta, delta.
5. Amplitude How tall are the waves?
6. Morphology Smooth vs sharp vs spiky.
7. Epileptiform activity Spikes and sharp waves.
8. Seizure? Rhythmic, organized, evolving activity?
9. Localization Where is the abnormality maximal?
10. Artifacts Eyes, muscle, pulse, electrodes, anatomy.
11. Activation Photic stimulation and hyperventilation.
12. Clinical correlation What was the patient actually doing?

The big picture

EEG interpretation is essentially the process of connecting electrical activity with anatomy, time, physiology and clinical behavior.

  • EEG records electrical activity generated by neurons, using electrodes placed on the scalp.
  • Always establish the montage and anatomical orientation first.
  • Evaluate the background before focusing on abnormalities.
  • Think in terms of frequency, amplitude and morphology.
  • Normal EEG changes substantially during drowsiness and sleep.
  • Photic stimulation and hyperventilation are activation procedures designed to expose abnormalities.
  • Spikes and sharp waves are epileptiform markers of increased seizure risk, not seizures by themselves.
  • A seizure generally demonstrates organized, rhythmic, evolving electrical activity and may have a clinical correlate.
  • A normal routine EEG does not rule out epilepsy.
  • An abnormal EEG does not automatically establish epilepsy.
  • Artifacts—including eye movements, muscle activity and pulse— can mimic abnormalities.
  • Clinical context is essential, particularly when an event has no obvious EEG correlate.
  • Video-EEG allows simultaneous assessment of clinical behavior and electrical activity.
  • Prolonged monitoring can improve the chance of capturing intermittent or subtle abnormalities.
  • Interictal abnormalities can provide useful information even when no seizure occurs during the recording.
  • Diffuse slowing may suggest diffuse cerebral dysfunction, while focal slowing may suggest a structural lesion.
  • EEG can help with diagnosis, classification, follow-up and seizure localization.
  • When EEG localization agrees with MRI and PET findings, the combined information can become particularly valuable in evaluating patients for epilepsy surgery.
The single most important principle: EEG findings must be interpreted in the context of what the patient is actually experiencing.

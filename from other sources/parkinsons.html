00 · Webinar context

What this session was trying to teach.

The opening discussion emphasised the unusually large overlap between psychiatry and neurology in Parkinson’s disease. The speaker said psychiatrists should be confident diagnosing Parkinsonism and should regard non-motor symptoms as a major area of psychiatric expertise.

THE SERIES

299th edition of Thursday Musing

The session was recorded for later viewing. The moderators framed it as a practical topic for psychiatrists because Parkinsonism can arise from psychiatric medications, while Parkinson’s itself can produce depression, anxiety, apathy, psychosis, cognitive symptoms and other psychiatric presentations.

HISTORY

James Parkinson and “Essay on the Shaking Palsy”

The speaker highlighted that James Parkinson was a general surgeon rather than a neurologist or psychiatrist. Of the six patients described in his essay, he personally saw three and observed three from his window walking on the street. The speaker also noted Parkinson’s interests in geology and political activity, using this as an example of how polymathic thinking can contribute to discovery.

DISCOVERY

How the name Parkinson’s disease emerged

The speaker noted that Parkinson himself called the illness “shaking palsy”, and that Jean-Martin Charcot later gave the disease the Parkinson name. He also used the historical relationship between neurology and psychiatry to argue for closer collaboration today.

SPEAKER

Movement-disorder perspective

The speaker described more than two decades of experience with Parkinson’s disease and movement disorders and deliberately chose an interactive format rather than a conventional review article.

01 · Clinical diagnosis

“If you know bradykinesia, you are already most of the way there.”

The speaker's central message was that Parkinson’s disease is primarily a clinical diagnosis and that psychiatrists should not be intimidated by it.

01

Bradykinesia ≠ just being slow

Ordinary slowness can occur with low mood, stroke and other motor-system disorders. Parkinsonian bradykinesia requires a progressive decrement in movement, particularly in amplitude and/or velocity during repetitive movement.

02

Finger tapping

Ask the patient to repeatedly tap the index/middle finger against the thumb, with the speaker suggesting roughly 25–30 repetitions. Look for progressive reduction in amplitude or speed rather than simply slow movement from the beginning.

03

Rest tremor

Typically a slow, visible rest tremor, often asymmetric and classically described as pill-rolling. Rest tremor is assessed with the limb relaxed on the lap or beside the chair.

04

Re-emergent tremor

Rest tremor disappears when the arms are held out, then returns after a few seconds, usually in the same limb and with similar frequency. The speaker described this as an especially strong clue to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease.

05

Rigidity

Rigidity is another core Parkinsonian sign. Clasp-knife is a description of spasticity/pyramidal involvement rather than typical Parkinsonian rigidity.

06

Gait and stance

The speaker emphasised that gait imbalance is generally later in idiopathic PD. A narrow stance was described as typical of true PD; a very wide stance should prompt consideration of ataxia or atypical/secondary Parkinsonism.

Bedside pearl: The speaker's shorthand was bradykinesia + rest tremor and/or rigidity = Parkinsonism. Modern diagnostic frameworks then add exclusions, red flags and supportive features to decide whether this is idiopathic PD.
02 · MDS framework

Core criteria, exclusions, red flags and support.

The webinar used the MDS clinical framework as a practical way to understand both diagnosis and differential diagnosis.

Core criterion

Bradykinesia plus either rest tremor or rigidity. The speaker contrasted this with older/UK Brain Bank-style descriptions in which gait impairment could appear in the definition of Parkinsonism.

Absolute exclusions

The speaker presented nine exclusion concepts as a memory aid for alternative diagnoses: clear cerebellar abnormality; vertical gaze palsy; cortical sensory loss/apraxia/aphasia; prolonged isolated lower-body involvement; clear FTD features; Parkinsonism attributable to dopamine-blocking exposure; poor levodopa response; normal dopamine-transporter imaging in the appropriate diagnostic setting; and another structural/neurological explanation such as basal-ganglia stroke or tumour.

Supporting features

Examples discussed included a clear response to levodopa, development of dyskinesia with levodopa and supportive ancillary investigations such as dopamine-transporter imaging.

Red flags

Rapid progression, prolonged lack of progression, early bulbar dysfunction, stridor, marked autonomic dysfunction, recurrent early falls, pyramidal signs and symmetrical Parkinsonism were highlighted as reasons to reconsider idiopathic PD.

Clinically established PD

The speaker summarised the conceptual requirement as core criteria, absence of exclusion criteria, adequate supportive features and no unresolved red flags; he also noted that supportive features can offset some red flags within the formal framework.

Why psychiatrists should know it

Knowing the exclusions gives a practical differential diagnosis rather than making the criteria a memorisation exercise.

Transcript fidelity: This page preserves the speaker’s teaching points and terminology rather than silently replacing them with an external guideline. Formal MDS criteria should be checked against the original criteria when making a diagnosis or conducting research.
03 · Atypical and secondary Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism is a syndrome. Not every case is idiopathic PD.

Progressive supranuclear palsy · PSP

Vertical gaze palsy, early falls—particularly backward—axial/truncal bradykinesia, frontal features, frontal furrowing and an applause sign were discussed. The speaker described PSP as a tauopathy. Neck extension and an upright/backward posture rather than the slight forward stoop of PD were also shown as clues.

Corticobasal degeneration / syndrome

Often markedly asymmetric, with apraxia and cortical sensory loss. The latter was explained as preserved basic touch/pain perception but impaired cortical discrimination—for example, difficulty identifying a number traced on the hand. CBD was also described as a tauopathy.

MSA

Autonomic dysfunction and ataxia are important clues. Stridor, pyramidal signs and other atypical features were also mentioned. MSA was placed with the synucleinopathies.

Dementia with Lewy bodies · DLB

Key clues discussed: early cognitive decline, fluctuating cognition, well-formed hallucinations and REM sleep behaviour disorder. Dementia before or within about one year of motor Parkinsonism was highlighted as a useful distinction from Parkinson disease dementia.

Vascular Parkinsonism

Often lower-body predominant, with preserved arm swing, wide-based short steps and vascular changes on MRI. The speaker associated it with small-vessel disease and periventricular hyperintensity.

Normal-pressure hydrocephalus · NPH

Wide-based, short-stepped, magnetic gait with the feet seeming stuck to the floor and predominantly lower-body involvement. The speaker demonstrated transient gait improvement after lumbar puncture and stressed that NPH is important because it is potentially treatable.

Drug-induced Parkinsonism

Often symmetric, but asymmetry and rest tremor do not exclude it. Drug exposure is essential history. Oro-lingual movements/tongue protrusion and dyskinesia before levodopa exposure were described as clues. Dopamine-blocking drugs can closely mimic idiopathic PD.

Other secondary causes

The webinar mentioned structural lesions such as tumour and stroke, and infections including tuberculosis, as possible secondary causes of Parkinsonism.

Functional / tremor mimics

Dopamine-transporter imaging was discussed as potentially useful in difficult cases where functional Parkinsonism or essential tremor is being considered against true presynaptic dopaminergic degeneration.

04 · Imaging

Use imaging to answer a specific question.

MRI: generally not diagnostic of PD

The speaker's clear practical answer was no: routine MRI does not usually diagnose idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. It is valuable for finding or supporting alternative diagnoses.

PSP: hummingbird appearance

Midbrain atrophy can produce a hummingbird-type appearance on sagittal imaging, supporting PSP in the appropriate clinical context.

MSA: hot-cross-bun sign

Brainstem/cerebellar pathway changes can produce the described hot-cross-bun sign, which can support MSA but does not diagnose idiopathic PD.

3T MRI: swallow-tail / nigrosome concept

The speaker discussed loss of the normal nigrosome-1 “swallow-tail” appearance on high-resolution 3T imaging as a possible supportive sign. He explicitly said it is not diagnostic and PD can exist without this finding.

DaT / dopamine-transporter imaging

The webinar described DAT imaging as a presynaptic dopamine-cell test, often using SPECT. A normal study argues against a presynaptic degenerative Parkinsonian syndrome in the appropriate context; an abnormal study indicates presynaptic dopaminergic loss.

What DAT imaging cannot do

It does not reliably distinguish PD from PSP, MSA or DLB, because these conditions can all have presynaptic dopaminergic loss. It answers “is there a presynaptic dopaminergic deficit?” rather than “which Parkinsonian disorder is this?”

When the speaker would consider DAT imaging: genuine diagnostic uncertainty—such as drug-induced versus true Parkinsonism, functional versus true Parkinsonism, or severe essential tremor versus Parkinsonian tremor. He did not recommend routine imaging before treatment simply because a patient is poor or rich.
05 · Starting treatment

Treat the patient, not the label.

The speaker repeatedly emphasised quality of life, function and avoiding unnecessary medication complexity.

No automatic rush

After diagnosis, spend time explaining the disease and ask how symptoms affect daily life. Some patients with mild symptoms may prefer observation initially.

No cure or reversal

The webinar stated that there is currently no medication that cures or reverses the underlying pathology. Disease-modifying/neuroprotective strategies remain difficult.

Rasagiline

The speaker presented rasagiline, a MAO-B inhibitor, as having the most evidence among the discussed agents for a possible neuroprotective effect, while stressing that the evidence is limited. The ADAGIO study was referenced, including a reported small UPDRS difference.

Levodopa remains the “master”

Levodopa was strongly defended against the practice of unnecessarily “saving” it for later. The speaker argued that if function is impaired and the patient needs symptomatic treatment, levodopa should not be withheld simply because of fear of future complications.

Young-onset strategy

In younger patients with mild symptoms, the speaker described dopamine agonists or anticholinergics as possible initial options in selected cases, but advised shifting to levodopa earlier if response is inadequate.

Older patients

The speaker's practical approach was levodopa-first in older patients, with particular caution about medications that worsen cognition, hallucinations and sleepiness.

Major webinar message: the fear that starting levodopa early inevitably causes earlier dyskinesia was challenged. The speaker discussed Ghanaian/Italian observational comparisons and the LEAP study as arguments that delaying levodopa does not protect against later dyskinesia and may deprive patients of useful functional years.
06 · Levodopa practicalities

Start low, build gradually, individualise.

Start low, go slowAvoid abruptly starting a high-frequency/high-dose regimen because nausea and dizziness can undermine adherence and make patients fearful of treatment.
Food interactionThe speaker advised separating levodopa from protein-containing meals because amino-acid competition can affect absorption. He described roughly one hour before food, and longer separation after meals, as a practical approach.

07 · Alternative medication classes

“Safer than levodopa” is not a useful blanket assumption.

Anticholinergics

The speaker rejected the assumption that these are automatically safer. Adverse effects highlighted were dry mouth, urinary problems, confusion, cognitive decline and glaucoma concerns. Their role was portrayed as limited and selective.

Dopamine agonists

Pramipexole and ropinirole were discussed. Important risks include excessive sleepiness/sudden sleep attacks, hallucinations and impulse-control disorders.

Impulse-control disorders

Examples included compulsive gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and punding. The speaker gave clinical anecdotes of compulsive gambling and prolonged repetitive perfectionistic activity.

Punding

A repetitive, purposeless or excessively prolonged activity was illustrated through an anecdote of a patient spending hours repeatedly arranging a bed/mattress to achieve perceived perfection.

Prolactin? Not the focus

The webinar's focus was not broad antipsychotic endocrinology; the key psychiatric prescribing issue was avoiding unnecessary dopamine blockade in Parkinsonism.

Cost-conscious treatment

The speaker repeatedly returned to the Indian context: for financially constrained patients, levodopa-based treatment was described as the practical backbone, with amantadine for dyskinesia and clozapine for psychosis when appropriate.

08 · Motor fluctuations

On, off, wearing-off—and the non-motor version.

Wearing-off

As individual levodopa doses begin lasting two to two-and-a-half hours rather than about four hours, the speaker's first strategy was to increase dosing frequency, sometimes to every three hours or, in advanced cases, every two-and-a-half hours.

Morning off

For severe early-morning immobility, he described a practical strategy of having a family member give an early levodopa dose around 4–5 AM so the patient can return to sleep and wake in a more mobile state.

Non-motor off

“Off” periods may present as anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain or bladder symptoms even when the motor difference is not dramatic. Increasing medication frequency can sometimes improve these non-motor fluctuations.

Safinamide

Presented as a later-stage adjunct for on-off fluctuations, not as early-PD therapy. The speaker discussed 50 mg and 100 mg, favouring 50 mg when effective.

MAO-B duplication warning

The speaker warned against combining rasagiline and safinamide without an appropriate washout. He specifically described a 15-day washout when switching in the context discussed.

“Simplify the regime”

As disease advances, the speaker advocated removing unnecessary medications and aiming for a simpler regimen—his phrase was essentially “less is beautiful.”

09 · Dyskinesia

Do not treat the video; treat the patient.

Who is bothered?

The speaker noted that mild dyskinesia may bother family members more than the patient. If the patient is comfortable and functionally better, treatment may not be necessary.

Typical pattern

Parkinsonian dyskinesia was described as choreiform/dancing movements, especially involving limbs and trunk. Oro-lingual movements or tongue protrusion should prompt consideration of a drug-induced movement disorder rather than ordinary levodopa dyskinesia.

First manoeuvre

If dyskinesia is troublesome, reduce individual levodopa doses where feasible and seek the patient's “sweet spot” between mobility and excessive involuntary movement.

Amantadine

Presented as a key anti-dyskinesia drug. The speaker described evidence supporting it and noted that it may also have some benefit for gait in later disease.

Amantadine adverse effects

Hallucinations, sleep disruption and livedo reticularis were mentioned; sudden visual complaints were also discussed as a reason for caution. The speaker advised avoiding a late-evening dose because of activation/sleep disturbance.

Clozapine

Low-dose clozapine was described as having evidence for dyskinesia and as particularly valuable when psychosis and dyskinesia coexist.

10 · Non-motor Parkinson’s

The “psychiatric” part is not secondary.

Depression & anxiety

Common comorbid symptoms and sometimes part of non-motor “off” periods. The speaker encouraged psychiatrists to actively manage these rather than leaving them solely to neurology.

Apathy

Apathy can be prominent and may appear before obvious motor symptoms. The speaker cautioned against diagnosing prodromal PD solely on common symptoms such as fatigue or apathy.

Cognition

Cognitive impairment can occur across Parkinsonian disorders and is particularly important when distinguishing DLB from Parkinson disease dementia.

Olfaction

Hyposmia/anosmia was highlighted as one of the non-motor features most closely linked to Parkinsonian synucleinopathy.

REM sleep behaviour disorder

Dream enactment—shouting, moving limbs or otherwise acting out dreams—was emphasised as a particularly strong association with future alpha-synucleinopathy.

Fatigue & pain

Both may be major contributors to disability and can fluctuate with dopaminergic state.

Constipation / GI dysfunction

Very common. The speaker linked early constipation to the gut–brain concept and discussed prokinetic treatment in selected cases.

Urinary symptoms

Frequency, urgency and nocturia were framed as common overactive-bladder symptoms. Pure urinary retention was described as less typical of PD and should trigger assessment for prostate/urological causes or infection.

Autonomic dysfunction

Particularly important in atypical syndromes such as MSA, where prominent autonomic dysfunction may be a diagnostic clue.

RBD point from the webinar: the speaker quoted a figure of more than 80% of people with isolated idiopathic RBD eventually developing an alpha-synuclein disorder and mentioned an overall conversion rate of about 6.3% per year. These figures are reproduced as presented in the webinar and should be verified against current literature before being used clinically.
11 · Prodromal disease & Braak hypothesis

Interesting science—but don't overdiagnose.

Braak hypothesis

The speaker described a proposed route of α-synuclein pathology beginning in the gut and/or olfactory system, travelling through the vagus/brainstem pathways and eventually involving basal-ganglia structures.

Why smell matters

The olfactory system provides another interface with the external environment, and early loss of smell may precede motor Parkinsonism.

Vagotomy observation

The speaker mentioned observational data suggesting lower Parkinson risk among people who previously underwent vagotomy for peptic ulcer disease. He explicitly framed the hypothesis as not 100% proven.

Probiotics

Probiotics were discussed as an area being explored in the context of gut-related hypotheses, not as an established preventive therapy.

Prodromal labels

The speaker mentioned research-oriented groupings involving cognitive symptoms, anxiety/depression, sleep, fatigue, pain and autonomic symptoms.

Clinical caution

Because anxiety, depression, fatigue, constipation and apathy are extremely common in psychiatry, the speaker cautioned that these symptoms alone should not be used to declare prodromal Parkinson’s disease. RBD and unexplained olfactory loss were presented as more concerning combinations.

12 · Psychiatry × Parkinson’s

This is a shared clinical territory.

Antipsychotic-induced Parkinsonism

Psychiatrists commonly encounter Parkinsonism caused or worsened by dopamine-blocking drugs. Risperidone and other dopamine antagonists were specifically mentioned in the opening discussion.

Clozapine

Presented as a major option for Parkinson-related psychosis and a potentially useful treatment for dyskinesia. The speaker favoured low doses in this context.

Quetiapine

Discussed as another relatively dopamine-sparing antipsychotic option in patients with Parkinsonian symptoms or Lewy-body pathology.

Pimavanserin

Discussed as a non-dopamine-antagonist treatment for Parkinson-related psychosis. The speaker reported extensive personal use and considered it relatively safe, though sometimes less effective than quetiapine or clozapine.

Hallucination hierarchy

The speaker's practical ordering was: anticholinergics → dopamine agonists → amantadine as more likely contributors to hallucinations, with levodopa comparatively less likely. Advanced PD psychosis was framed as multifactorial rather than simply “levodopa toxicity.”

Psychosis + diabetes/dyslipidemia

In the Q&A, the speaker advised caution with all antipsychotics in medically complex patients, suggesting pimavanserin first in the scenario discussed, then quetiapine, while keeping clozapine as a later option where appropriate and with ECG/monitoring considerations.

Important: This section reproduces the webinar's medication discussion. It is not a prescribing protocol. Antipsychotic selection in PD requires assessment of QT risk, cognition, falls, autonomic function, metabolic factors, drug interactions and local monitoring requirements.
13 · Sudden deterioration

Before declaring “rapid progression,” run the checklist.

Medication error

Look for newly introduced dopamine antagonists such as levosulpiride and for unnecessary polypharmacy.

Infection

UTI, sepsis and other systemic illnesses can dramatically worsen motor and cognitive symptoms.

Subdural haematoma

Consider acute structural causes when there is sudden or unexplained deterioration.

Autoimmune disease

When confusion, seizures and atypical movement symptoms appear, the speaker suggested considering treatable autoimmune causes such as NMDA-receptor or VGKC-associated disease where clinically appropriate.

Polypharmacy

Remove unnecessary medicines. The speaker repeatedly advocated simplifying the regimen as disease becomes advanced.

Withdrawal

Do not assume stopping medication is harmless. Sudden withdrawal of levodopa—or even amantadine—can produce serious deterioration.

14 · Dopamine withdrawal

Never treat medication withdrawal casually.

Acute dopamine-deficient syndrome: the speaker described historical “drug holidays” and warned that abrupt dopaminergic withdrawal can cause profound rigidity, chest/respiratory stiffness and potentially life-threatening deterioration. If medication needs to be stopped, the webinar's message was to taper rather than abruptly withdraw, with appropriate clinical supervision.

Amantadine withdrawal

A patient taking amantadine for dyskinesia was described as deteriorating after sudden discontinuation, with severe symptoms and confusion. The speaker therefore advised gradual reduction when stopping it.

Levodopa withdrawal

Sudden levodopa cessation was described as particularly dangerous. The speaker showed a case with severe generalized rigidity and breathing difficulty after abrupt withdrawal.

Drug holidays

The older idea was to stop medication so dopamine receptors could “reset.” The speaker strongly discouraged this approach when it involves abrupt cessation.

15 · Advanced therapies

When oral therapy is no longer enough.

Apomorphine rescue

Subcutaneous apomorphine was described as a rapid rescue therapy for sudden “off,” with improvement potentially occurring within minutes. The speaker described a short duration of benefit.

Apomorphine infusion

Continuous infusion can provide sustained dopaminergic stimulation, but the speaker described nausea/vomiting, postural hypotension, local nodules, cumbersome daily handling and cost as practical limitations.

DBS

Deep brain stimulation was presented for carefully selected advanced PD with disabling motor fluctuations and/or troublesome dyskinesia despite medical management.

Do not operate too early

The speaker stressed diagnostic confidence and suggested that disease should have had time to declare itself because early Parkinsonian presentations can later evolve into atypical syndromes such as PSP.

Psychiatric/cognitive selection

Significant dementia or major uncontrolled psychiatric problems were described as reasons to be cautious about DBS candidacy.

“5–2–1” practical concept

The speaker described a practical DBS selection heuristic: more than 5 oral levodopa doses/day, more than 2 hours of daily off time, and more than 1 hour of troublesome dyskinesia, provided the patient is otherwise suitable.

16 · Cost and Indian context

Practical realities matter.

Lower-cost medical strategy

For financially constrained patients, the speaker favoured levodopa as the core treatment, adding amantadine for dyskinesia and clozapine for psychosis where clinically appropriate.

Advanced disease

For severe advanced fluctuations, he acknowledged that low-cost options become limited and patients may need very frequent levodopa dosing.

DBS cost discussion

The speaker gave approximate Indian figures during the webinar: government-centre DBS around ₹8–10 lakh, and private-centre costs approximately ₹13–25 lakh, with more recent practical figures around ₹16–25 lakh depending on the system/lead.

Apomorphine cost discussion

A cost of roughly ₹30,000/month was mentioned for an apomorphine pump in the context of the discussion.

Drug affordability

Benserazide/levodopa was described as potentially better tolerated in some patients with gastrointestinal adverse effects but considerably more expensive than levodopa/carbidopa.

System-level message

The speaker expressed concern that dependence on imported DBS technology increases cost and suggested that local manufacturing could improve affordability.

17 · Other research topics discussed

What is promising is not necessarily established.

Monoclonal antibodies / alpha-synuclein

The speaker said a discussed monoclonal-antibody trial had failed and raised the possibility that alpha-synuclein might be a bystander rather than the primary pathogenic driver. He presented this as an unresolved scientific question.

Metformin

When asked whether metformin is neuroprotective, the answer was not currently in routine clinical use for that purpose; the speaker acknowledged ongoing studies.

L-carnosine, probiotics, CoQ10

These were grouped among agents that have been proposed as neuroprotective based on preclinical/animal or proof-of-concept work, but the speaker stated that they do not currently have established clinical neuroprotective value.

Dextromethorphan-related research

A question about a dextromethorphan-related molecule was acknowledged as an area with proof-of-concept trials, but the speaker did not describe an established clinical role in Parkinson’s disease.

rTMS

The speaker, who described having a TMS laboratory, said rTMS has research potential for gait, cognition and psychosis but that benefits observed in their work were short-lived, lasting hours to about a day. He did not recommend it as routine prescription treatment for PD.

Non-invasive vagal stimulation

Discussed as another experimental approach with some observed cognitive/gait/psychosis effects, but the speaker characterised these as short-lived and still research-oriented.
